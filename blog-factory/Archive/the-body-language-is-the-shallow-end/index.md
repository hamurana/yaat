# The Body Language Is the Shallow End

{{< lead >}}
Sit asymmetrically, don't fidget, narrow your eyes on the important line. All of that works a bit. It's also the outermost layer of something with about four layers under it, and the bottom one is the only part that lasts.
{{< /lead >}}

### Layer one: the visible stuff

Worth having, easy to learn, and shallow.

- **Take up space comfortably.** Feet at least shoulder-width when standing. Arms open rather than blocking. Move around a room like you've been in it before.
- **Vary your eye contact.** Giving slightly less attention early makes people work a bit for it. Then, on the line that matters, hold it properly.
- **Stop fidgeting.** This one's harder to fake than posture, because anxiety tends to override whatever you decided to do with your hands.

Useful. Also the layer that's most obviously technique, which is why on its own it reads as someone who has recently watched a video about body language.

### Layer two: not reacting

Deeper and harder. When something lands — a jab, a mistake, someone testing you — the composure is the signal, not whatever you say next.

Overreacting does two things at once: it makes the immediate situation worse, and it tells everyone watching that circumstances have a grip on you.

There's a version of this for anyone leading through something bad. Staying quiet while other people panic buys you thinking time you wouldn't otherwise get, and it steadies the room rather than confusing it. Not coldness — just not adding your own noise to a situation that already has plenty.

### Layer three: slowing down

The counterintuitive one. Composure under pressure comes from taking *longer*, not from having a fast answer ready.

A pitch gets rejected and you take twenty full seconds before responding. It feels unbearable from the inside and reads as complete control from the outside.

There's a useful asymmetry here: we give other people about 30% less time to respond than we'd want for ourselves. So the pause that feels endless to you is barely registering with them. When something feels urgent, that feeling is the argument for waiting a beat.

### Layer four: being willing to walk

Persuasion works better when you're not trying to persuade. Screening the other side as much as they're screening you changes the shape of the conversation.

People partly judge how much they want something by how much you seem to need it. So share your interest — just stay genuinely able to leave.

The caveat matters though. Walking away with a scene attached — raised voice, you'll-regret-this — is the opposite signal. The version that works is closer to: this isn't a match, no hard feelings.

### The bottom layer

Underneath all of it is believing you'll be fine whatever happens.

And you can't reason your way there. You get it by doing the things you're avoiding and noticing you survived them. Don Draper only becomes genuinely settled late on, when he starts telling the truth at real cost — losing an account, admitting things he'd hidden. Having lived through the worst case, there's nothing left to perform.

### One thing worth remembering

He's a fictional advertising executive, and the show is fairly clear that this composure coexists with a life he's making a mess of. Take the layers. Don't take the whole man as a model.

### A few small things to try

- Wait two seconds longer than feels natural before answering something hard
- Notice your hands in the next tense conversation
- Say your interest in something plainly, and stay willing to walk
- Do one social thing you've been avoiding, and notice you're still here
