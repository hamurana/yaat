{{< lead >}}
These aren't two versions of the same appliance. An iron uses heat and pressure to stretch fabric flat and hold it there. A steamer uses moisture and gravity to let fibres relax on a hanger. Different physics, different jobs — and shops sell them as alternatives.
{{< /lead >}}

### What each is actually doing

**The iron** runs somewhere between about 250°F and 360°F. The plate loosens the bonds in the fibres while the weight stretches the cloth flat, and it sets in that shape as it cools. That cooling step is the bit people skip — it's what makes a crease stay.

**The steamer** heats water to somewhere between 200°F and 400°F and lets steam relax the fibres while the garment hangs. Gravity does the pulling. Bigger stand-up models let you set the temperature — hotter for cotton and linen, cooler for delicates — while portable ones run cool with no controls at all.

Modern irons have steam and spray functions now, which hasn't made steamers redundant. Steam from an iron is still being applied under pressure against a board.

### Reach for the iron when polish matters

- Sharp creases in trousers. Nothing else does this.
- Dress shirts.
- Heavy, durable weaves — linen, wool, denim.
- Anywhere you want precision, because you're placing the heat rather than waving it around.

The costs are real. Sleeves, pleats, and anything curved are awkward without extra kit — a tailor's ham or a sleeve board. Leave it resting too long and you'll scorch or shine the fabric. And it needs a board and somewhere to keep it, though a towel on a table genuinely works in a pinch.

### Reach for the steamer when the fabric is fragile

- Silk and synthetics, which an iron will happily melt.
- Delicate wool, velvet, corduroy — anything with a pile that crushes flat under a plate.
- Structured jackets, which an iron cannot handle at all.
- Freshening something between washes, which is most of what it'll get used for.
- Travel. Portables are the whole reason people own one.

It's also simply easier. Hang, fill, plug in, sweep down. And because you're not pressing layers together on a hard surface, you can't accidentally iron a second crease into a sleeve.

Where it loses: slow on thick fabric, incapable of setting a crease, tiring on the arm across a whole wardrobe, and over-steaming can loosen seams if you keep going.

### The answer is both

Not a fudge — they genuinely don't overlap. Shirts and trousers get the iron. Jackets, knitwear, silk, and anything you're just freshening get the steamer. A suit typically wants both in the same session.

### Safety, briefly

Neither goes near leather, suede, waxed fabric, or anything that can melt. Check the care tag first, and when you aren't sure, run cooler — you can always go up. Keep your hand away from the nozzle, since steam at that temperature burns faster than a plate does. Turn it off at the wall rather than trusting the auto shut-off.

### A bit of history, because it's better than expected

Irons started as metal pans filled with hot coals in China in the first century BCE. Then cast-iron slabs and box irons in the 1600s. Then a stretch of fuel-burning irons running on kerosene, ethanol, gas, and — genuinely — whale oil, some of them plumbed into the house, until electricity took over around the Second World War. The electric iron is credited to **Henry W. Seeley** in New York in **1882**, and the electric steam iron to **Max Skolnik** in Chicago in **1934**, which reached most homes through the Steam-O-Matic in the forties and fifties.

Compact fabric steamers reportedly grew out of steaming and re-blocking men's hats — a job people used to do over a kettle.

A few small things to try:

- Work out which of the two you've been using for jobs it's bad at.
- Buy a tube of the other one before your next trip.
- Let a pressed crease cool before you move the garment.
- Check the care tag once, then run cooler than it says.
