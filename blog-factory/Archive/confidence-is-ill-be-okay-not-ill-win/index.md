# Confidence Is "I'll Be Okay", Not "I'll Win"

{{< lead >}}
Most people wait to feel confident before doing the thing, and define confidence as expecting to succeed. That definition guarantees the wait never ends. The workable version is much lower: knowing you'll survive the realistic worst case.
{{< /lead >}}

### It's built, not issued

Dwayne Johnson grew up poor, was arrested several times before 17, and was depressed after being cut from the CFL. What he has now came from hundreds of live reps in front of crowds and some acting lessons. Not from birth.

The pattern repeats in smaller ways. Someone very shy in high school spent a semester in Costa Rica deliberately approaching strangers for directions and sitting with people he didn't know. Back home, a girl who'd previously turned him down told a mutual friend he looked great. Same haircut. She was responding to something built through reps.

Which is the useful framing: it's more like the gym than a personality trait. Uncomfortable social situations are the load.

### It doesn't look the same on everyone

Five rough types, and copying the wrong one is why a lot of advice fails:

- **High-conviction** — believes so completely you go along with it. Conor McGregor calling his knockouts.
- **Authentic** — no gap between the public and private version. Keanu Reeves giving up his seat on the subway.
- **Funny** — humour threaded through everything. Kevin Hart.
- **Energetic** — completely committed to the bit whether or not anyone joins. Jack Black.
- **Empathetic** — makes you feel so seen you tell them more than you meant to. Oprah, Steven Bartlett.

To find yours, look at two things: what draws you to the people you admire, and what came naturally when you were a kid, before you learned to keep it down.

### The worst-case question

The tool for the confidence part is one question: *what's the most reasonable worst case?*

Not the catastrophe your brain offers. The realistic bad outcome. Usually it's a slightly awkward two minutes that nobody remembers by Thursday — and then you notice you'd survive it, which is all confidence actually required.

### You're broadcasting either way

You signal what you believe about yourself constantly, and it runs in a loop. Change the surface — stand taller, hold eye contact a little longer, open up your posture — and people respond differently, which shifts what you believe, which makes the posture natural.

The clearest small example: a friend at an event leaning in, broadcasting need, getting nowhere. One adjustment — lean back — and the other person got visibly more interested. Same words. One physical change.

### Likeable is forgettable

Trying to be liked by everyone sands you down to nothing. Standing out gets you people who are genuinely drawn to you and people who aren't, and the second group is the cost of the first.

The test is easy: share an opinion you've been sitting on. Honestly, not aggressively. Watch who leans in. Those are your people, and you'd never have found them by being agreeable.

### Fear wears a sensible disguise

This is the part I'd underline. Fear rarely shows up as fear — it shows up as a practical-sounding reason.

The example that makes it obvious: a friend refused to go and talk to a woman because she "looked dumb." Someone else did. She'd gone to Harvard. The reason wasn't a judgement, it was fear wearing a costume.

Naming it is apparently about half the work. Saying "I'm afraid" out loud strips the disguise off, and then you do a small, safe version of the thing.

### A few small things to try

- Ask what the *reasonable* worst case is, not the catastrophe
- Set your posture deliberately before something that matters — stand taller, take up space
- Say one opinion you've been holding back and notice who moves closer
- When you catch a practical-sounding reason not to do something, ask whether it's fear
- Break the script in one ordinary interaction today — ask the person serving you how their day's actually going
