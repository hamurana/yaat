{{< lead >}}
Most men past sixty aren't underdressed. They're wearing the wrong version of right. Chinos sit in the middle of every casual outfit, joining the shirt to the shoes, and when they're slightly loose or slightly tired the whole thing quietly comes apart.
{{< /lead >}}

### The failure is never dramatic

A clean button-down, a light jacket, decent shoes — and chinos a bit wide through the thigh, thin enough to crease at the knee. Nothing you could point at. You just look tired.

Change only the trousers, keep the same shirt and the same shoes, and the outfit reassembles itself. That's the whole argument for spending attention here rather than anywhere else.

The line worth remembering: **most men don't need better clothes, they need better fit.**

### What to buy, by what it's for

**The everyday pair — around $40 to $70.** Uniqlo and Dockers both do this properly. Uniqlo's are lightweight cotton blends with a little stretch and a cut that doesn't fight the body. Dockers have decades of predictable fit behind them, having been created by Levi's specifically for business casual. You don't need expensive trousers to look sharp. You need trousers that do the job.

**The fit problem, solved — $90 to $130.** Bonobos exists because men couldn't find trousers that fit, and it still offers genuinely different cuts: slim but not tight, tailored and balanced, athletic with more room where it's needed. If your body has changed shape — and after sixty it has — having three cuts to choose from matters more than the fabric does.

**The step up — $80 to $120, usually less on sale.** Banana Republic and J.Crew are where this starts to look deliberate. Banana Republic uses heavier cotton that drapes better and creases less. J.Crew runs slightly more tailored with cleaner lines. This is the tier where people register that you dress well without being able to say why.

**For travel — $120 to $150.** The Lululemon ABC trouser is technical fabric with a normal silhouette: light, breathable, and it doesn't crease. Traditional chinos lose their shape over a long flight and several hours of sitting; these arrive looking like they did at the airport. That's a narrow use case and a real one.

**The nice pair — $150 and up.** Peter Millar sit at the top, cotton blended with performance fibres. No sagging, no collapsing at the knee, no end-of-day droop. Worth it for the occasions where you'd notice.

### You don't need all five

That's the honest version. Three pairs cover a life:

- One reliable everyday pair.
- One well-fitted pair for going out.
- Optionally one good pair for when it matters.

Buying five brands is how you end up with five mediocre options and no clear choice in the morning.

### The trap in the middle

Comfort is a legitimate priority and it's also the excuse men use to buy trousers with no structure left in them.

Comfortable and shapeless are not the same purchase. Everything above is comfortable — the ones that fail are the ones that stopped holding a line, which is a fabric problem rather than a comfort one. You can have both, and the brands above are essentially five different answers to how.

### What changes when they fit

The useful part isn't the compliment. It's that you stop thinking about your clothes at all — no adjusting at the waist, no pulling at the thigh, no wondering whether you look untidy.

That's what people are actually reading. Not the trousers. The absence of fidgeting.

A few small things to try:

- Look at your chinos at the thigh and the knee, not the waist.
- Own three pairs with three jobs instead of six with one.
- If you fly regularly, get one technical pair and see.
- Check whether "comfortable" has quietly become "shapeless".
