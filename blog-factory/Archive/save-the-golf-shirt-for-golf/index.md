A shiny synthetic polo with a logo on the chest is sportswear. It's very good at being sportswear. Worn to the office or out to dinner it reads as company merch, and no amount of fit fixes that. The most common polo mistake there is.
{{< /lead >}}

### The trap

Performance polos are built for moving and sweating, which is why they're slick, stretchy, and covered in branding. All of that is a feature on a course and a problem anywhere else. Bright synthetic sheen doesn't read as effort. It reads as a conference giveaway.

What you want instead, for everything that isn't sport:

- **Cotton piqué**, **knit cotton**, or a **linen blend**
- Neutral colours — navy, cream, grey, olive, burgundy
- Small branding, or none

### Three types, three different moods

- **Classic cotton piqué.** Breathable, textured, the everyday one. Works with chinos, jeans, or shorts.
- **Knit polo.** More elevated, especially in a neutral. This is the one that holds up at dinner.
- **Long-sleeve polo.** Smart casual on a cooler day, and rarer than it should be.

Whichever it is, it should sit cleanly on the shoulder with sleeves ending around mid-bicep. Left untucked, the hem wants to land near the middle of the fly or just below the belt line.

### The collar is what you're paying for

It's the only thing separating a polo from a t-shirt, so a floppy, tiny, or oversized collar undoes the whole shirt.

**Johnny collars** — banded, no buttons — are having a moment and read as genuinely elegant. Popping the collar is best left alone unless you're deliberately going somewhere retro with it.

### Tuck or don't, but decide

Untucked with shorts or jeans is relaxed and completely fine, as long as the hem isn't heading down your thigh.

Tucked into proper trousers with a leather belt and loafers is a different garment altogether. It reads as considered rather than thrown on, and it works especially well with knit polos and with pleated or higher-rise trousers.

The mistake isn't picking one. It's letting the shirt decide by default.

### The history closes the loop nicely

Despite the name, the polo shirt doesn't come from polo. It comes from tennis.

**René Lacoste** introduced the short-sleeve cotton piqué shirt with a soft collar in **1933**, as a lighter and more practical alternative to the long-sleeve kit players were stuck with. **Ralph Lauren** picked it up decades later and turned it into an American preppy staple through the **1970s**.

Which is a neat thing to sit with. The polo always was sportswear. It just used to be sportswear made of cotton, cut properly, in a plain colour — and that's precisely the version that still works off the court.

### Where to buy one

- **Budget:** Uniqlo, Gap, Old Navy, H&M Premium
- **Middle:** Spier & Mackay, Banana Republic, Charles Tyrwhitt, Suitsupply, Quince
- **Higher end:** Lacoste, Ralph Lauren, Sunspel, Luca Faloni

If you're only buying one, make it a navy or cream knit polo with a Johnny collar and a clean fit. It's the most flexible thing in the category and the hardest to get wrong.

A few small things to try:

- Go through your polos and pull out anything shiny. Keep them for actual sport.
- Buy one knit polo and wear it tucked, with trousers, to something you'd normally wear a shirt to.
- Check where the hem lands. Mid-fly, not mid-thigh.
- Stop buying the one with the big logo. It's the part that dates fastest.
