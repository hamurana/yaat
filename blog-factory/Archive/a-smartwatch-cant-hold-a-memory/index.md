# A Smartwatch Can't Hold a Memory

{{< lead >}}
The breaking point was notifications — even the battery warnings. But the reason the Apple Watch got demoted to gym duty rather than replaced is different: you will never keep it. Software support ends, the battery dies unserviceably, and it goes in a drawer.
{{< /lead >}}

### The disposability problem

A smartwatch has a shelf life measured in operating system updates. Nobody is passing one to their children.

That's not a complaint about the technology, which does its job well. It's a category observation — it's a computer, and computers get replaced. Once you see it that way, wearing one *as jewellery* starts to feel like the wrong use of your wrist.

The alternative isn't nostalgia. It's that a mechanical or quartz watch survives long enough to accumulate the places you wore it.

### The three technologies, briefly

- **Quartz digital.** A quartz crystal vibrating 32,768 times a second, read by a circuit. Extremely accurate — seconds per month — and with so few parts it's more sensible to replace than repair.
- **Quartz analog.** Same underlying tech, traditional hands, ticking once per second.
- **Mechanical.** No battery, no electronics — gears and springs, ticking several times a second so the hand sweeps smoothly. Either automatic, wound by the motion of your arm, or hand-wound every day or two, which plenty of expensive watches still are.

Knowing which you're buying matters more than the brand, because it determines what owning it will be like.

### Where to start cheaply

- **Casio F91W**, about $22. Roughly seven years on a battery, and the closest thing watches have to a Toyota Corolla.
- **Casio A168** — the same thing with a metal bracelet and a backlight that glows across the whole face.
- **Casio Royale**, with multiple time zones, which genuinely matters if you fly — no manual adjusting on landing — plus 100m water resistance and a decade of battery.
- **G-Shock**, if durability is the point. One survived being dropped from a helicopter.
- **Citizen Eco-Drive**, charged by light, so the battery question never comes up. One sat in a dark closed box and started ticking the moment it saw daylight.
- **Seiko 5**, an automatic on the NH35 movement — a reasonable and cheap entry into mechanical watches.
- **MoonSwatch**, if you want something with a story attached to it before you've made one.

### The actual argument

The list of watches he owns is really a list of events.

A Timex Expedition bought twenty years ago at a scout camp — the most expensive thing he bought there — used for rope climbing and swimming, and now earmarked for a child who takes up the outdoors. An Invicta given by his parents at graduation, which is what got him into mechanical watches, and which he replaced after landing a teaching job. A G-Shock bought on a boss's advice, where every scratch recalls a specific shoot.

None of that works with a device you'll bin in four years. The object has to outlast the occasion for the occasion to attach to it.

### One caveat

There's a warning attached and it's a fair one: this gets addictive. The distance between "a cheap Casio so I stop looking at notifications" and a growing collection is shorter than it looks.

The useful version stops early. One good watch that isn't connected to anything solves the original problem completely.

### A few small things to try

- Take the smartwatch off outside of workouts for a week and see what you miss
- Buy a Casio F91W for about the cost of a takeaway and wear it
- Work out which of the three technologies actually suits how you live
- Notice which watch you'd want to still have in thirty years
