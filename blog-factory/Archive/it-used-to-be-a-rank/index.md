"Gentleman" wasn't a compliment originally. It was a legal category — the bottom rung of the English landed gentry, below an Esquire, above a Yeoman, and available only to men with a blood connection to nobility. You couldn't earn it. That it now means something you choose daily is one of the better things that's happened to a word.
{{< /lead >}}

### How it drifted

- First recorded around 1275, but it entered general use after 1413, when Henry V required legal writs to state a defendant's "estate, degree, or mystery." Younger sons with no title of their own suddenly needed something to write down.
- By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it had tangled up with the right to bear arms — carrying them signalled the rank, the rank implied the right.
- By the nineteenth century it mostly signalled that you didn't have to work. English cricket ran the distinction openly for decades: unpaid *Gentlemen* on one side, paid *Players* on the other. Same game, different changing rooms.

Then the whole apparatus fell away and left behind a definition anyone can meet: a man of good and courteous conduct.

### Which now mostly means paying attention

The modern version of the code is unglamorous and comes down to what you do with your attention.

- **Learn people's names and say them correctly.** Ask twice if you need to. It's less awkward than the alternative, which is a year of avoiding using someone's name.
- **Keep your phone off the table.** A phone sitting face-down between two people creates distance even when nobody touches it. It's a small object making a claim about who else might be more important.
- **Hold eye contact somewhere around sixty to seventy percent of a conversation** — that's a Western benchmark, and it shifts considerably elsewhere, so travel with it loosely.
- **Sit up.** There's a study out of Texas Tech from 2018 tying upright posture during social interaction to lower anxiety and more confidence. Whether or not the effect is large, the cost is nothing.

### The story that sticks

A business student sat a final exam and found one question he couldn't answer: what is the name of the woman who cleans this building?

He'd walked past her for a whole semester. He went on to run Charles Schwab, and has described that question as the most important thing he learned in the class — which is a nicer outcome than most exam questions manage.

The rule is old and unimproved: treat people the same regardless of what they can do for you.

### Some of it is genuinely optional

There's a cluster of chivalry rules here that get presented as part of the package, and they're worth separating out.

Helping anyone struggling with bags, a pram, or stairs — yes, obviously, and it's striking how often two people will sit and watch someone struggle rather than stand up. Making sure a friend who's had too much gets home rather than working it out alone — yes, that's just looking after people.

Paying for a date you initiated is fine as a default, but the reasoning usually given for it is bad. *Many women won't go on a second date with a man who didn't pay* is an argument about tactics, not conduct, and if that's the reason then it isn't manners at all. The simpler version holds up better: you did the inviting, so you pick it up. If she did the asking, it flips.

### The bits that are pure ritual, and fine

Taking your hat off indoors traces back to knights removing helmets to show they weren't a threat, which is a nice thing to know while doing something otherwise meaningless.

Writing by hand when something actually matters is in the same category. There's no practical advantage over a message. That's precisely why it registers — the effort is the content.

A few small things to try:

- Learn one name you've been avoiding needing.
- Put your phone in your pocket instead of on the table.
- Stand up next time somebody nearby is struggling with something.
- Write one thing by hand this month.
