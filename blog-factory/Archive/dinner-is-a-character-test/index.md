# Dinner Is a Character Test

{{< lead >}}
Restaurant etiquette gets taught as a set of rules about cutlery and bread. It's really one thing repeated eight ways: whether you're paying attention to anyone other than yourself.
{{< /lead >}}

### The eight

- **Book a table.** Even for something spontaneous, a phone call beats turning up hopeful. Whoever's doing the inviting handles it — that's the actual rule, not seniority.
- **Turn up on time.** Give yourself ten or fifteen minutes of slack. If you're going to be late, say so early enough that the other person can decide whether to wait, rather than working it out from your empty chair.
- **Stand when someone arrives.** Whoever they are. It lets you shake hands or embrace properly, which sitting doesn't, and it reads as being pleased they came.
- **Say something nice.** On a date, about what they're wearing or something you've just learned about them. As a guest, about the place they picked. It's small and people notice its absence more than its presence.
- **Tear the bread, then butter it.** Bite-sized piece, butter that piece, eat it. More of a European habit than an American one, but it spares everyone the sight of a slice with a bite taken out of it.
- **Be decent to the staff.** This is the one that actually matters. Rudeness to a server is the clearest available signal of how you'll behave when someone can't push back. Thank them, ask how they're doing, tip properly. Most of what goes wrong in a meal isn't theirs.
- **Order sensibly.** Skip things that eat messily on a date, skip heavy garlic, and if the bill's being split, stay mid-priced. Ordering the expensive whisky when someone else is paying half is a decision they'll remember.
- **Read the room.** Notice how the evening's actually going and adjust.

### The pattern underneath

Look at those together and only two are really about food.

The rest are about noticing: that someone might be waiting, that they made an effort with how they look, that the person bringing your plate is having a day, that your dinner companion might be quietly working out what the bill's going to do to their week.

Which is why the staff one carries the most weight. Everything else in a first impression is a performance aimed at the person opposite you. How you treat a server is the bit you're not performing, so it's the only reliable data available.

### Where I'd push back slightly

The bread rule and the collar-and-cuffs end of this can tip into something else — etiquette as a way of quietly scoring other people rather than making them comfortable.

That's the opposite of the point. If you find yourself noticing that someone buttered a whole slice, the etiquette has stopped working, because the entire function of it is to make the meal easier for everyone at the table. A rule you're using to feel superior isn't manners any more.

Take the version that's about attention. Leave the version that's about grading.

### A few small things to try

- Ask your server how they're doing when they introduce themselves, and mean it
- Stand up next time someone joins you at a table
- If you're going to be more than five minutes late, message before you're late, not after
- When splitting a bill, order like the other person's budget is real
