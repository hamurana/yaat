{{< lead >}}
Most people treat a conversation as a small competition for attention. The ones everybody likes are doing the opposite — handing the attention over, on purpose, repeatedly. It reads as warmth. It's actually just generosity with the floor.
{{< /lead >}}

### Smile with your eyes, which you can't do on command

We work out whether a smile is real by the muscles around the eyes, not the mouth. And we notice when it's missing, even if we couldn't say what tipped us off. A mouth-only smile doesn't read as neutral — it reads as slightly untrustworthy.

You can't fix this from the outside. Trying to flex the right muscles produces exactly the thing you're trying to avoid. The only route in is to actually find something funny, which mostly means being willing to find yourself funny.

Laughing at yourself doesn't cost you status, incidentally. It's close to the fastest way to gain some.

### Build up rather than cut down

The sarcastic option is always right there and it usually gets the bigger laugh. It also makes everyone slightly more careful around you afterwards.

The better move is to laugh *with* rather than *at*. And when you do tease, hide a compliment inside it — mocking a friend for whingeing about how heavy his costume was, while mentioning the abs he got for it. The tease lands and he comes out ahead.

Some people build a whole style on this: making the punchline of every joke that their friends are attractive, well-liked, or better than them at the thing.

### Come in one notch above the room

Matching the room's energy is safe and forgettable. Arriving one level more enthusiastic — one level sillier than the situation strictly calls for — is a small risk that almost always pays, including in rooms that think of themselves as serious.

The clearest example of this working is an audition that the producers described as possibly the least welcoming environment they'd ever run. Asked whether she could do anything to lighten the mood, and then whether she could dance, she did the robot. Not a nervous half-version — the robot. The stone-faced company president smiled. She had the part about ten seconds after leaving the room, with barely any acting experience behind her.

Nobody hires you for being appropriately serious in a room that's already too serious.

### Listening, done actively

This is the one that works invisibly, which is why it works. People come away liking you and can't quite point to why.

- Validate the joke that didn't quite land. Getting the room behind someone after a weak line turns an awkward beat into shared goodwill, and they'll remember it.
- Repeat their phrase back to them. "It's weird, isn't it?" Small, and it proves you were actually there.
- Use the small affirmatives. *Yeah, exactly.* People keep talking when they can hear you're still with them.
- When someone piles onto your story and makes it funnier, let them have it. Handing the moment over makes you look better than holding it would have.

### The whole thing in one line

You don't get people to like you by being impressive at them. You get there by being in a good mood and letting it spill over. Everything above is a way of making sure it lands on somebody.

A few small things to try:

- Repeat back one phrase in your next conversation.
- Make your next tease end on something true and flattering.
- Arrive one notch more cheerful than the meeting deserves.
- When a joke of yours gets topped, laugh hardest.
