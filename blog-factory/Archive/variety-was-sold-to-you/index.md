# Variety Was Sold to You

{{< lead >}}
The idea that you shouldn't be seen in the same outfit twice is quite recent, and it was sold to you by people who benefit from it. A lot of capable men wear more or less the same thing daily and it hasn't hurt them.
{{< /lead >}}

### What repeating actually buys

Executive function is finite. Whatever you spend deciding between shirts before eight in the morning isn't available for the first difficult conversation of the day.

That's the honest case for a uniform: it isn't about looking good, it's about not spending anything to look fine. You move from *what should I wear* to *what should I build*, which is a better question to be holding at breakfast.

There's a distinction worth keeping here between a uniform and a costume. A costume is what someone wears while still working out who he is. A uniform is what's left once he's stopped searching.

### Why it reads as steady rather than boring

An appearance that shifts with every trend suggests convictions that do the same. Consistency in how you present is one of the few things people can observe directly and reasonably read as consistency of character.

Most men avoid this because they're afraid of being forgettable. But presence doesn't come from a loud shirt — the person influencing a room is usually the one who isn't distracted by it.

### The condition attached

You can't build a fortress out of cardboard. If you're wearing the same few things constantly, they have to be genuinely good.

The maths here is real, whatever you think of the framing. A £300 shirt still crisp after three years beats a £40 shirt that's gone limp in the collar after five washes and reached a landfill within six months. Heavy cotton, high-twist wool, reinforced seams — these are the difference between a uniform and simply owning fewer bad clothes.

To make it work you need to know three things about yourself: your shoulder-to-waist proportion, where your trousers should break, and which colours actually suit you. That's the whole specification. Then buy a small number of excellent pieces in it and stop.

### Where I'd be honest about the source

This is brand content, and it's selling blazers. The $300-shirt example is doing double duty as an argument and as a price anchor.

That doesn't make the reasoning wrong — cost per wear is a sound way to think, and decision fatigue is real. But the version you should take away doesn't require premium anything. Four identical well-fitting shirts from a mid-range shop achieve the entire benefit: no morning decision, consistent presentation, and clothes chosen once rather than continuously.

The uniform idea is free. The specific uniform being recommended is not.

### A few small things to try

- Notice how long you spend deciding what to wear, honestly, for one week
- Work out your three specifications: proportion, trouser break, colours that suit you
- Buy two or three of a shirt that already works instead of one of something new
- Wear the same thing twice in a week somewhere it'll be noticed, and see if anything happens
