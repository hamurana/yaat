{{< lead >}}
Everything fits when you're standing in front of a mirror with your shoulders back. Then you sit down, reach for something, climb a flight of stairs, and the clothes tell the truth. Eight ordinary movements will tell you more about fit than any amount of standing still.
{{< /lead >}}

### The eight tests

**The chair.** Sit down. A waistband that looked fine standing can cut into your stomach, flare the pockets open, or pull across the thighs the moment you bend. What you want is a medium or slightly higher rise, secure without squeezing, with enough room through the seat and thighs to sit like a person.

**The doorway.** Walk through one and watch the shoulders. The seam is the first thing anyone registers — before brand, before price — and it's also the hardest part of a jacket to alter. A tailor will happily reshape a waist. Shoulders are a different job entirely. The seam should end at the natural edge of your shoulder with the sleeve falling cleanly, neither collapsing inward nor bulging out.

**The handshake.** Putting your hand out puts the whole sleeve-cuff-watch arrangement on display, which is why it's worth getting right. The jacket sleeve should end near your wrist bone with roughly a quarter to half an inch of shirt cuff showing. The shirt sleeve itself should reach the base of your thumb.

**The pocket reach.** Slide a hand into a trouser pocket and everything shifts — shoulders, waist, hips. A jacket that fits moves with you. Too tight and you'll see diagonal pull lines across the stomach. Too loose and the whole thing slides and drops off one shoulder.

**The collar.** The area closest to your face and the one that gets the least attention. One finger should slide comfortably between a buttoned shirt collar and your neck. And the jacket collar should sit flush against the shirt collar — a visible gap there, which has its own name, is the tell that a jacket doesn't fit however good the rest looks.

**The staircase.** Climbing exposes what standing never will. Tight trousers pull sharply above the knee; loose ones swing and gather behind it. The answer isn't slim or loose — it's moderate room through the seat and thighs with a gentle taper below the knee, and a slight break where the front just touches the shoe.

**The camera.** A photograph shows structure that a mirror hides at close range. Take front, side, and walking shots with the camera at waist or chest height — never from above, which distorts everything — and look at the big shapes first: where the jacket divides you, where the waistband sits, the line from trouser hem to shoe. Only then look at the watch and the pocket square.

### The eighth one is the best

**The closet test.** Look at what you actually reach for.

The pieces pushed to the back have usually been rejected by your body rather than your taste. You've unconsciously stopped choosing the collar that tightens after an hour, the waistband that needs adjusting all afternoon, the jacket you can't quite move in. Nobody makes that decision consciously and everybody makes it.

So compare your three most-worn pieces against three you avoid, and look for the pattern. Rise, collar size, sleeve, shoulder width — something will be consistent. That pattern is your actual size, and it's more accurate than anything printed on a label.

### What to do with the results

A near miss is usually cheap to fix. Sleeve length, trouser break, waist shaping — all routine tailoring, all worth doing on something you like.

But if the shoulders are wrong, or the proportions fight your body no matter what gets adjusted, let it go. There's no version of that garment that becomes yours, and keeping it just means owning something you'll never wear while believing you have more clothes than you do.

A few small things to try:

- Sit down in whatever you're wearing and watch the waistband.
- Check for a gap between your jacket collar and your shirt collar.
- Photograph yourself from chest height, not from above.
- Pull out the three things you never wear and work out what they share.
