# Simple Things Outlive Clever Ones

{{< lead >}}
The things that last decades have almost nothing in common except what they lack — no proprietary parts, no sealed housing, no features designed to expire. Durability turns out to be mostly an absence.
{{< /lead >}}

### Five that hold up

- **A cheap Casio.** The A158 or the F91W. Not a warranty story — just robustness, with decades of people reporting the same watches still going. The battery alone runs about ten years. Around $20–35 used, and the adjustable band means you never need a shop to size it. A G-Shock if you want the indestructible version.
- **A Rotring 600 pencil.** German, launched 1989, full metal body with a knurled grip. Takes standard 0.5 or 0.7 leads, so no proprietary anything. One weakness: drop it on the tip and it bends — but the tip is a replaceable part. The black one wears to brass at the edges, which looks better with age rather than worse.
- **A ThinkPad T480.** Widely considered the last really good one: removable battery *plus* an internal one so you can hot-swap, two drive slots, two RAM slots, replaceable keyboard, screen, trackpad. Standard Phillips screws throughout. A physical webcam shutter, which newer premium laptops still don't have. Upgraded and running Linux, ten to fifteen years is a realistic expectation.
- **An Opinel No. 8.** French, made since the late 1800s, about $20. Beechwood handle, carbon steel blade, a metal collar that twists to lock. No springs, no mechanism. The warranty is nearly meaningless and it doesn't matter, because there's essentially nothing in it that can fail. People are still using ones from the 1950s.
- **Goodyear-welted boots.** Red Wing Iron Rangers or anything built the same way. The point isn't the brand, it's the construction: the sole is stitched rather than glued, so it can be replaced over and over. Buy the method, not the label — Thursday and LL Bean's Katahdins qualify at lower prices.

### The pattern

Read those back and the common feature is refusal.

No proprietary lead. No glued sole. No sealed battery. No system that stops being supported. Every one of them is repairable because it was never complicated enough to be otherwise, and simplicity turns out to be the actual mechanism behind things lasting.

Which is a slightly deflating conclusion if you were hoping durability could be bought. Mostly it's chosen — by picking the version with fewer things in it.

### Buy them used

The part that makes this genuinely accessible rather than an expensive philosophy: over half of these were bought secondhand, often 30–50% below retail. Iron Rangers for about $180 instead of $500-plus. LL Bean boots for $85.

That inverts how people usually approach buy-it-for-life, which is to save up and buy new. If a thing is genuinely built to last, then a used one has already proven it — and the resale market is full of them precisely because they don't break.

Checking secondhand first isn't a compromise on this stuff. It's the sensible entry point, and it's how you find out whether you actually want the thing before paying full price.

### A few small things to try

- Look at something you've replaced more than twice and ask what keeps failing
- Check whether the next thing you buy uses standard parts or proprietary ones
- Search the secondhand market before buying anything built to last
- Ask whether a sole can be replaced before buying boots
