{{< lead >}}
Five dress shoes will cover almost everything, but only if you buy them in a particular order and — this is the part people miss — in deliberately different browns. Two pairs in near-identical shades is one wasted pair.
{{< /lead >}}

### Black comes in one shade, so buy it once

Start with the black Oxford. It's your formal anchor and it does business, funerals, and anything black tie.

If you're only ever buying one, make it a **black wholecut Oxford** — cut from a single piece of leather, with just one subtle seam at the back on an off-the-rack pair. With no medallion on the toe it's correct even for white tie, which nothing else on this list can claim.

The **cap-toe balmoral** without broguing is the more common classic and slightly less severe. Either wears with navy, charcoal, or grey suits, with a navy blazer and grey flannels, and with a dinner jacket.

Black comes in exactly one shade. That's why one pair is enough and why every subsequent purchase is a brown problem.

### Then the browns, spaced out

Space them deliberately, because for any given pair of trousers one shade will flatter noticeably more than the other. Two similar browns means you own one useful shoe and one understudy.

**A full-brogue wingtip Derby, in medium brown** — antique cognac is the reference. The open lacing makes it more forgiving than an Oxford, which matters more than you'd think when your feet swell on a long flight. The broguing pushes it casual, so it goes with suits, sport coats, corduroys, chinos, and denim about equally.

**A loafer, in a different brown** — something with a reddish or medium-brown undertone, clearly distinct from the Derby. Penny loafers are the safe version, named for the slot the coin went in; tassels read more modern than people expect. Good with seersucker and sockless in summer, and best avoided under a double-breasted suit. Fit matters more here than anywhere else, because there's no lacing holding your heel in — the last has to grip your mid-foot or you'll walk out of them.

**A double monk, in reddish brown** — clean last, no broguing, buckles sitting where they should. This is the most flexible thing on the list: jeans, slacks, a polo, a sweater, a t-shirt, or a suit. One coordination rule — match the belt leather to the shoe, and the belt buckle metal to the shoe buckle.

**A half-brogue Oxford, in a lighter medium brown or antique tan** — cap toe with broguing and a medallion, sitting exactly between the plain Oxford and the wingtip Derby. Formal enough for an office and brown enough for tweed, which makes it the pair that gets worn three seasons out of four.

### The formality ladder, so you can place anything

From dressiest down: **black Oxford → half-brogue Oxford → brown wingtip Derby → loafer → double monk.**

Two rules generate the whole ordering. Wholecuts and clean lasts read more formal. Broguing and open lacing read more casual. Once you can see those two variables, you can rank a shoe you've never encountered.

### The mistake worth avoiding

The classic beginner purchase is a discounted Goodyear-welted shoe bought because it's discounted and welted.

The trap is that a shoe can be well made and still be stylistically confused — a Derby with full-brogue wingtip detailing, in black, is a casual construction with casual decoration in the most formal colour available. It combines with nothing. Stiff, slightly too big, and never worn is a worse outcome than a cheaper shoe that goes with your trousers.

### Why five is the right number

Because the goal is combinations, not shoes. Each pair here pairs cleanly with things the others don't, so five gets you close to complete coverage and frees the rest of the budget for clothes.

A few small things to try:

- Put your brown shoes next to each other in daylight. If two look the same, one is redundant.
- Buy the black Oxford first if you don't have one, then stop buying black.
- Check your loafers actually grip your mid-foot.
- Match your belt to the shoe you wear most, not to the trousers.
