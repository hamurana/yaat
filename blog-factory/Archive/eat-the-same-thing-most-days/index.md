# Eat the Same Thing Most Days

{{< lead >}}
You make roughly 200 food decisions a day. Each one costs something, and the account is empty by evening — which is exactly when discipline is supposed to hold. The people who stay lean mostly stopped deciding.
{{< /lead >}}

### The argument is about systems, not food

Almost every diet is a set of rules applied by willpower, and willpower is a depreciating asset across a day. By 9pm — after work, commuting, children, and a few hundred small decisions — the resource the plan depends on is gone. This is why so many otherwise disciplined days end badly, and why the failure feels like a character problem when it's really a design problem.

The alternative is to remove the decisions rather than win them. Lock down breakfast, lunch, and weekday dinners so they require no thought, and reserve your decision budget for the meals that actually warrant it.

A repeated rotation of three to five meals is what most lean people quietly run. It isn't glamorous, and it isn't a diet in the usual sense — it's an operating system.

### Where the evidence supports this, and where it doesn't

This deserves a careful reading, because the claim circulating alongside this advice — that eating less varied food produces something like 37% more weight loss — is not something I can verify, and the literature is more interesting than that anyway.

What's reasonably supported: [dietary variety is associated with weight gain and dietary monotony with weight loss](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2161831322006147) in correlational and short-term experimental work, and [successful long-term weight-loss maintainers tend to eat with limited variety](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15919842/). Reducing the number of different snack foods available predicts lower overall calorie intake and greater weight loss.

What complicates it: the effect depends heavily on **which** foods you're restricting. Greater variety in fruits, vegetables and grains is associated with *less* overweight, and among people cutting calories, those eating a wider range of those foods lost more weight at six months and kept more off at two years. There's also evidence that limiting variety in one food group produces compensation elsewhere in the diet.

So the honest version is narrower than "eat monotonously." It's: **reduce variety in energy-dense, easily-overeaten foods, and keep variety high in vegetables, fruits, and whole grains.** That's a more useful rule than the blanket one, and it happens to be what a good repeated rotation looks like anyway.

The Blue Zones observation fits here too — Okinawan and Sardinian populations known for longevity eat largely the same foods across decades. Worth holding loosely, since Blue Zones research has attracted real methodological criticism, but the pattern is at least consistent.

### The shape of a rotation that works

**Meal one: front-load protein.** Around 50g of protein before 9am is the single most commonly skipped move. A workable version: 300g of low-fat Greek yoghurt, a scoop of whey protein, a tablespoon of psyllium husk, and 100g of frozen fruit — assembled the night before so the fruit thaws and the psyllium expands. Thirty seconds in the morning.

**Meal two: the low-effort one.** This is the meal to automate hardest, because it lands mid-workday when you have the least capacity to make good decisions. Whether that's a meal-prep service, a repeated assembled lunch, or something portable matters less than that it requires no thinking. Psyllium with a large glass of water 20–30 minutes beforehand blunts appetite and slows the glucose rise.

**Meal three: the one you actually cook.** 300g or more of lean protein — sirloin, salmon, chicken, lean beef — plus 100–200g of cruciferous vegetables and 100–200g of a single-ingredient carbohydrate like sweet potato, potato, rice, or legumes. An air fryer removes most of the friction.

The point of the anchor meal isn't nutritional so much as structural: it's the meal worth spending decisions on, and often the one eaten with other people.

### Why psyllium keeps appearing

It's doing real work rather than acting as filler. Psyllium is a soluble fibre that slows digestion and increases satiety, and it has [good evidence for lowering LDL cholesterol and apolipoprotein B](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(22)03007-6/fulltext) at doses around 10g daily. Taken before a meal it reduces how much you want afterwards — which is appetite management through physiology rather than resolve.

### Control is what buys flexibility

The counterintuitive part: rigidly automating Monday to Friday is what makes weekends relaxed. If you know precisely what five days look like, the other two need no tracking, no measuring, and no anxiety. Flexibility isn't the opposite of structure — it's what structure pays for.

**And don't overhaul everything at once.** Adopt one meal, run it for seven days, then add the second. Replacing your entire eating pattern on a Monday is the same willpower bet that failed before, just larger.

### Summary

Willpower runs out daily; systems don't. Reducing food decisions by running a repeated rotation is the mechanism behind most sustained leanness. The evidence supports restricting variety specifically in energy-dense foods while *keeping* variety high in vegetables, fruit and whole grains — the blanket "less variety is better" claim is not well supported and the widely-repeated 37% figure isn't something I could verify. Front-loading around 50g of protein before 9am, using psyllium for satiety and lipids, and cooking one anchor meal covers the practical structure.

**What to actually do:**

1. **Pick one meal and eat it every day for a week** before changing anything else.
2. **Get 50g of protein before 9am.** Assemble it the night before so morning-you has no decisions.
3. **Automate the midday meal hardest** — it lands when your decision budget is lowest.
4. **Keep vegetable and fruit variety high** even as you narrow everything else. That's where the evidence diverges from the slogan.
5. **Use psyllium before meals** for appetite, with the cholesterol benefit as a bonus.
6. **Leave weekends untracked.** Rigid weekdays are what make that affordable.
