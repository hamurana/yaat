# The Phrases That Quietly End Closeness

{{< lead >}}
"Get over it." "I don't care." "Fine." Three phrases that end a conversation without ending the relationship on paper — and if you use them often enough with someone close to you, the distance becomes the relationship.
{{< /lead >}}

### They're usually not cruelty

Worth saying up front: people who use these phrases mostly aren't unkind. They're protecting themselves from feeling exposed.

That's the uncomfortable part. It works. You do stay protected. You just pay for it in closeness, and the bill arrives slowly enough that it's hard to connect to the thing that caused it.

Anthony Hopkins' long estrangement from his daughter is the example that made this land for me — in one short interview clip he reaches for all three.

### Distance doesn't close by itself

When there's a gap between you and someone — from hurt, resentment, something old — it doesn't drift shut on its own. You either hold the distance or you climb toward them, a step at a time.

Where most people fail isn't refusing to try. It's making one half-vulnerable move, having it not land perfectly, and withdrawing. Hopkins had his wife send the invitation rather than sending it himself, after decades. That's an attempt, technically. It's also still protected.

### Stoicism makes a very good hiding place

This is the bit I found genuinely uncomfortable.

Lines like "I don't carry resentments, that's death" or "I'm not a victim, onward" sound grounded and mature. Sometimes they are. But they can also be a well-dressed way of not going near something that hurts.

The refusal to ever feel like a victim is a clever coping tool. It gets you through the hard part. Then afterwards it keeps telling you the same thing, long after it stopped being useful — and you can in fact sit with pain and work through it without it destroying you. That turns out to be a requirement for being any use to someone else who's hurting.

### Six steps that actually close a gap

- **Go first.** You reach out. Not through someone else.
- **Show you care**, even with no solution. "I do care, I just don't know what to do about it" instead of "I don't care." "Is there anything I can do to help us get past this?" instead of "get over it." "That makes me sad, but I'll respect what you need" instead of "fine."
- **Listen** without arguing or fixing. The bar is being able to say their feelings back to them well enough that they agree that's what they meant.
- **Own your part.** Not their whole version of events — just your actual share of it, without shrinking it. "I can see how much it hurt to be left by me that many times. I'm sorry I wasn't there."
- **Share your side** as a bridge, not an excuse. Hopkins' coldness traced back to his grandfather's "no use crying, onward." Saying that out loud makes two people instead of a villain and a victim.
- **Go easier on yourself.** How you talk to other people in pain is usually how you talk to yourself. If "get over it" comes out of you readily, it's probably been aimed inward for years first.

### Holding both at once

The people who seem best at this hold two things together: the part that says move forward, and the part that says it's fine to feel this and I'm here while you do.

Not one suppressing the other. Both. That seems to be the long, awkward, worthwhile work.

### A few small things to try

- Notice which of the three phrases is your default, and what you're protecting when you reach for it
- Replace one this week with a version that admits you don't have a fix
- Reach out yourself to someone you've let drift — no intermediaries
- When someone tells you something painful, say it back to them before saying anything else
