# Keep It Long Enough to Notice

{{< lead >}}
Three years between one everyday-carry video and the next, and the list got shorter rather than longer. That gap is the actual advice — skipping the yearly upgrade until you'd genuinely feel the difference when you finally switch.
{{< /lead >}}

### The rule underneath

Buy a small number of well-made things and then use them long enough that replacing one means something.

That inverts how most upgrading works. The annual refresh means you never find out whether the old thing was good, because it never got the chance to fail or to prove itself. Wait long enough and the next purchase is informed by three years of evidence rather than by a release date.

The proof point that keeps recurring is unglamorous: well-made items used daily for years still look close to new. That's not an argument for spending more, it's an argument for spending once.

### What survived the cull

- **A phone three years old**, in a case chosen for actual protection, with an upgrade planned around a real reason rather than a cycle.
- **A card holder** — one folded piece of leather, single snap, six to eight cards, no bulk. Fitted with a tracker after nearly losing a previous wallet down the back of a hotel sofa.
- **One watch that gets about 80% of the wear**, even on trips where others came along. That ratio is worth noticing: owning several and reaching for one is information about which one you actually like.
- **A bracelet and two rings**, kept because of what they mark rather than how they look — a wedding band, and one from the early days of doing the work.
- **Sunglasses with light see-through lenses**, chosen specifically so people can still see his eyes. That's a small and genuinely thoughtful reason to pick a pair.
- **A few bags, chosen by job** — commuting, travel, errands — rather than one bag doing everything badly.

### Two things I'd take from it

**Function is allowed to carry meaning.** Several of these are kept for sentiment as much as use, and the note doesn't pretend otherwise. An object that marks something is doing a real job, and it's more honest than justifying it on performance.

**Cohesion beats matching.** He won't swap a genuinely good item just to make the set look consistent, which is the correct call. A carry that all matches is a collection; a carry where everything works is a carry.

### The obvious caveat

The specific list runs Rolex, Hermès, Tiffany, Rimowa. That's a lot of money, and it's fair to notice that "buy once, keep for years" is easier to demonstrate at that price.

But the principle doesn't depend on it, and arguably shows better at the other end. A cheap watch worn for a decade demonstrates the same idea more convincingly than an expensive one. The question is only ever whether you'd notice its absence — and that's answered by how long you've had it, not what it cost.

### A few small things to try

- Skip one upgrade you were planning and note whether you actually miss it
- Look at what you reach for most and ask whether the rest is earning its place
- Put a tracker in whatever you'd be most upset to lose
- Keep one thing purely because of what it reminds you of, without justifying it
