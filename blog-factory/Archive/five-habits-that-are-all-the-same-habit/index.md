# Five Habits That Are All the Same Habit

{{< lead >}}
A shy doctor who never raised her hand ended up enjoying rooms full of strangers, and the change wasn't personality. It was five very small adjustments — and looked at together, they're one adjustment applied in five places.
{{< /lead >}}

### The five

- **Hold eye contact about two seconds longer than feels natural.** Not a stare — just a beat past comfortable. Holding roughly 60–70% of the time reads as more confident and more trustworthy. Tested on a barista, it made her smile wider; used with patients, their shoulders came down.
- **Reset your posture before you speak.** One second: uncross the arms, drop the shoulders, turn your torso toward the person. Your body has already introduced you before you say anything.
- **Use their name three times.** Once when you greet them, once naturally in the middle, once when you leave. Hearing your own name is a small hit of something pleasant, and saying it out loud immediately is also how you stop forgetting names. Don't overdo it — "so Kim, tell me Kim" is unsettling.
- **Pause for one breath.** Before answering, or right after making a point. People who pause read as more thoughtful and more authoritative. She learned this in radiology training, making herself wait before reading a scan rather than blurting; a senior colleague told her she seemed unusually composed under pressure.
- **Ask one more question.** When someone tells you something, go one layer deeper. "What's been the best part of the work lately" rather than "how's work." "What do you like most about living there" rather than "where are you from."

### The one thing underneath

Every one of those slows you down and points you outward.

The longer eye contact, the breath before answering, the extra question — none of them are performances. They're all the same act of not rushing, and of aiming your attention at the other person rather than at how you're coming across.

Which is why the last one is the most useful. Most people try to be interesting. The inversion is to be interested, and the reliable side effect is that the other person relaxes, opens up, and then turns the curiosity back on you. You get to be interesting by demonstrating that they are.

### One claim I'd hold loosely

The posture point is framed partly as changing how *you* feel, not just how you're read. The "others read you differently" half is uncontroversial. The "you'll feel different inside" half has had a rougher time in the years since it became popular, and I'd treat it as unsettled rather than established.

That doesn't undercut the habit. Standing open rather than folded still changes what people see, and it costs you a second.

### Why small beats ambitious here

None of these require becoming someone else, which is the reason they survive contact with a real week.

A plan to "be more confident" has nothing to actually do on Tuesday morning. Holding eye contact two seconds longer while ordering coffee does. The scale is deliberately trivial, and trivial is what makes it repeatable.

### A few small things to try

- Add two seconds of eye contact the next time you order something
- Take one breath before answering the next question you're asked
- Use one person's name three times today, lightly
- Ask one follow-up question you'd normally skip
