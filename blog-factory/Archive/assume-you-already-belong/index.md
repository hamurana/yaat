# Assume You Already Belong

{{< lead >}}
Most social nerves come down to one quiet belief: that you don't quite belong here. Fix that and the techniques stop mattering, because there's nothing left for them to paper over.
{{< /lead >}}

### Conditional belonging is the problem

Almost everyone belongs *somewhere*. With old friends, in familiar rooms, around people at roughly your level you're relaxed and funny and fine.

Then the room changes — someone you admire, a group that intimidates you a bit — and you shrink. That's conditional belonging. It isn't that your social skills left. It's that underneath, you don't believe you're supposed to be there, and everything you do gets shaped by that.

Unconditional is the other version: wherever you go, you belong. When someone actually has it, the hard situations just stop being hard.

### The question that switches it on

The quickest way in is asking yourself: *what if this stranger was already my friend?*

Then watch what happens to your greeting. It changes on its own, without you managing it.

IShowSpeed meeting the sprinter Noah Lyles is the clearest version of this — no careful "nice to meet you," just grabbing him, joking straight away, treating him like someone he'd known for years. The whole quality of that interaction came from the assumption he walked in with.

And it applies at every level. He treats a friend's mum with the same warmth as a celebrity. That consistency — not adjusting your warmth based on someone's status — might be the useful part of the whole thing.

### Enthusiasm on its own can backfire

Worth a caveat here. Enthusiasm alone can read as trying too hard, or as wanting approval, and that pushes people back.

What seems to work is enthusiasm *plus* a willingness to be playful — warm toward someone while also teasing them a bit and not treating the conversation as important.

The line between friendly and hostile teasing is simpler than it looks:

- Friendly banter isn't trying to win. It keeps a game going.
- You can take it as well as give it, and you're smiling either way.
- Hostile teasing is trying to gain something at the other person's expense.

If you notice you're trying to *win* an exchange rather than play in it, that's usually the belonging thing showing up again. Wanting to come out on top is what people do when they're not sure they're wanted in the room.

### What it looks like when it's missing

The Brie Larson example is a good illustration. In settings where she seems unsure of her place, jokes come from trying to establish status and don't land. In interviews where she's clearly comfortable, the same kind of banter flows fine.

Same person, same ability. Different belief underneath.

### It reinforces itself

The nice thing is that this loops. Assume familiarity, people respond warmly, that warmth becomes evidence, the belief gets easier to hold. It builds on itself as long as you go first.

Which means the entry cost is small. You don't need to become someone else — just to act, once, on the assumption you'd hold if you already belonged, and see what comes back.

### A few small things to try

- Treat one stranger today like someone you already know
- Before walking into a room that intimidates you, ask what you'd do differently if you belonged there
- Watch whether your warmth changes with someone's status — and try to flatten it
- If you catch yourself trying to win a bit of banter, let it go and keep the game running instead
