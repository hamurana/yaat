{{< lead >}}
People work out how much weight to give you in about a sentence. Not from what you said — from how you left it. And the most reliable sign of someone worth listening to isn't a good voice. It's a willingness to stop talking.
{{< /lead >}}

### The gap does most of the work

Silence is uncomfortable, so people fill it. That filling is the tell. A pause is doing something specific in each spot it lands:

- Before a phrase, it gives that phrase weight.
- After a sentence, it gives the other person room to take it in.
- Before you answer a question, it reads as respect rather than reflex. Answering instantly looks like you weren't really considering it.
- Instead of a run-on, it buys you thinking time and stops the rambling.
- After someone else finishes, it opens space for them to say the deeper version of what they just said.

That last one is the sneaky one. Most people never hear anyone's second thought, because they jump in before it arrives.

### Say less and each word lands harder

A conversation is a container. The more words you pack in, the harder the other person has to work to find your actual point.

There's a decent three-step for when you're put on the spot — pause, ask, then the one thing:

- **Pause.** Don't start talking while you're still deciding.
- **Ask** a clarifying question. "Quick update on the campaign?" could mean five different things, and answering all five is how a two-minute reply becomes half an hour.
- **The one thing.** Start your answer with those words. It forces you to pick a single point instead of laying out everything you know.

### Land your sentences lower

Ending every sentence on a rising pitch turns statements into questions. In Australia it reads as cooperative, an invitation to keep going. In the US it mostly gets heard as uncertainty, whether or not you feel any.

The fix is small and physically odd at first: let the last few words drop in pitch. It sounds blunt in your own head and normal to everyone else.

### Somewhere to put your eyes

Eye contact goes wrong in both directions — darting around, or locking on so hard it turns into a staring contest.

- One-on-one, move slowly between three points: left eye, right eye, bridge of the nose. Giving your attention a small job takes the strangeness out of it.
- In a group, one person, one thought, then move. Hold someone's eyes for a full sentence or idea, then shift to someone else.
- What to avoid: scanning left to right like a machine, working the room in a fixed A-B-C pattern, or looking just above everyone's heads.

### Make people see it

Information gets forgotten. Pictures don't. Three things carry nearly all of this:

- **Be specific.** "A stressful meeting with senior leaders, and the CEO was there" beats "it was stressful."
- **Compare it to something.** "Sent into battle on my own with a handful of twigs" beats "I'm overwhelmed."
- **Tell it as a story** rather than stating the conclusion.

### One thing I'd frame differently

This usually gets sold as a list of bad habits to cut. Only the pitch one really is a habit. The rest are things you have to build, and they're harder in a different way — nobody notices you adding a pause, so there's no feedback telling you it's working. It just quietly is.

A few small things to try this week:

- Let one answer start three seconds late.
- Drop your pitch on the last five words of a sentence.
- When someone finishes talking, count to two before you respond.
- Swap one vague description for a specific one.
