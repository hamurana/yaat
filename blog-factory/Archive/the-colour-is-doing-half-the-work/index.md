Polo advice nearly always stops at fit. But two polos can fit identically and land completely differently, and the thing separating them is colour. Faded warm tones drain your face. Deep saturated ones do the opposite, and they're slimming besides.
{{< /lead >}}

### The tones that work against you

Washed-out warm colours are the problem — pale yellow, light orange, some reds. They sit badly against older skin and read as tired rather than relaxed.

It's rarely the colour on the hanger that's wrong. It's the colour after thirty washes. A polo that started sharp can fade into exactly the shade you'd never have bought.

### The ones that hold up

- **Navy.** The default for a reason.
- **Forest green.** Quietly the best of the lot, and underused.
- **Burgundy.** Warm without being loud.
- **Mid-grey and white.** Easy, and they let everything else do the talking.

All of these sit well with chinos or trousers, photograph properly, and suit most skin tones. Darker and more saturated also reads slimmer, navy and forest green especially — a real effect, not a trick of the light.

### Patterns, briefly

A fine two-tone stripe is the ceiling. Past that you're into bold stripes, big chest logos, and graphic prints, all of which pull attention to the shirt and away from you.

### Colour can't rescue a bad fit

It can carry a lot, but not that.

- Shoulder seam sits at the **edge** of your shoulder, not down the arm.
- Sleeve ends at **mid-bicep** — not at the elbow, not bunched up top.
- Chest is relaxed, not tight and not billowing. Raise your arms and the hem shouldn't ride up.
- Hem lands just below the waistband. Long enough to tuck cleanly, short enough to leave out.

Between sizes, size **down** and have the sides taken in. It's a cheap alteration with a bigger effect than most people expect. Sizing up to cover a midsection does the opposite of what you want — a box shape announces the thing it was meant to hide.

### Two shop tests worth thirty seconds

**Fold the collar over.** It should spring back. A ribbed, tightly knit collar holds its shape for years; a soft woven one goes limp fast. Once a collar curls or waves and won't sit flat after a wash, that polo is finished. No amount of ironing brings it back.

**Pinch the fabric.** It should feel like something and spring back when released. Piqué cotton — that slightly textured weave on a classic polo — is still the one to beat. It breathes, holds structure, and survives washing. Thin clingy jersey and heavy synthetic blends pill early and cling in the wrong places. This doesn't need to be expensive. Mid-range cotton is completely fine.

### Where the advice overreaches

The usual framing is that certain colours *age* you, which is a bit absolute. Pale yellow isn't a crime and plenty of people wear it well.

The narrower claim is the true one: as your hair and skin lose contrast, low-contrast clothes stop giving you any edges. That's why saturated colours suddenly work better than they did at thirty — not because pastels became forbidden, but because you now supply less of the contrast yourself and the shirt has to supply more.

A few small things to try:

- Take two polos to a window and hold each up to your face. The difference is usually obvious and takes five seconds.
- Go through the drawer for faded ones and be honest about which have crossed over.
- Buy the next one in forest green instead of another navy.
- Fold every collar you own. Anything that doesn't spring back has already retired.
