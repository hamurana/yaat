# Never Spend the Principal

{{< lead >}}
Old money isn't a look you can buy, which is inconvenient for everyone selling it. Underneath the aesthetic there are about five habits, and only one of them requires having money already.
{{< /lead >}}

### The financial rule

Live off the interest, never the principal.

That's the whole mechanism behind families staying comfortable for four or five generations, and it's brutally simple. The capital is untouchable. You spend what it produces. Every generation that breaks the rule shortens the timeline for the ones after.

It scales down, too. The version available to most people is that savings are not a fund you dip into for a nice weekend — they're a thing that generates something, eventually, if left alone long enough to compound.

### The wardrobe rule

Buy for the decade rather than the season, and judge everything on cost per wear. The most expensive thing you own is the one you wore once.

The goal, oddly, is to look like you've had your clothes for ten years. Heavy Irish linen that gets better as it creases, high-twist wool that breathes through travel, Egyptian cotton. Steve McQueen in a navy Harrington, Paul Newman in a soft unlined blazer — neither wearing anything with a name on it. They wore shapes, not labels.

### The composure rule

The line worth keeping from all of this: composure is the most expensive thing you can own.

In practice that means not reacting, being comfortable in a silence, being decent to staff, and being able to walk away from any table where you're not being treated well. None of that has a price and all of it is harder to acquire than a jacket.

### The hobby rule

What you do on a Saturday morning says more than what you drive. The distinction offered is between hobbies that build a skill and hobbies that just consume — sailing is really navigation, polo is really horsemanship.

That's a genuinely useful filter and it doesn't need a boat. Anything where you're worse at it now than you'll be in a year counts.

### Where this gets silly

I should be straight about the rest of it. There's a private villa in Beirut, a family estate in the Scottish Highlands, vintage Porsches as an inflation hedge, watch collecting for horological investment value.

That isn't a philosophy, it's a shopping list for people who already arrived, and dressing it up as a mindset is how these videos work. Treating a 911 as a store of value is only available to someone who can afford to be wrong about it.

Strip that out and what remains is unglamorous and actually transferable: don't touch the capital, buy few things and keep them a decade, pick hobbies that make you competent at something, and stay calm. Four habits, none requiring an estate.

The last one — seeking a teammate rather than a trophy, offering a life that's already complete rather than a ride — is good, and slightly undercut by arriving in a video about which Porsche to buy.

### A few small things to try

- Work out what your savings would produce if you never touched the principal
- Divide the cost of your last clothing purchase by the times you'll realistically wear it
- Pick one hobby where you're accumulating a skill rather than a collection
- Practise being the calmest person at the table once this week
