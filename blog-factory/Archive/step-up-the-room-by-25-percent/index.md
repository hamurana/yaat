# Step Up the Room by 25%

{{< lead >}}
You're not competing with the best-dressed men on the internet. You're competing with the nine or ten other men in whatever room you walk into. Work out the dress code, then go about a quarter above it. That's the entire strategy.
{{< /lead >}}

### Start with an honest assessment

Four questions before buying anything:

- **What does your life actually require?** Job, hobbies, climate, where you live. Most wardrobe mistakes are clothes bought for a life you don't have.
- **What message do you want to send?** Decide this, because your clothes are sending one regardless.
- **What's already in there?** Keep what fits and what people compliment. A shirt that fits well is a template — take it shopping and match it.
- **What can you actually spend?** Prioritise what's visible: shirts, trousers, shoes, jackets. Nobody's assessing your underwear.

### Fit, then colour

**A tailored $200 suit beats a badly fitted $2,000 one**, every time, and this is the single highest-return thing in the whole subject. Have off-the-rack pieces altered as a default rather than an exception.

Collars matter more than people realise — anything with a collar near your face beats a collarless shirt. A pointed collar balances a rounder face; a spread collar suits a narrower one.

On colour, treat strong shades **like spice**. An average wardrobe is roughly 20% white, 18% grey, 14% navy, 13% black, and the rest scattered — that's not a failure of imagination, it's a workable base. Keep the expensive essentials conservative and experiment on cheaper shirts and accessories.

For which colours suit you: look at your veins. Clearly blue suggests a cool tone, so silver and steel work. Greenish suggests warm, so gold. And repeating your eye or hair colour in what you wear makes your face the thing people look at.

### The wardrobe maths

Four shoes, four shirts, four pairs of trousers, four jackets. If everything genuinely works with everything, that's sixteen pieces producing up to **256 outfits**.

That number is the argument for interchangeability over quantity. It only holds if you buy for compatibility rather than for individual appeal, which is a different instinct entirely.

There's also a decent way to justify spending: **value = (times worn × how it makes you feel) ÷ price**. A £300 boot worn constantly is cheaper by that measure than a neglected cheap pair, which is a more honest calculation than either "it was a bargain" or "it's an investment piece."

### Why it works at all

The claim is enclothed cognition — that clothes change how you perform, not just how you're read. The example given is the white-coat study, where students told their coat was a doctor's outperformed those told it was a painter's smock.

I'd hold the specific study lightly; the broader idea that what you wear affects how you carry yourself is easy enough to verify on yourself, and doesn't need a citation to be useful.

The more concrete version is the fireman thought experiment. Two men run into a conference shouting "get out." One's dishevelled, one's in uniform. Same words, different room response. Clothing is doing authority work whether or not you've decided to use it.

### The part people skip

You learn this by wearing things, getting it slightly wrong, and adjusting. Find icons who share your build and colouring rather than copying whoever's most famous. Bring in your own heritage if you have something to draw on — a guayabera, a tartan, a dashiki.

Then just get 1% better at it repeatedly, which over a year compounds into something quite different.

### A few small things to try

- Find out the dress code for your next event, then plan one notch above it
- Take your best-fitting shirt shopping as a measuring template
- Check your veins and pick metals accordingly
- Have one thing tailored this month instead of buying something new
