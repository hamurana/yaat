# Earn Attention Before You Ask For It

{{< lead >}}
Most talks are lost in the first ten seconds, and not because the speaker is bad. It's because they opened with their name, their job title, and some context — and the room quietly switched off before anything started.
{{< /lead >}}

### The problem with the safe opening

Introductions are what everyone expects, which is exactly why they don't work. An audience decides whether to pay attention very early, and a predictable start gives them nothing to decide in your favour.

Obama's "I'm Michelle's husband" works for one reason: it breaks the pattern the room was braced for. Every technique below is a version of the same move — do something unexpected before you ask for anything.

### Five ways in

- **Ask a real question.** Something they have to actually think about. "What's the scariest thing you've ever done?" A question hijacks the brain — it starts hunting for an answer whether or not anyone speaks. They're engaged before they chose to be.
- **Say something surprising.** A statistic or claim that cuts against what people assume, so there's a beat of *wait, is that right?* That doubt is attention. Delivery matters more than usual here — say it slowly, and pause both before and after. "400… the average office desk has 400 times more bacteria than a toilet seat." The pause is what lets it land.
- **Start inside a story.** Two of the most-watched TED talks ever open this way. Stories work because people stop assessing you and start watching a film instead. The mistake is background — skip it entirely. Where are you, when is it, what are you doing, what's going wrong. Drop them straight into the scene.
- **Promise something.** Everyone in the room is quietly asking what's in it for them, so answer it. Not what you'll cover — what they'll walk out with. Not "today I'll talk about body language" but "by the end of this you'll know how to look calm in a high-pressure room."
- **Do something instead of saying something.** The hardest and the most memorable. It needn't be theatrical — walk on and say nothing for five seconds. Hold up an object without explaining it. Write one word on a flip chart before you speak. Curiosity before a single sentence.

### The thing they share

All five are pattern interrupts placed at the top, before the audience has finished deciding about you.

That timing is the whole point. Once a room has settled into not-listening, you're working uphill for the rest of the talk. Ten seconds earlier, it costs almost nothing.

### One caution

There's a version of this that goes wrong: surprise with nothing behind it. A shocking statistic unconnected to your point, or a dramatic pause that turns out to be the most interesting thing you do.

The opening is a promise. It has to be one the rest of the talk keeps, otherwise you've bought attention on credit and spend the next twenty minutes losing it back.

### A few small things to try

- Cut your name and title from the opening entirely — put them later, if at all
- Write your first line as a question the room would actually have to think about
- If you're using a statistic, practise the pause on both sides of it
- Say what they'll leave with, not what you'll cover
