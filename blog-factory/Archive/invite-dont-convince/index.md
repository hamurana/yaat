{{< lead >}}
Trying to convince someone is a way of telling them you need something from them. That's the part that pushes people away — not the argument. Say where you're going and let people decide. You end up with fewer debates and better company.
{{< /lead >}}

### The difference is small and it's everything

Inviting sounds like: *I'm going this way, who wants to come?*

Convincing is what happens after they've answered and you keep going. It's not the pitch — it's the refusal to accept the reply. And people feel that immediately, because it means their answer wasn't really allowed to be no.

Filtering works better than convincing. Show what you're actually about, let the wrong people opt out, and stop spending energy dragging anyone across the line.

One thing this doesn't cover: saying clearly what you need from a partner or a close friend isn't convincing. That's just being honest about your own terms, and going quiet about it to keep the peace is its own problem.

### Asking, with nothing to trade

There's a story I think about a lot. A group of friends had a list of things they wanted to do before they died, and one of them was to play basketball with Barack Obama. No connections. Nothing to offer in return.

So they walked around Washington DC and asked people. Just told them what they were trying to do and asked for help. Within about three days that got them into a senator's office.

He flaked on the first arrangement. They aired the episode anyway. He saw it, and asked an aide why he hadn't played basketball with those guys yet. They got the game.

None of that was persuasion. It was saying the true thing out loud to enough people. Most of us never find out what that would do, because we never say it.

### Small lies are more expensive than they look

Catch someone exaggerating a story you happen to know the truth of, and something changes. You don't stop trusting them exactly — you just start quietly discounting everything, including the parts that were true.

It runs in the other direction too. "I'm on the way" when you haven't left teaches a part of your own brain that you're sometimes not to be relied on. That leaks into how you say everything else, including the things you mean completely.

There's a harder version of this one: not lying to a friend, but not raising the thing you know you should raise, because it would be uncomfortable. Withholding is quieter than lying and it costs about the same.

### Go first, in the right order

Being the first to make the joke, give the compliment, or admit the awkward thing lowers what the room thinks is normal. Everyone relaxes about a notch. It's the most useful thing you can do as the person who arrived first.

But the order matters and it's the part people skip:

- Fun and trust first.
- Then compliments.
- Then anything genuinely vulnerable.

Skip to the third with people who haven't done the first two and it isn't going first. It's unloading on strangers, and the room gets tighter rather than looser.

### The one I'd hold loosely

There's advice here about skipping small talk and telling people early what you deeply care about. I half agree.

Small talk is often how people check whether you're safe to talk to. It's boring on purpose. Announcing a mission to someone still running that check reads as intensity, and intensity early is hard to come back from.

Share the real thing sooner than you're comfortable with. Just not before they've decided you're a person.

A few small things to try:

- Say the thing you want out loud to one person who can't obviously help.
- Notice the next time you keep arguing after someone has already answered. Stop there.
- Tell a small truth you'd normally smooth over.
- Be first with the joke.
