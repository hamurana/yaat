# The Fitness You Keep Is the Boring Kind

{{< lead >}}
Getting in shape is slow. Getting out of shape is fast. The difference isn't the programme — it's that people revert to who they were. Which is why the durable version starts from identity and ends with habits dull enough to survive a bad month.
{{< /lead >}}

### Start from who, not how

Most fitness advice is tactical: this split, that protocol, these macros. Tactics are fine, and they're not what fails.

What fails is the underlying bet — that you'll force yourself through something indefinitely. Habits that stick are the ones attached to a decision about the kind of person you are, because those become non-negotiable rather than optional. A programme you push through has an expiry date built in.

The practical consequence is to work backwards. Decide the identity, then pick the smallest habits consistent with it, then add.

### Walking is the underrated one

For most people walking beats running, and the reasons are mechanical.

Every pound of excess body weight translates to roughly **four pounds of pressure through the knees and six through the hips**. Asking someone carrying extra weight to run it off loads the exact joints most likely to end the attempt. Walking is joint-friendly, accessible without instruction or equipment, lowers cortisol rather than raising it, sharpens cognition, and — importantly for anyone trying to lose fat — doesn't drive the compensatory hunger that harder cardio does.

If you want walking to also build aerobic capacity, there's a well-studied protocol. **Interval walking training**, developed by Nose and Masuki at Shinshu University, alternates **three minutes at a brisk pace with three minutes easy**, repeated for around 30 minutes, four or more days a week. [In a study of 679 participants averaging 65 years old](https://www.mayoclinicproceedings.org/article/S0025-6196(19)30473-2/abstract), five months of this raised peak aerobic capacity by **14%** and lowered a lifestyle-disease score by **17%**. The analysis also found that accumulated time at the *high* intensity, not total walking volume, drove the result — fifteen hard minutes inside a thirty-minute session beats forty-five minutes of cruising.

Nearly everyone can do this, which is the point.

### All-day movement beats the single session

Naturally lean people frequently accumulate 10,000–15,000 steps daily without describing any of it as exercise. Average figures in sedentary populations sit closer to 4,000.

That gap isn't a workout gap — it's a background-activity gap, and one gym hour doesn't close it. The mental model worth borrowing: circulation is like a drain. Keep things moving through the day and they flow; run hard for an hour then sit for ten and things go stagnant.

Practically, this means restructuring the day rather than adding to it. Walking meetings. A treadmill desk if you can. Taking calls on your feet. Someone moving from 2,000 steps to walking through their meetings typically ends the day with *more* energy, not less — which is the counterintuitive part.

### Train legs and grip specifically

Muscle declines roughly **3–8% per decade from around 30**, accelerating after 60, and aggressive dieting without resistance training accelerates it further.

**Legs decide independence.** The difference between managing stairs at 80 and calculating every one of them is leg strength, and it's determined by what you did decades earlier. Spend time among people in their seventies and eighties and the divide is visible immediately — who grips the rail, who needs the walker, who moves freely.

**Grip is worth training as a byproduct.** Not with a squeeze gadget, but through loaded carries — a reasonable target is carrying your own body weight for a minute. That builds forearms, trunk, posture, and the real-world capacity to carry things, and it transfers into deadlifts and pull-ups.

And it genuinely isn't too late at any age. Beginners see the fastest gains, and people starting resistance training in their sixties, seventies and beyond build measurable strength. The tissue stays responsive long after most people assume it doesn't.

### The gym rules that remove friction

**The only habit that matters at first is opening the door.** Not the session quality, not the programme — attendance. Everything else can be optimised later.

**Use machines for the first four to six weeks.** They require less skill and dictate the movement path, so you can work hard without a technique apprenticeship. Free weights come later.

**Keep the same exercises for months, not weeks.** Twelve to sixteen weeks minimum, up to a year. Constant variation prevents the progressive overload that actually drives adaptation.

**When a lift stalls and starts feeling hard, don't switch it.** That's the signal you've reached the productive part. Add smaller increments and continue.

### On calories, honestly

If weight isn't moving, you aren't in a deficit — the arithmetic doesn't negotiate. But that's not a character verdict, because tracking is genuinely inaccurate for most people: untracked bites, misjudged portions, photo-estimating apps that vary wildly.

The useful framing: calories are a *measure*, not a cause. Money isn't the cause of debt; spending is. And the body actively resists a deficit — ghrelin rises, leptin falls, hunger increases. Tracking combined with external accountability works considerably better than tracking alone.

### Summary

Durable fitness starts from identity rather than programming, because programmes get abandoned and identities don't. Walking outperforms running for most people on joint load, cortisol, and hunger, and interval walking — three minutes brisk, three minutes easy, for 30 minutes — raised peak aerobic capacity 14% over five months in adults averaging 65. All-day movement matters more than a single session. Legs determine independence in old age; grip strength tracks total muscle and is best built through loaded carries. Muscle falls 3–8% per decade from 30, and beginners at any age gain quickly.

**What to actually do:**

1. **Decide the identity first**, then attach the smallest habit to it.
2. **Walk daily, and try interval walking** — three minutes brisk, three easy, 30 minutes total.
3. **Restructure the day for movement** rather than adding a session — walking meetings, calls on your feet.
4. **Train legs deliberately.** They decide whether you stay independent.
5. **Add loaded carries**, working toward your body weight for one minute.
6. **Start on machines, keep the same exercises for months**, and when a lift gets hard, add small increments instead of switching.
7. **If you track calories, add accountability.** Tracking alone is unreliable.
