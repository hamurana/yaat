# Dress for the Message, Not the Attention

{{< lead >}}
There's a story in here about a very fit friend at a conference who could turn heads in a tight shirt. The point isn't that he shouldn't — it's that turning heads and being taken seriously are different goals, and summer makes it easy to pick the wrong one by accident.
{{< /lead >}}

### The nine

- **Defaulting to flip-flops.** Perforated leather shoes breathe genuinely well — the holes are there for that — and worn with no-show socks they're barely warmer. A horse-bit loafer with linen trousers and a plain polo does more than any amount of sandal.
- **All-white, all at once.** White linen shirt, white chinos, white sneakers are all excellent *separately*. Together they're a costume, and the full look belongs at an actual white party.
- **Undershirts worn as shirts.** The lightweight deep-V things sold in packs go *under* something. Sleeveless versions and tank tops are gym clothes, and gym clothes should stay at the gym.
- **The wrong sunglasses, unprotected.** Three shapes cover nearly everyone: aviators, whose teardrop suits most faces; wayfarers, a design that's survived fifty years; and clubmasters, which give contrast without shouting. Then keep them in a soft case — leaving a pair on your chest and walking indoors is how lenses get scratched.
- **Sunglasses indoors.** Push them onto your head or into the case.
- **Board shorts.** Around 80% of men should skip them. They came from 1960s surf culture where there was a reason for the length, then got mass-produced in the eighties cut for thin, athletic bodies. They exaggerate a stomach and make a shorter, heavier build look stouter.
- **Not switching to classic trunks.** Elastic waist, shorter inseam. If you're 5'6" or 5'7", the shorter cut does a lot for your proportions.
- **Cargo shorts as the default.** Fine as utility wear for a day out with kids. As the everyday choice they're tired. A shorter, non-cargo cut in light grey or tan with a polo is the swap.
- **Anything too tight.** The look that arrives quietly after twenty or thirty pounds. If there's material in the seams, have it let out. If there isn't, put it away rather than wearing it.

### The idea underneath

Almost every item is the same decision: *what do I want this to say?*

Too-tight clothing on a genuinely fit man isn't a fit problem, it's a message problem — it broadcasts one thing very loudly in a room where you'd rather be known as the capable one. The cargo shorts say something too, just less deliberately.

That reframing is more useful than a list of bans, because it survives contexts the list doesn't cover. Not "is this allowed" but "is this what I want the first ten seconds to communicate."

### Where I'd soften it

The board shorts figure — 80% of men — is the kind of number that sounds researched and probably isn't. And "most men shouldn't wear X" advice tends to assume a fairly narrow idea of what a body should look like.

The defensible version is smaller and more useful: clothes cut for a build unlike yours will fight you, and swim shorts are one of the few places men never think about cut at all. That's worth knowing without needing a percentage attached.

### A few small things to try

- Buy a soft case for your sunglasses today
- Try classic trunks alongside your board shorts and look at both in a mirror
- Move the sleeveless things to the gym bag permanently
- Before dressing for something that matters, decide what you want it to say first
