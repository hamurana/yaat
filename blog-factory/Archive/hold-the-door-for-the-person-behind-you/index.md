Hold a door for your date and let it swing shut on whoever's behind her, and you've just told her everything. Not that you have bad manners — that your manners are aimed at her. There's one test underneath every etiquette slip worth worrying about: would you still be doing this if nobody who mattered was watching?
{{< /lead >}}

### The seven that get noticed

- **Courtesies with an audience of one.** The door, the coat, the chair — good, unless they only happen when she's looking. Do them for the person behind you, the older man at the next table, the person with their hands full.
- **Complimenting only how she looks.** It's the compliment she's had most often from people who weren't paying attention. Say something about how she thinks, how she treats people, how she listens. That one requires having noticed, which is the point.
- **Being underdressed for what you planned.** If she's dressed for the evening and you're dressed for a hardware shop, she'll spend the first twenty minutes recalibrating what this is. Mismatched effort is its own message.
- **Having no plan.** A vague evening pushes all the logistics onto her, and she can't relax while she's managing the schedule. Have a time, a place, and something to do next if it's going well.
- **How you speak to staff.** This is the one people watch hardest, particularly anyone who's worked those jobs. Kindness that depends on what someone can do for you isn't kindness, it's an account being managed, and everyone can tell the difference.
- **Eyes moving around the room.** It reads as boredom no matter what's actually going on in your head. Nobody gets credit for the intention behind a wandering gaze.
- **Turning it into an interview.** Questions stacked on questions, with nothing offered back, feels like being processed.

### The fix for the last one is a swap, not more questions

Pair every question with something of your own. An observation, an opinion, a small admission — anything that hands over a bit of yourself.

Otherwise you've built a very polite one-way arrangement where she's expected to keep being open and you're expected to keep nodding. Connection needs both people slightly exposed. Only asking is a way of staying safe while looking interested.

### Two habits nearly nobody has

Standing when she leaves the table and standing again when she comes back. It's very old-fashioned and it's noticed every single time, often by other tables.

And offering to take her coat properly — *may I help you with your coat?* — rather than waiting to see if she manages it.

Neither costs anything. Both are rare enough now to register.

### The eye contact thing is fixable, quickly

Worth saying because it's the one people assume is a personality trait. It isn't. Scanning a room is a habit, usually formed by years of half-attention, and it responds to a few weeks of deliberate practice.

The improvement doesn't stay in dating either. It shows up with friends, colleagues, everyone. Attention is a general-purpose skill and most people never practise it directly.

### What all seven have in common

None of these are really about etiquette. They're about whether your behaviour is consistent when there's nothing in it for you.

Manners that switch on for one person are a performance, and performances have a tell — they're slightly too good in one direction and absent in the other. Manners that are just how you operate don't have a tell, because there's nothing being switched.

That's a slower thing to build than a list of rules. It's also the only version that survives a second date.

A few small things to try:

- Hold the next door for whoever's behind you, whether or not you're with someone.
- Compliment something that isn't appearance.
- Pair your next question with an observation of your own.
- Learn your server's name and use it once.
