# Past a Point, the Collection Owns You

{{< lead >}}
Antonio Centeno stopped counting his fragrance bottles somewhere past 700. The real number was over a thousand. He's now selling nearly all of it — and his reasons apply to almost anything people accumulate.
{{< /lead >}}

### The four problems

**It stopped being manageable.** Even with systems and family helping, bottles went missing, things didn't get put back. There's a size where organising the collection becomes its own job.

**There was more than a lifetime of it.** He worked out roughly 443 years of fragrance at normal use. And that assumed nothing new arrived, which wasn't realistic — companies were sending ten to twenty bottles a month. It was growing faster than any human could get through it.

**The money was just sitting there.** Even at a conservative $50 a bottle across 400 bottles, that's $20,000 on a shelf doing nothing.

**Perspective went with it.** Two parts to this. An opened bottle degrades as air gets in, so more bottles used more slowly means more of them quietly going off. And owning hundreds of something makes you a strange person to take advice from — the average man wants three to ten fragrances he can rely on, and someone with a thousand has lost the ability to think like him.

### The inversion

That last point is the one I keep turning over, because it applies well beyond fragrance.

There's a threshold where a collection flips. Below it you own the things and use them. Above it they own you — they need storing, sorting, insuring, thinking about. The pleasure gets replaced by admin, and it happens gradually enough that you don't notice the day it turned.

Books do this. Tools do it. Clothes definitely do it. Anything where acquiring is a bit easier than using.

### Worth being honest about the source

This particular video is largely an ad for a resale platform called Recented, which doesn't make the observation wrong but is worth knowing.

The underlying point holds without the pitch: the best version of a collection is the one that gets used, not the one that fills a room. And constraint tends to improve judgment rather than limit it — someone choosing between five things thinks harder about the choice than someone choosing between five hundred.

### A few small things to try

- Pick a category you accumulate in and roughly work out how long it'd take to use what you own
- Ask what the pile is worth if sold, and whether you'd rather have that
- Notice whether managing the collection has quietly become part of the hobby
- Try setting a number — the count you'd keep if you had to choose — and see how far off you are
