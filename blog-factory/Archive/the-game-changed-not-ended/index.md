# The Game Changed, Not Ended

{{< lead >}}
A lot of young men are grieving a life that ended before they got to it — one income, a house, a pension at sixty. The grief is understandable and the target is odd, because that life had trade-offs nobody mentions and it isn't coming back either way.
{{< /lead >}}

### The trap of the dead game

Most of the doom is a comparison against a specific mid-century arrangement. Measured against that, yes, things look worse.

But that arrangement involved one employer for forty years, very little geographic choice, and effectively no way to build anything of your own. Romanticising it means wanting the security and quietly forgetting the constraint.

The more useful question is what this era actually hands you that no previous one did.

### What's genuinely new

**Making money without permission.** This is the first generation that can earn online without premises, stock, or a gatekeeper deciding you're allowed. The niche thing is real too — small style channels now out-perform the magazines that used to own that territory, because serving a specific audience is finally economic.

**Learning anything for free.** Video turns out to beat textbooks for most practical skills. Someone with three years of journalism school learned the editing software he actually needed from free tutorials, because a classroom moves at one speed for thirty people.

**Not living where the job is.** Remote work detaches income from postcode. Earn on one economy's wages and live somewhere cheaper — whether that's a lower-tax state or another country entirely. A month working from Buenos Aires at roughly $50 a day, taxis and meals out included, is the kind of thing that was recently only available to the wealthy.

**Meeting people outside your circle.** Dating apps are easy to be cynical about and they do one genuinely unprecedented thing: put you in front of people you'd never otherwise cross paths with. The advice attached is sensible — decent photos, an actual bio, and take advice about women from women rather than from men speculating about them.

**Being healthy cheaply.** Gyms at £10 a month, every workout ever filmed available free, and better food options than any previous generation had.

### Where I'd add friction

Some of this is sunnier than I'd write it.

The stats quoted — 87% of Gen Z training three times a week, 27% never drinking — are cheerful and I'd hold them loosely. Making money online is possible for anyone and *works* for a small fraction; the barrier moved from permission to competition, which is better but not the same as easy. Geo-arbitrage assumes a passport, a remote employer, and no caring responsibilities, which quietly excludes a lot of people. And dating apps have real documented costs alongside the access.

None of that restores the doomer case. It just means the honest version is "different trade-offs, some genuinely better," not "the best time ever, obviously."

### The bit that's actually load-bearing

It begins with what you're paying attention to, and social media is built to over-serve the downside because that's what holds people.

Every generation gets a hand with some good cards in it. Spending your twenties mourning a hand you were never dealt is the one strategy guaranteed not to work.

### A few small things to try

- Write down what you'd need to earn your income from anywhere
- Learn one skill you've been putting off, from free material, this month
- Check what your money would be worth somewhere cheaper
- Notice how much of your pessimism arrived through a feed
