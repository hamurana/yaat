# The Only Luxury Test Is Repetition

{{< lead >}}
The useful question about an expensive thing isn't whether it impressed you when you bought it. It's whether it's still quietly improving something you do every single day, long after the excitement wore off.
{{< /lead >}}

### The frame

Our lives are mostly made of repeated moments. Getting ready. Leaving the house. Packing. Sitting down to eat.

So the things worth spending on are the ones sitting inside those repetitions — not the ones that produce a good afternoon once. That reframes the whole category: luxury as friction removed from something you'll do four thousand more times, rather than as a signal to anyone.

### What passes the test

- **A hair dryer that actually works.** Sounds trivial. It's used daily, it's faster, and the styling is predictable — enough that hotel dryers become genuinely annoying. Daily use is exactly the point.
- **A slim card holder.** Two compartments, no hardware, fits any pocket. Room for a tracker card after nearly losing the last one in a hotel sofa. Simplicity here frees up a small amount of attention, permanently.
- **A recovery tracker with no screen.** No notifications. The value isn't the data, it's noticing which behaviours actually affect how you feel and adjusting.
- **Luggage with a real warranty.** Travel has enough variables. Bags that roll properly and a repair process that works removes the background worry about damage entirely.
- **A key holder.** Keys get handled several times a day, so even a small improvement compounds hard. Nearly a decade of use and no longer thought about — which is the highest compliment available.
- **A crossbody bag that disappears.** Fits camera, tablet, passport, sunglasses, and turns into an everyday bag on landing. The best travel objects don't ask for attention; they just work.
- **Dining chairs chosen on purpose.** A lot of what matters happens around a table. Picking something personal rather than the chair in every restaurant, and letting it age into the family.
- **A camera with a fixed lens.** One focal length removes the gear decisions and leaves composition and light. The camera gets out of the way, and photography gets more enjoyable.

### The obvious objection

This list is Hermès, Leica, RIMOWA, Louis Vuitton, Dyson. It's framed as being about function rather than status, and the brands are doing a fair amount of unacknowledged work.

Both things can be true. The *test* is genuinely good — does this keep paying you back in repeated moments — and a Casio, a £15 card holder and an ordinary hair dryer pass it just as cleanly. Nothing in the framework requires the price tag.

So take the test and leave the shopping list. The question "will I touch this every day for ten years" is the useful part, and it will point you at a good pen and a well-made chair far more often than at a luxury house.

### The honest version of the rule

Spend disproportionately on what you're in contact with constantly, and pay almost nothing for what you touch rarely.

That's an unglamorous conclusion, and it explains why a decent mattress and a comfortable chair matter more than nearly anything visible to guests.

### A few small things to try

- List the five objects you physically handle most, and check whether any are actively annoying
- Before buying something expensive, guess how many times you'll use it in a year
- Ask whether the cheap version would pass the same repetition test — often it does
- Revisit an old purchase and ask whether it's still paying you back
