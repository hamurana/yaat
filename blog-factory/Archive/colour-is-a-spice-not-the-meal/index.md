Colour does the same jobs in a wardrobe that it does in nature: hide, attract, or warn. An office dress code is camouflage. A red tie is a warning light. Most people never choose which one they're doing, and then wonder why a room reads them a particular way.
{{< /lead >}}

### Four colours doing most of the work

- **Navy** — calm and steady. Read as reliable, professional, grown-up. The most useful colour in menswear and it isn't close.
- **Charcoal** — poised and a bit formal. Tends to read as elegant, and as more intelligent, which is a strange thing for a fabric to do.
- **Brown** — warm and stable. Consistently comes out as one of the most trustworthy colours you can wear.
- **Red** — loud on purpose. Energy and dominance, and the one that commands attention whether or not you wanted it to.

Red is the interesting one because it works and it's a blunt instrument. There's a much-repeated study where the same lecturer gave the same talk in a red jumper and a blue one, and the red audience rated him noticeably more believable. The sourcing on it is vague enough that I'd hold it loosely — but the general finding, that red gets read as power and dominance, turns up often enough to take seriously.

### The vocabulary is worth ten minutes

Four words let you talk about colour precisely instead of pointing.

- **Hue** — the pure colour, nothing added.
- **Shade** — hue plus black. Blue becomes navy.
- **Tint** — hue plus white. Blue becomes sky blue.
- **Tone** — hue plus grey. Blue becomes steel blue.

Most of what people call "the wrong blue" is a tint or tone problem, not a hue problem.

### Build it in layers

Seven colours will carry roughly ninety per cent of a wardrobe across almost any skin tone: **navy, grey, pure white, burgundy, beige, charcoal, teal**.

Put seventy per cent or more of your money into neutrals — white, black, navy, light grey, beige, light blue, tan, olive. Then add two to five accent colours, held mostly in shirts, trousers, and casual jackets, which are the things you'll replace in a few years anyway.

That ratio is the whole system. Neutrals are the meal, accents are the seasoning.

### Three ways to combine

- **Tonal.** One hue head to toe, varied by texture, with neutral shoes. Hardest to get wrong, quietly impressive when it lands.
- **Complementary pairing.** Two colours that oppose each other, everything else neutral — navy with rust, olive with burgundy.
- **Restrained palette.** Three depths of one neutral, plus at most one quiet accent.

All three share the same discipline: one idea per outfit.

### Skin tone decides your accents

The neutrals work for nearly everyone. The pop colour doesn't — the same green that makes one person look healthy makes the next look ill.

A few that hold up: salmon and powder blue on very fair skin; coral and sea green on fair; peach and pale green on light; amber and turquoise on medium; bronze and pale taupe on olive; crimson, emerald, and rust on brown skin; gold, royal blue, and deep violet on the deepest.

And the ones to be careful with are usually anything sitting too close to your own colouring. Very dark shades wash out lighter skin, which is why a charcoal dinner suit often photographs better than a hired black one.

Worth being clear: this is optimisation, not a rulebook. You can wear anything. Some things just do more for you.

### The test everyone should already be doing

Hold the garment up to your face in daylight and look at your face, not the garment.

Women have been doing this in shops for decades and men mostly don't. It takes three seconds and answers the question better than any chart.

### One last piece of history

Purple reads as regal because it used to be ruinous. Ancient dye-makers — the credit usually goes to the Greeks — killed thousands of sea snails for a single batch. Toxic to produce, absurdly expensive to buy, and the association outlived the process by two thousand years.

A few small things to try:

- Count what's actually in your wardrobe by colour. Most people are surprised.
- Add one accent colour, not four.
- Hold your next shirt up to your face before looking at the price.
- If you wear red, wear it once and deliberately, not as a habit.
