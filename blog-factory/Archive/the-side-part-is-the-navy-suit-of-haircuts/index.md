# The Side Part Is the Navy Suit of Haircuts

{{< lead >}}
A good haircut sits between two failures: so fussy it eats your morning, or so utilitarian it looks like you've stopped caring. Most classic cuts are just different answers to that one problem.
{{< /lead >}}

### The six

- **Classic side part.** Scissors only, no clippers. Cary Grant, Sean Connery, Don Draper. Two versions — longer and slicked with pomade for a 50s feel, or shorter with matte clay for something current. It frames a face the way a navy suit frames an outfit, which is exactly why it works on nearly everyone.
- **Slicked back.** Straight back, no part. Gordon Gekko, Patrick Bateman, and lately a lot of finance. Needs a strong jaw and a full head of hair — if your hairline's going, this points straight at it.
- **Curtains.** Bangs falling forward. Big in the 90s with DiCaprio and Depp, back via Timothée Chalamet, and apparently traceable to Oscar Wilde. Youthful, a bit rebellious, reads more posh boy than professional. Also high maintenance — blow dryer, sea salt spray. Best if you're young and not chasing authority yet.
- **Ivy League.** A crew cut with a bit more on top. Less work than a side part, more considered than a buzz. Damon, Reynolds, Gosling, Pitt. Styles forward or down, so it's two looks from one cut, and it survives thinner hair. Its flaw is that it's unremarkable — nobody will compliment it, and nobody will ever think it's wrong.
- **Pompadour.** Serious volume at the front, from James Dean and Elvis. Looks great and costs you a blow dryer plus several products every morning. Tips into cartoon easily. The restrained version is roughly Harvey Specter.
- **High and tight.** Very short or shaved sides into a longer top, military in origin, mainstream via *Peaky Blinders*. Almost no daily upkeep — product on top and out the door — but you'll be at the barber constantly to keep the sides clean. Avoid the hard disconnected undercut; a gradual fade looks far better. People will assume military or police.

### How to pick

Four things decide it, and only one is about looks:

- **Your hair.** Thickness, texture, how much volume it has on its own.
- **Your mornings.** Be honest about how much you'll actually do. A cut you won't maintain is a bad cut regardless of how it looks in the chair.
- **Your face.** Chiselled or soft, and how the hairline's doing.
- **What you want to signal.** Authority points toward the side part or Ivy League. Youth points toward curtains or a pompadour.

If you want the short version: the side part and the Ivy League are the two that are hard to get wrong. The slicked back and the pompadour ask for a specific face and full hair. The high and tight is clean but comes with associations. Curtains are fine while you're young and actively work against being taken seriously.

### The thing worth noticing

The cuts that last aren't the ones that look best in a photo. They're the ones that still look right in ten years and don't need a device to achieve.

Which is roughly the same principle as everything else in dressing well — most of the benefit comes from picking something durable and then not thinking about it again.

### A few small things to try

- Work out how many minutes you'll genuinely spend, then pick within that budget
- If you're between options, take the side part — it's the safest good answer
- Ask a barber what suits your face rather than bringing a photo of someone else's
- Pick something you could still be wearing in a decade
