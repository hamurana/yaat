If your shirt comes untucked a dozen times a day, no tucking trick is going to save you. The fabric spilling over your belt isn't a technique problem — it's too much shirt, cut too short, worn slightly crooked. Sort those three and you stop thinking about it.
{{< /lead >}}

### The three things that actually decide it

**Fit.** Plenty of men are tucking in ten to fourteen inches of surplus fabric around the waist. Even six inches is too much on a lean build. That excess has nowhere to go, so it bunches over the belt and works its way out through the day. A tailor darting the shirt at the midsection is cheap and permanent.

**Length.** The shirt should fall at least three inches below your belt line, and longer is better. Cheap shirts are cut short because fabric costs money, and short shirts untuck themselves — there's simply not enough anchored below the waistband. If you're having shirts made, order them slightly long. You can shorten a shirt. You cannot lengthen one.

**The gig line.** This is the invisible vertical running down the front placket of your shirt, and it should line up with your belt buckle and with the fly seam of your trousers. When those three are out of alignment it reads as slightly askew even to people who've never heard the term.

Get those right and a plain tuck holds all day.

### Then pick a technique for the situation

**The standard tuck.** Tuck it in. Works fine when the shirt fits, and it's the one that comes loose when the shirt doesn't.

**The military tuck.** Reach round to the sides, slightly toward the back, put your thumbs in, fold the surplus into flat pleats and tuck those down. A belt cinched over it holds a lot of fabric in place. It takes a bit of practice to do quickly.

Worth noting the name oversells the pedigree — plenty of people who swear they learned it in the army probably picked it up in the Scouts.

**The undershirt tuck.** Commonly misunderstood. You are not tucking your dress shirt into your underwear. You tuck the **undershirt** into your underwear, and the dress shirt then layers over the top as a separate piece. Two layers moving independently, and the outer one stays put considerably longer.

**Shirt stays.** Elastic straps running from your shirt hem down to your socks, pulling the shirt back into place every time it rides up. This is genuinely the military one — dress uniforms, parades, honour guards — and it's the answer if you're going to be on your feet, moving, and presenting to people for an hour.

### When each is worth it

- Sitting at a desk in a shirt that fits → standard tuck.
- Shirt with surplus you haven't tailored yet → military tuck.
- Long day, lots of moving about → the undershirt layer.
- Standing, speaking, gesturing, being looked at → shirt stays.

There's also grip underwear with a rubberised strip around the outside of the waistband, which holds the shirt hem without anything attached to your socks. Less committed than stays, more effective than hope.

### The point of all this

Not to become someone who thinks about tucking. The opposite.

Every one of these exists so that your shirt stops being a thing you manage. Fix the garment once, pick the technique that matches your day, and then get to spend the day not touching your own waistband — which, incidentally, is a habit people notice.

A few small things to try:

- Measure how far your shirt falls below your belt. If it's under three inches, that's the problem.
- Take one shirt to be darted and compare it against the rest.
- Check your gig line in a mirror before you leave.
- Try the undershirt-into-underwear layering tomorrow. It costs nothing.
