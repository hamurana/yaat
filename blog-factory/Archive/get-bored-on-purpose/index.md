# Get Bored on Purpose

{{< lead >}}
Eleven habits for climbing out of doom-scrolling, from someone who makes videos about digital minimalism and still catches himself scrolling. That admission is worth more than expertise would be.
{{< /lead >}}

### The eleven

- **Leave a gap in the day.** Ten or fifteen minutes with no phone, no music, nothing. Washing up or driving in silence works well. It's practice at tolerating being unstimulated.
- **Delete one high-dopamine app.** You may not miss it at first, then notice how much room it was taking. Even deleting the app while keeping the account works, because you won't re-download and log back in just to have a look.
- **Rewatch things.** Shorts and Reels run on novelty specifically. Going back to music and shows you already love breaks that, and the stretches of his life spent on more considered content were, looking back, better ones.
- **Put the phone further away.** Another room, or a bag. The walk to fetch it gives you time to ask why you're fetching it, and that question alone often ends the trip.
- **One thing at a time.** Not watching something while eating, not two screens at once. Days feel slower and better, and things you enjoy get properly enjoyed rather than half-noticed.
- **Stop treating boredom as an emergency.** Most of us can't sit in a waiting room any more. Catch the automatic reach and just be bored — a lot of what gets consumed isn't even enjoyed, it's just filling something that felt uncomfortable.
- **Choose slower content deliberately.** Curate the subscriptions and playlists. Slower doesn't mean dull — books, drawing, walking, lower-stimulation games. What surrounds you sets your baseline mood.
- **Fewer windows open.** Finish one thing before opening the next. One or two tabs. The overhead of switching is larger than it feels.
- **Have one offline hobby.** Not monetised, not content, not for an audience. Walking, the gym, sketching, reading. Something that exists purely because you like it.
- **Stop the background audio.** It usually starts as "at least I'm learning while I do chores" and degrades into noise. The problem is what it does over time: anything that isn't enhanced starts to feel unbearable.
- **End the night without an algorithm.** Don't let an app pick what you watch before bed. Choose something, or read. If you only take one of these, do tonight without the phone and see how tomorrow feels.

### The single thread

Every one of them is the same move: rebuilding your tolerance for not being stimulated.

The empty fifteen minutes, the silence in the car, sitting bored in a waiting room, dropping the background podcast — none of them are about screens exactly. They're about the fact that a brain kept at a constant high level stops registering anything quieter, and then ordinary life feels flat through no fault of its own.

Which reframes the whole thing. It isn't self-denial or a digital detox with a finish line. It's slowly lowering the volume until normal things are audible again.

### Why the admission matters

He isn't claiming to have solved it, and that makes the list more usable than a triumphant version would be.

Anything framed as a cure invites you to fail once and quit. Something framed as ongoing maintenance survives a bad evening, which is the only realistic standard for a habit that competes with software engineered by very well-paid people to defeat exactly this.

### A few small things to try

- Do one daily task in silence — no podcast, no music
- Move the charger out of the bedroom
- Sit through one wait without reaching for anything
- Pick tonight's viewing yourself instead of taking what's offered
