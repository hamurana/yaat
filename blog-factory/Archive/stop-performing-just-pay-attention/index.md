# Stop Performing, Just Pay Attention

Three completely different situations — a deep one-on-one conversation, casual small talk, and staying calm under pressure — and they all seem to come down to the same small move. Stop trying to be impressive. Just be there.

### The temptation in all three

In a deep conversation, the temptation is to prove you're insightful or helpful — jumping in with advice, finishing people's sentences, steering toward your own story. In small talk, the temptation is to be witty or interesting, which usually just makes the exchange feel forced. Under pressure, the temptation is to react — defend yourself, raise your voice, show you're not intimidated.

All three temptations come from the same place: worrying about how you're coming across, instead of paying attention to what's actually happening in front of you.

### What it looks like to just pay attention, in a real conversation

- **Barely interrupt.** Let people finish their thoughts. The one exception worth making: interrupting gently to go *deeper* — "wait, you really mean that?" — when someone rushes past something that clearly mattered to them.
- **Say less when things get emotional.** The instinct is to reassure or change the subject. Often the better move is quiet — sitting with someone, saying almost nothing, letting them keep going at their own pace.
- **Reflect back what they actually said, not what you assume they meant.** There's a real difference between repeating someone's own words back to them and projecting your own experience onto theirs. Only the first one builds trust.
- **Ask questions that cut to what matters.** Not "where do you live," but "why do you live there" — it gets at what someone actually values instead of just logging a fact.
- **Drop your own agenda.** Everything above rests on this. The moment you're privately rehearsing what to say next, you've stopped listening.

### What it looks like in small talk

- **Open with a genuine compliment, then move on.** Don't linger on it or make it weird — just lower the guard a little and continue.
- **Follow the tangent instead of forcing a topic.** When a subject runs dry, ask yourself what it reminds you of, and let the conversation drift there naturally instead of trying to steer it somewhere impressive.
- **Ask "why" instead of "what."** It surfaces what someone cares about, which gives you something real to talk about next.
- **Laugh when something's actually funny, and repeat someone's last few words back to them once in a while.** Both are just small, honest signs that you're actually listening, not performing your side of the exchange.

The video's own conclusion on this one is worth keeping as-is: good small talk isn't about being witty. It's about creating the conditions for the other person to enjoy talking. That's the whole throughline in one sentence.

### What it looks like under pressure

- **Move and speak slower than you feel like you should.** Rushing reads as anxious. Calm reads as unbothered, even when you don't feel it yet.
- **Let there be silence before the important part.** A pause before the key point pulls people in more than rushing through it.
- **Don't react to provocation.** Staying visibly calm where someone else would snap is one of the simplest ways to project confidence — and it's not about suppressing panic, it's about genuinely not needing to win the moment.
- **Have real options, and let that be your calm.** Actual confidence under pressure usually comes from having somewhere else to go if this doesn't work out — a backup plan, another opportunity, a choice you're not desperate about. That quiet backup is what makes the calm real instead of performed.

### The same move, three settings

Deep listening, easy small talk, and calm under pressure all ask for the same thing underneath: stop managing your own image for a minute, and actually attend to what's in front of you — the person's words, the thread of the conversation, the situation as it actually is rather than how threatening it feels.

A few small things to try:

- In your next real conversation, go three exchanges without offering your own opinion — just reflect back what the other person said.
- In small talk, swap one "what do you do" for a "why" version of the same question.
- Next time you feel provoked, slow your speech down on purpose before responding at all.
