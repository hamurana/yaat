Before anyone registers your fit, your fabric, or what you paid, they've already read the outfit as a block of colour. That takes about three seconds. Too many colours competing and it reads as busy — even when every single piece was a good choice on its own.
{{< /lead >}}

### Three jobs, three colours

The old 60-30-10 idea is more useful when you stop treating it as arithmetic and treat it as roles.

- **An anchor** — the weight and the authority.
- **A light** — clarity, and it goes near the face.
- **An accent** — warmth, small, one place only.

The combination that consistently works, particularly past sixty: **navy, soft ivory, cognac brown.** Each doing exactly one job and nothing else.

### Navy rather than black

Black looks like the obvious anchor and it isn't, not any more.

It throws a hard contrast against your face, and hard contrast deepens every shadow and line it finds. The result is technically sharp and makes you look tired. Navy carries the same authority and weight with a softer edge and a more approachable read.

If navy looks flat or severe on you, that's a texture problem rather than a colour one. Hopsack wool, brushed cotton, suede, or a fine knit all keep it from going dead.

### Ivory rather than bright white

Same logic, one layer closer to your face.

Bright white draws a hard line right under the chin, which emphasises redness and any unevenness in the skin. Warm ivory, cream, or ecru still reads clean and pressed, and it supports the face rather than competing with it.

### Cognac, and only a little

The accent lives in leather — shoes, a belt, a watch strap, a bag. It adds warmth and a bit of character without asking for anything.

The failure here is over-coordination. Cognac trousers *and* jacket *and* belt *and* shoes *and* watch strap doesn't read as coordinated. It reads as someone who planned it in a mirror for twenty minutes. Matching every leather piece exactly is the tell.

Two more ways it goes wrong: swapping black back in for navy, and picking a brown that's too orange or too shiny, at which point the accent stops supporting and becomes the first thing anyone sees.

### Adjust the shades to you

The structure holds. The exact shades shouldn't be copied.

- **Fair skin or white hair** — navy with some texture rather than a flat near-black, and ivory rather than white.
- **Medium to deep complexions** — a richer navy and a deeper cognac both carry easily.
- **Noticeable redness in the face** — go to muted tobacco or walnut. Orange-toned tan will find the redness and echo it back.

### Three tests, none of which need a shop

- **Stand back from a mirror** and look only at the blocks of colour. Does your eye move across it smoothly, or jump?
- **Photograph the outfit and shrink it** on your phone until it's a thumbnail. If the shoes or the belt still dominate at that size, the accent is too loud. This one is brutal and very fast.
- **Hold bright white against soft ivory** under natural light, by your face. Don't ask which looks brighter. Ask which makes your face look healthier and calmer.

### Four outfits from the same three colours

- **Errands** — navy chinos, ivory polo, cognac leather sneakers.
- **Smart casual dinner** — unstructured navy blazer, cream knit polo, mid-grey trousers as a quiet fourth, cognac loafers.
- **Travel** — navy overshirt, ecru tee, dark jeans, tobacco suede shoes.
- **Family occasion** — navy sport coat, ivory Oxford, stone trousers, cognac brogues.

None of that requires buying anything if you already own the pieces. Refinement here isn't spending — it's making sure each colour has one job and isn't quietly doing two.

A few small things to try:

- Photograph your next outfit and shrink it. Look at what still shouts.
- Swap the black jacket for a navy one and see whether your face changes.
- Hold ivory and white next to each other in daylight.
- Un-match one leather piece on purpose.
