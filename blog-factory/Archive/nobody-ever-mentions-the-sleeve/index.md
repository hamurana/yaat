Polo advice covers the collar. It covers the fit. The sleeve gets skipped almost every time, and it's quietly doing more than either. Too wide or too long and your arms look thinner and your shoulders narrower — whatever the shirt cost.
{{< /lead >}}

### What a sleeve should be doing

- Ends around the **middle of the upper arm**. Not at the elbow.
- Follows the arm without gripping it.
- The opening sits lightly near the arm rather than flaring away from it.
- Moves when you move — no flapping, no twisting round.

This isn't about showing muscle. It's about having an outline. A sleeve that flares turns your arm into a vague shape somewhere inside a tube, and the eye reads that as smaller.

### Check things in an order, and put the brand last

Most people buy a polo by looking at the label, then the colour, then everything else. Reverse it.

1. Collar
2. Fit
3. Sleeves
4. Fabric
5. What you're wearing it with

Brand, logo, and colour come after all five. They're the easiest things to notice and the least likely to be the problem.

### The daylight test

Fabric that looks acceptable in a shop looks different outside, and outside is where you'll wear it.

- Too thin and it clings at the stomach.
- Too shiny and it reads cheap the moment sun hits it.
- Too stretchy and the collar and sleeves give up early.
- Creases too easily and a careful man looks careless by lunchtime.

What you want is enough weight to **drape** instead of cling. Piqué cotton is the classic. A solid cotton blend is fine if it holds shape through washing. A weightier knit polo is the dressiest of the three and works well for dinner or travel, as long as it isn't tight.

### The bottom half decides which way it goes

A polo sits between casual and smart, and it doesn't choose for itself. Whatever's below the belt does the choosing.

Baggy cargo shorts, faded jeans, sweatpants, worn running shoes — these pull a good polo down to their level, and they will win. Straight-leg chinos, dark clean denim, lightweight trousers or tailored shorts, finished with loafers or clean leather sneakers, pull it the other way.

Same shirt. Same body. Completely different read. Which is why buying another polo is usually the wrong fix — the shirt is rarely what's letting you down.

### It isn't an age problem

The usual framing is that men over fifty need to be careful with polos. That's not really it.

A collapsed collar looks the same on a twenty-five-year-old. Cargo shorts and worn trainers do the same job at any age. What changes isn't the rules, it's the margin — you get less benefit of the doubt, so the sloppy version reads as *given up* rather than *relaxed*.

So the answer isn't dressing younger or dressing older. It's intention. The fit should skim rather than squeeze, the hem should land around mid-fly when untucked, and the whole thing should look chosen. Not dressed up. Just ready.

A few small things to try:

- Stand in front of a mirror and look only at your sleeves. It's the one part you've never checked.
- Wear your best polo outside and look at it in daylight.
- Fix the bottom half before buying another shirt — chinos, a leather belt, better shoes.
- Retire anything whose collar no longer sits flat out of the wash.
