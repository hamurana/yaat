# Your Attention Is the Thing They're After

{{< lead >}}
Most of us were taught that strength looks like defending yourself — speaking up, fighting back, proving the point. That habit spends your scarcest thing on people who never earned any of it.
{{< /lead >}}

### Attention as the actual currency

Time on its own isn't worth much. Time with your attention pointed at something is.

So every time you respond to a slight, get pulled into someone's drama, or spend three hours mentally rewriting a comment a colleague made, you've spent capital and got nothing back.

The filter is one question, asked before responding: *does this deserve it?* Not whether they'll think you're rude. Just whether it's worth the spend. If no, move on — and you don't owe anyone an explanation for that.

### Why silence unsettles people

Firing back confirms the hit landed. You've told them their words had enough weight to pull you in, which is exactly what they were checking.

Silence does something stranger. Brains expect a response, and when one doesn't come there's nowhere for the exchange to settle. They start wondering. You've already moved on.

There's a second thing here worth noticing: explaining yourself is also information. It tells people what you care about and where you're soft. Silence hands over no map.

### Being available all the time makes you furniture

We price things by how rare they are, and presence is no exception. Always reachable, always accommodating, always saying yes — and you stop being noticed.

That's less a tactic than a description of how people work. The fix isn't playing games; it's being properly present when you're there and selective about when that is.

### Not reacting is a move, not an absence

When someone provokes you they're trying to steer how you feel. Reacting as expected means they've got the wheel.

Don't react and the loop stays open on their side. They replay it, looking for a resolution that never arrives, burning energy on a fight you're not in.

Which is why non-reaction isn't passivity. It says something quite specific: you don't have a grip on me.

### Cutting cleanly

Three groups worth being honest about:

- **People working against you.** Don't confront, don't expose — just stop supplying attention and keep building. Doing well is more effective than any confrontation.
- **Permanent critics.** People who only ever take apart. Don't give them an audience.
- **Energy drains.** Always a crisis, never a solution, because the crisis is the point. Protecting your capacity here isn't selfish.

Cut quietly. The drama version costs you more than the person did.

### Deciding what gets to land

The idea underneath the whole thing: you decide what affects you and how much, rather than whoever's nearby.

Two steps. Work out your actual triggers — what makes you defensive, what makes you need to explain, whose approval you chase without meaning to. Then go near those things on purpose, repeatedly, until they stop landing.

When someone's tools stop working, they either start respecting you or they lose interest. Both are fine outcomes.

### Where I'd add a caution

This framework is genuinely useful and it does have a failure mode worth naming: it works just as well as a way to avoid every difficult conversation you should be having.

Non-reaction with someone testing you is strength. The same silence with someone who loves you and is trying to reach you is just distance with a better story attached. The difference is whether the relationship is worth having — and that's a judgment the framework itself won't make for you.

### A few small things to try

- Before responding to something irritating, ask whether it deserves the energy
- Say nothing once when you'd normally defend yourself, and watch what happens
- Name one trigger honestly, then stop protecting yourself from it
- Check you're not using "not reacting" to dodge a conversation that matters
