{{< lead >}}
Men don't look cheap because their clothes were inexpensive. They look cheap because their clothes are trying too hard. Which means the fastest upgrade available is usually to remove the thing you paid extra for — the logo, the buckle, the watch that arrived in its own box.
{{< /lead >}}

### Logos out, texture in

A brand name across the chest doesn't say you can afford it. It says you'd like that noted, and a grown man saying that loses authority instantly.

Replace the graphic with a surface. Cotton piqué, wool blends, suede, linen, brushed cotton, a proper knit. Loud branding makes noise; texture makes depth, and depth is what reads as expensive.

Simple test: if the first thing anyone notices is the logo, the outfit is doing too much work for the wrong end.

### The belt is in the middle and nobody looks at it

Men buy one belt and then stop seeing it. Everyone else keeps seeing it — the peeling edge, the stretched-out holes, the buckle that's too big.

Two belts is the whole answer. One brown for tan, olive, navy, denim, and casual leather shoes. One black for black shoes and anything formal. They don't need to match the shoes exactly; they need to belong to the same family.

### Glasses and watch should whisper

This is the pair that quietly ages an otherwise good outfit, and it's the least examined.

Scratched sporty frames, or a frame shape from twenty years ago, date everything above the neck. A bulky digital watch pulls the whole thing toward the gym.

What works: clean acetate frames in tortoise or dark brown, and a simple watch on a leather strap. Dark frames add strength, tortoise adds warmth, thin metal reads classic. None of it costs much and all of it is visible in every conversation you have.

### The other five, briefly

- **Fit, in order.** Shoulders, sleeve length, trouser length. The wrong fit can make a $300 shirt look like a clearance rail, and puddling trousers make you shorter.
- **Shoes.** Maintenance beats price by a distance — an expensive pair left to crack looks worse than a cheap pair kept clean. Replace grey laces, use shoe trees, brush the suede, polish dry leather, sort out worn heels. Your shoes don't need to impress anyone. They need to confirm that you notice things.
- **Colour near the face.** As hair silvers, faded black drains you and beige flattens you. Swap white for cream, black for charcoal or navy, anything bright for a deeper version of itself.
- **One structured layer.** When everything is soft and loose, the shoulders go. A navy chore jacket, a suede bomber, a field jacket, or an unstructured blazer over a plain tee and jeans makes the same outfit look finished. No extra zips, patches, or cargo pockets.
- **Grooming.** A sharp outfit can't rescue an uneven beard or neglected nails. And the distinction worth holding: nobody looks wealthy because they look *perfect* — perfect looks artificial. They look **maintained**, which is a discipline rather than an appearance.

### Then take a colour out

Five or six competing items produce visual noise, and when everything is competing nothing reads as expensive.

Build around two or three colours. Navy, grey, brown. Cream, olive, dark denim. Charcoal, tan, white. If an outfit feels busy, remove one colour before you consider adding anything.

Three combinations that work with no thought at all:

- **Weekend** — dark jeans, cream knit polo, brown suede jacket.
- **Dinner** — charcoal trousers, navy blazer, soft white shirt.
- **Travel** — olive chinos, navy overshirt, clean brown shoes.

### The end state

Every step here is free or nearly free, and none of it involves buying something impressive.

What it adds up to is looking like a man who no longer needs to prove anything — which, awkwardly for the whole industry, is the thing everyone was trying to buy in the first place.

A few small things to try:

- Take the most-branded item you own out of rotation.
- Buy the second belt. You almost certainly own one.
- Look at your glasses honestly, or ask someone to.
- Count the colours in your next outfit and remove one.
