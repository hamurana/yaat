The most common fountain pen mistake isn't technique. It's the drawer. A good pen bought and then protected does nothing at all, and every benefit it has — a signature that's genuinely hard to forge, handwriting that helps you remember things — exists only in daily use.
{{< /lead >}}

### Everything else is a small tax

Once you decide to actually carry it, the care rules are short and mostly one-time.

**Use the right paper, or the right nib.** A fountain pen lays down far more ink than a ballpoint, and thin office paper drinks it and spreads. Thicker cardstock or proper fountain-pen paper costs barely more. If you know you won't manage paper, buy an extra-fine or fine nib instead — less ink means it looks good on anything. A note written on heavy cotton paper also lands differently with whoever gets it.

**Stop pressing.** The nib is split down the middle. Pressure widens the line, which is lovely on a signature, and too much of it bends or breaks the thing. It also scratches, tires your hand, and tears paper. If you write long letters, a slightly heavier pen glides on its own weight and removes the temptation.

**Use fountain pen ink and nothing else.** Calligraphy ink and cheap ink are chemically different — too thick or too quick to dry, and they clog the feed. That's the whole rule.

The upside is colour. There are hundreds, and almost everyone defaults to royal blue, which is the best-selling colour on earth and therefore the least yours. Dark green still contrasts properly on white and immediately makes a document look like it came from a person. It also makes a signature harder to copy. If you write rarely, pick a slow-drying ink so the pen still starts after a fortnight.

### Cleaning, and the expensive way to learn it

Water only. Never alcohol, never acetone.

With decent ink and regular use you may never need to clean it at all. When you do: take it apart if it comes apart, soak it, flush water through the feed, and use a soft toothbrush.

An ultrasonic cleaner is the gentle option — water and vibration, no scrubbing. One warning that's worth more than the machine. Many of them heat the water, and heat plus vibration will expand old materials. A vintage celluloid-bodied Meisterstück put through a heated cleaner came out with the internal metal pushed up into a permanent bump in the barrel. It still writes. Its collector value is gone.

So: cold water, short runs, check at five minutes rather than ten.

### The two things that end pens

**A drop onto the nib means a professional.** Steel nibs are cheap to replace. Gold nibs run $200 to $500 for the nib alone. Do not straighten it yourself — you'll wreck the feed and the flow along with it. Prevention is just capping it when idle and carrying it in a pouch rather than loose.

**Nib up when flying.** Cabin pressure pushes ink out of a pen lying flat or sitting nib-down. Open it after landing and your fingers are stained for days, because good ink is colourfast and doesn't care about soap. Stand it nib-up in a pocket and the problem disappears entirely.

### The short version

Fine nib if you're lazy about paper. Real ink, in a colour that's yours. Light hand. Water only. Cap on. Nib up in the air.

That's the entire cost of writing in a way no ballpoint can match, and none of it applies to a pen sitting in a box.

A few small things to try:

- Put it in your bag today rather than in a drawer.
- Buy one bottle of ink that isn't blue or black.
- Write one thank-you note by hand this week.
- Check which way up your pen travels.
