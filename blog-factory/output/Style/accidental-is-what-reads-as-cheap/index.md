{{< lead >}}
Expensive clothes can still look cheap, and there's one thing behind it every time: something in the picture looks accidental rather than chosen. Almost none of the fixes involve buying anything. Most of them are half an hour and a brush.
{{< /lead >}}

### Comfort and collapse are different things

The first place an outfit goes is fit, and "relaxed" is the word people use to avoid noticing.

A shirt sliding off the shoulder makes the upper body look weaker. A polo bagging at the waist adds weight that isn't there. Trousers piling on the shoes make the bottom half look untidy. None of that is relaxed — it's collapsed.

What you want is shoulder seams sitting at the edge of the shoulder, fabric that skims without clinging or floating, and little to no break in the trouser over a loafer. Every one of those is a tailoring job on clothes you already own, and it costs a fraction of replacing them.

### Fabric and colour give up before you notice

Cloth ages against you. A curling collar, a pilling sweater, a limp washed-out chino — people can't name it and they feel it. Weight and structure are what hold up: Oxford cloth, midweight cotton, wool blends that keep their shape, structured chinos, knitwear with firm cuffs. Fabric should stand with you rather than collapse on you.

Colour goes the same way, more quietly. Navy fades to a flat grey-blue. Black goes dusty. Olive drifts to something muddy. Nothing announces it — the garment just stops doing anything for your face.

The versions that work are the clean ones: navy that's still rich, charcoal that's still crisp, cream that's warm rather than yellowing. Burgundy, dark brown, forest green, deep blue.

### You cannot hide tired shoes

Shoes carry far more visual weight than most men assume, and there's nowhere to put them.

Scuffed, collapsed, dusty, or bulky sporty shoes pull the whole outfit down — not slightly. The fix isn't painful formal shoes. It's an adult choice: brown leather loafers, dark suede chukkas, clean derbies, a well-kept Chelsea boot, or minimal white leather sneakers.

Then keep them. Brush the suede, wipe the leather, replace laces that have gone grey, clean the edge of the sole, use shoe trees. This is maybe ten minutes a week and it is the single highest-return habit in menswear.

### Ask what's anchoring it

Sometimes everything is individually fine and the whole thing still looks like nothing. Pale polo, light chinos, grey trainers, thin beige jacket — one flat dusty picture with nothing leading the eye.

The question to ask at the door is: **what is anchoring this outfit?**

If the answer is nothing, add one stronger thing. A structured jacket. Dark brown loafers instead of the grey trainers. A good belt. One deep colour to give it a direction. You only need one.

### Details fail in both directions

Too loud is one failure — big logos, shiny synthetics, an oversized watch, a shouting belt buckle. No effort is the other, and it's more common: curled collar, wrinkled hem, cracked belt, pockets stuffed until they bulge, sleeves an inch too long.

The middle is quiet and takes five minutes. Empty your pockets. Steam the shirt. Get the sleeve length right.

Grooming sits in the same bracket. A good outfit can't carry an uneven beard, hair creeping over the ears, or neglected nails — they argue with the clothes and they usually win.

### The last one isn't clothing at all

Two men in the same outfit — dark sport coat, light shirt, clean trousers, brown shoes. One tugs at his cuffs, slouches, adjusts his collar every few minutes. The other stands straight and gets on with it.

The second man's clothes look better, and they're the same clothes.

Fidgeting reads as apologising for having made an effort. One check in the mirror before you leave — collar, cuffs, button the jacket when standing — and then stop touching it. Walk a bit slower. That's free too.

A few small things to try:

- Pick your best outfit and ask what's anchoring it.
- Spend ten minutes on your shoes this week.
- Empty your pockets before you leave the house.
- Notice how often you touch your own clothes, then stop.
