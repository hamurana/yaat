{{< lead >}}
Here's the rule almost nobody applies: stop dressing like a different man every day. Blazer and good shoes on Tuesday, faded tee and worn trainers on Wednesday. The clothes aren't the problem. The inconsistency is, because it means nobody ever forms a picture of you.
{{< /lead >}}

### A style language, not a wardrobe

Well-dressed older men have a small set of things they know work — particular colours, a trouser shape, a couple of silhouettes — and they refine those rather than reinventing themselves each morning.

The garments change. The message doesn't: calm, clean, deliberate, the same man each time.

That's what consistency actually buys. Not blandness — **recognisable rather than predictable.** People who meet you three days running should come away with one impression instead of three.

### Four more things they never do

**They don't let comfort look like surrender.** Comfort isn't the enemy; absent structure is. The body softens after sixty, and when the clothes soften at the same time everything reads as tired at once. Oversized sweatshirts, baggy jeans, stretched polos, collapsing trainers. The same comfort with shape — a knit polo with a firm collar, straight-leg dark jeans, a lightweight jacket, slim leather sneakers — reads completely differently while feeling identical.

**They don't wear colours that fight their face.** The face is the centre of it now. Hair lightens, skin changes, facial contrast softens, so the colours that worked twenty years ago start draining you. Heavy black turns severe against grey hair. Bright white washes you out. The right depth — navy, charcoal, camel, olive, cream, burgundy, soft blue, warm grey — makes the eyes clearer and the grey hair look deliberate rather than accidental.

**They don't let shoes tell a different story.** Shoes are the final proof of the outfit, and people read them faster than men expect. Casual isn't the issue; mismatch is — gym-shaped running shoes under a blazer collapse the whole thing. Brown loafers, a simple leather sneaker, a suede chukka, a polished derby, or a slimmer walking shoe all work, and none has to be expensive. It only has to look cared for.

**They don't confuse simple with boring.** This is the trap on the other side. Simplicity is powerful — loud patterns and logos make an older man look like he's borrowing confidence from his clothes. But thin, flat, shapeless simple doesn't read as classic. It reads as forgotten.

The lever is texture. Cotton twill, suede, brushed flannel, linen, a cashmere blend, a weighty knit polo, herringbone, one good watch. With fewer pieces, each one has to carry more. Don't dress loudly, and don't disappear.

### What connects them

None of these are about looking younger, and that's worth saying because it's the assumption underneath most advice aimed at men over sixty.

They're about **control** — over the image, the details, and the thing people decide about you before you've spoken. The requirement isn't a designer wardrobe. It's clothes that fit the body you have, support the face you have, respect your age, and add to your presence rather than subtracting from it.

Which brings it back to consistency, because control isn't something you demonstrate once. It's something people notice you doing repeatedly.

A few small things to try:

- Write down the three colours and one trouser shape that actually work on you.
- Check whether someone meeting you twice this week would see the same man.
- Add one textured piece to your plainest outfit.
- Look at your shoes and ask what story they're telling.
