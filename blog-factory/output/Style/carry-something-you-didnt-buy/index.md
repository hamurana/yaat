Everyday carry has drifted into gear collecting — tactical this, titanium that, six things doing the job of two. The best item in most men's pockets isn't any of it. It's the pocket knife that belonged to a grandfather, and it's better than anything you could order.
{{< /lead >}}

### Inherited beats bought

An object you were given carries something a purchase can't. You know where it came from. You'd be upset to lose it. You use it carefully.

That's most of what people are actually chasing when they buy an expensive tool — the sense that the thing in your pocket is *yours* rather than a model number. If something has come down to you, carry it. If nothing has, buy things capable of becoming that later, which mostly means buy once and keep it.

### Six things, and that's the lot

The whole framework fits in a sentence: wallet, watch, pen, notebook, sunglasses, knife. Adjust each to your own life and stop there.

- **A slim leather wallet, packed with only what you need.** A well-edited wallet says something about restraint that a thick one can't argue with. Rugged version if you're outdoors a lot.
- **A classic watch, chosen for subtlety.** Pick the complication that matches your actual life rather than the most impressive one — a second time zone if you work across them, a timer if you cook or run, dive capability only if you dive.
- **A good pen.** Signing a bill, marking up a document, writing a note by hand — all of it feels different done with something weighted and yours. Fountain pens if you like them, a good ballpoint if you don't. People notice, and they notice kindly.
- **A pocket notebook.** The one with the lowest commitment and the highest hit rate.
- **Sunglasses in a timeless shape.** Aviators, rounds, or Wayfarers, fitted to your face, in a neutral colour. Buy decent ones and keep them in a case. American Optical and Persol have between them dressed early astronauts, athletes, and royalty, which is a reasonable filter for "will this shape date".
- **A small knife or multi-tool.** The most underrated of the six. Nothing bulky, no gimmicks — a small blade, a bottle opener, a torch. Pick for your actual week.

### Try the notebook for a week

If you take one thing from this, take the cheapest one.

Carry a pocket notebook for seven days. Not as a system, not as a commitment — just carry it. Ideas, receipts, a page torn off and handed to someone. Most people find at the end of the week that they were losing more than they realised, and the ones who don't have lost nothing but a week.

### Where restraint tips into performance

Worth saying: there's a version of this that becomes its own kind of showing off. Six perfect objects, arranged, photographed from above.

The point isn't producing beautiful things in public. It's that a small number of well-chosen items removes friction and a large number of gadgets adds it. If you find yourself buying a nicer version of something you already carry happily, you've crossed from equipping yourself into shopping.

A few small things to try:

- Ask whether anything in your family is worth carrying. Often something is, sitting in a drawer.
- Empty your pockets and count. If it's more than six, work out which two are duplicates.
- Carry a notebook for a week and then decide.
- Replace the gimmicky multi-tool with a plain small blade.
