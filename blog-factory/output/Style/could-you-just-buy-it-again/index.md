A thousand bottles, cut down to about ten. The thing that decided it wasn't which ones smelled best. It was a much plainer question: could you walk into a shop tomorrow and buy this again? If yes, it goes. That filter is harsher and more useful than any review.
{{< /lead >}}

### Good is not the same as kept

This is the part that surprises people. Plenty of genuinely excellent fragrances fail the test.

Creed Original Vetiver, Original Santal, and Viking are all good. None of them survived. Bvlgari Aqua Amara is well made and hard to find, and it went too. Tom Ford Black Orchid sat in the rotation for years as a deliberate step outside the men's aisle — still sold, because it's easy to buy back if the regret shows up.

Nothing was wrong with any of them. They just never got attached to anything.

### What actually survives

The bottles that stay are the ones doing a second job.

- **Tied to a person.** A Viktor & Rolf Spicebomb first worn by a thirteen-year-old son, then handed back years later. Unworn for over a year and still not going anywhere.
- **Tied to a place.** Atelier Rebul Bosphorus, found in the brand's own shop in Istanbul, in the rain, on a trip. You can't reorder that.
- **Tied to someone who came before.** Creed Bois du Portugal, an old-school barbershop scent, and the barbershop belonged to a father.
- **Tied to a life you used to have.** Hermès H24 has a metallic note that reads as a firing range to anyone who spent time on one.
- **Tied to a reaction.** A bottle stays because of how someone else responds to it, which has nothing to do with whether you'd pick it for yourself.

Notice how little of that is about smell.

### The reframe

A big fragrance collection isn't really a collection of fragrances. It's a collection of memory triggers, and most of them aren't triggering anything. That second pile has a name, and the name is inventory.

Two things follow from that:

- Anything replaceable is a *purchase*, not a *possession*. You can sell it and rebuy it and lose nothing but the difference.
- Anything irreplaceable should probably never have been on the sell list in the first place, no matter how little you wear it.

### Where the filter gets soft

Worth saying plainly: this test is generous to nostalgia and quite hard on quality.

Keeping a bottle you haven't worn in a year isn't fragrance collecting. It's keeping an object because of who handed it to you, which is a fine reason — it's just a different reason. Call it what it is and the shelf makes sense again. Dress it up as curation and you'll end up with a thousand bottles a second time.

The same goes in reverse. A few things earn their place on merit alone. Roja Dove Elysium holds up better than the entire Creed Aventus line for a lot of people — brighter, easier to find, still feels like something. Parfums de Marly Carlisle is sweet tobacco and smoke and does the job of a good sweater. No story attached. They just work.

### If you're selling the rest

Counterfeits are a genuine problem in fragrance resale, and the cheapest listings are cheap for a reason. Rescentage exists specifically to deal with this by vetting people on both sides of the sale. Use something that checks, rather than an open marketplace where nobody does.

A few small things to try:

- Pick up five bottles and ask the buy-it-again question out loud. The hesitation tells you more than the answer.
- Separate "I like this" from "this is mine". Only the second pile is a collection.
- Stop buying blind on other people's favourites. That's where replaceable bottles come from.
- If a scent is only sold in one city you visited once, quietly move it out of the rotation and keep it.
