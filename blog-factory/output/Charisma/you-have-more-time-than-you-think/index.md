{{< lead >}}
Here's a strange bit of arithmetic about pressure: we give ourselves roughly thirty percent as long to answer as we'd happily give somebody else. Which means the urgency you feel to respond right now is largely invented, and the two seconds you take will read to everyone else as composure.
{{< /lead >}}

### Two or three seconds, deliberately

In a tense exchange, wait before you answer. Not a dramatic silence — two or three seconds.

It does two things at once. It buys you enough room to say the sentence you meant rather than the first one available. And from outside it looks like someone who isn't rattled, which is most of what people mean when they say somebody handled a situation well.

The feeling that you must respond immediately is the thing to distrust. Nobody else in the room is timing you.

### Eye contact is a dial, not a switch

Holding someone's eyes constantly during a confrontation doesn't project strength, it escalates. Two people locked on each other is a fight looking for a starting gun. What you want is to break contact deliberately, and where you break matters:

- **Look down** to take the heat out of it. Effective, but it does read as backing off.
- **Hold** if the only message that matters is that you're not intimidated.
- **Hold for three to five seconds, then break to the side** — not down. This is the useful one. It calms things without conceding anything, because a sideways break isn't a submission signal.

Most people only have the first and second in their repertoire, which is why most disagreements end with someone either caving or squaring up.

### Attention is the thing you actually control

There's someone I think about here — a friend who simply won't turn away from whoever he's talking to. If a third person interrupts, he finishes the current conversation first, then turns.

It's mildly infuriating to be the interrupter. It's excellent to be the person he's listening to. And the effect is that his attention feels like something you were given rather than something you took, which makes people value it.

That's the honest version of "be selective with your attention": give it completely to one thing at a time, and don't let whoever is loudest reassign it.

### Don't apologise if you're not sorry

Apologise properly and quickly when you're actually wrong. That's not in question.

But apologising to end an uncomfortable silence is a different act wearing the same words, and it's corrosive. It teaches the other person that pressure works on you, and it teaches you the same thing. Being willing to say the true and unwelcome thing — knowing it might cost you — is the clearest available signal that you'll be fine either way.

### Where the model breaks down

All of this is drawn from a character written to be frightening, and that's worth naming rather than glossing over.

The composure transfers. The pause, the eye contact, the refusal to apologise on demand — those are real and usable.

What doesn't transfer is attention as a power move. Withholding your attention because it makes people want it is a fundamentally different act from giving it fully to one person at a time, even though they look similar from the outside. One is presence. The other is a small, tiring game that works on strangers and quietly damages everyone close to you.

Fictional characters get to be interesting without having to live with the consequences the following year. Take the composure. Leave the strategy.

A few small things to try:

- Count two seconds before your next answer in a tense conversation.
- Practise breaking eye contact sideways instead of downward.
- Finish the sentence you're in before turning to whoever interrupted.
- Catch one apology you were about to make for nothing.
