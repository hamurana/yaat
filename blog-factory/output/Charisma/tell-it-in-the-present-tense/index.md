{{< lead >}}
Most people tell a story as a report of something that already finished. Switch to *"so I'm standing there and he walks in"* and the same story gets noticeably better, immediately, with no other changes. It's the cheapest upgrade available to anyone who talks.
{{< /lead >}}

### Five things that make a story land

You don't need all of them. One or two moves the needle straight away.

- **Present tense.** As above. It's happening now, in the room.
- **Different voices.** Actually do the other people. Not an impression — just enough change in pitch and pace that we know who's talking.
- **Exaggerate against yourself.** Make yourself slightly more foolish than you were. Never the other way.
- **Talk through a smile.** Smiling while you tell it primes everyone else to smile, which is a large part of why the same joke gets a laugh from one person and silence from another.
- **Pause on the punchline.** Land it, then stop, before you carry on.

Present tense and voices are the two to start with.

### Pause in the middle, not at the end

Almost everyone pauses where the sentence stops, which is where people naturally start talking. For a serious story, pause somewhere the sentence obviously isn't finished — *"and what you didn't see behind you was…"* — and the room holds. We're not built to walk away from an unfinished sentence.

The practical problem is getting cut off during a long emotional pause. The fix is physical: keep your hand up, mid-gesture. It reads clearly as *not done yet* and nobody consciously notices you're doing it.

### Tease yourself, but only where it doesn't sting

Self-teasing reads as confidence, on one condition — it has to be about something you genuinely don't mind.

The distinction is a burn with no heat. Joking about being shorter than a professional basketball player works because nobody, including you, thinks it's a real problem. Joking about the thing that actually keeps you up at night reads differently, bothers you afterwards, and gives everyone else permission to keep going.

Pick the harmless ones. There are more of them than you'd think.

### Say "talk about"

The single most useful listening habit is a phrase rather than a technique.

Instead of a question, say *talk about* — talk about how that project started, talk about the year you moved. It's open, it hands over the floor completely, and it doesn't corner anyone into a yes or no. One well-placed one can produce four uninterrupted minutes from someone who'd been giving one-line answers all evening.

Two rules make it work:

- Aim at things people are proud or excited about.
- Aim especially at things nobody thinks to ask them about. Everyone gets asked about the obvious thing. Almost nobody gets asked about the part they actually care about.

### Arrive louder than feels natural

Most people enter a room quietly and small, then spend twenty minutes warming up. Coming in with a bit more energy than you feel makes the rest of the evening much easier, because you've already set your own tone rather than adopting the room's.

You don't have to be the person starting a wave. Smiling every time you walk into a room is enough, and it's a low enough bar to actually do.

One caveat worth keeping: the playful register only works if you can drop it. The people who are genuinely good at this switch to serious the moment something real comes up, without a wobble. Permanent high energy isn't charm, it's a wall.

A few small things to try:

- Retell your next story in present tense.
- Do one other person's voice.
- Replace a question with "talk about…" this week.
- Smile as you walk into the next room, before anyone's looking.
