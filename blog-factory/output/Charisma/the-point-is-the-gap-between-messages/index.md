{{< lead >}}
A text isn't the thing. What she thinks about in the hours after reading it is the thing. Once you see it that way, the goal stops being clever messages and becomes something simpler: does she arrive already looking forward to seeing you?
{{< /lead >}}

### Five things worth sending

- **Something thoughtful, unprompted.** Most men send nothing, on the theory that she already knows. She doesn't — people need telling, and the ones who've been told recently behave differently.
- **Something a bit exposing.** *Last night was one of those rare ones I didn't want to end.* You're risking a flat reply, which is exactly why it registers. Nothing that costs you nothing to send is worth much to receive.
- **An actual compliment.** Obvious, and the first thing to disappear from a long relationship. Send one for no reason on an ordinary Tuesday.
- **A shared memory** — but it has to be about both of you and it has to be a good one, so that what she's remembering and how she feels about you are the same thing. *Sitting at my desk still thinking about last night.*
- **What she means to you**, pitched at the actual stage you're at. Ten years in, that can be everything you'd say at a wedding. Ten days in, it's much lighter — curiosity about who she is rather than a declaration.

Two rules on top: emoji are fine and warm, roughly one every other message, and proofread the thing. A typo costs you more than the emoji earned.

### The claim underneath it

Someone unattractive by every surface measure, no impressive job, not especially pleasant — and a woman flying to see him every other month, unable to wait to get off the plane. Because in between, he'd built something worth arriving for.

I don't think this proves texting beats everything. But it does show anticipation is a real and underrated thing to be good at, and that almost nobody is deliberately building any.

### The part I'd throw away

The example messages. All of them.

Scripts are the weakest bit of any advice like this, because a borrowed line reads as borrowed. She's not comparing your text to nothing — she's comparing it to how you talk in person, and any gap between those two shows up immediately.

Take the five categories. Write them in your own words, badly if necessary. A slightly clumsy sentence that's clearly yours beats a polished one that isn't.

Also, the claim that emoji users have more sex is the kind of statistic that gets repeated because it's fun. It's a correlation at best, and probably says more about who uses emoji than about what emoji do. Use them because warmth is hard to get across in text, not because of that.

### The one that matters most is the least clever

Everything above works hardest on people who've stopped doing it — the ten-years-in couples who've quietly agreed that this sort of thing was for the beginning.

There's no technique in *I loved how you looked heading out this morning*. It's just a person deciding to say the thing they thought and normally wouldn't. That's most of it.

A note on the framing: this gets sold as seven texts. There are five moves and two rules, which is fine, but worth knowing before you go looking for the missing two.

A few small things to try:

- Send one compliment today attached to no occasion.
- Reference one specific detail from the last time you saw each other.
- Say one thing you'd normally leave unsaid because it's slightly exposing.
- Reread before sending. Every time.
