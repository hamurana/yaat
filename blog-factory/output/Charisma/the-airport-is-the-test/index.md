{{< lead >}}
Almost nobody sees what you earn or what you drive. Everybody sees how fast you're moving. Speed is the most visible thing about a person, and hurrying reads as *behind*, which is why the airport is the honest test of everything else.
{{< /lead >}}

### Slow is the whole trick

Nearly every mannerism people read as composure is one thing wearing different clothes: acting as though you have enough time.

- Walk at an even pace. Rushing looks late and slightly undignified even when you aren't late.
- Sit like you're staying. Open posture, taking up the space you were given. Folding inward gets read as someone easy to push around, fairly or not.
- Leave a beat before answering — half a second is enough. It buys you thinking time and quietly tells the other person that what's coming was considered.
- Pack the night before and get to the airport early, so the version of you that moves through security isn't the frantic one.

That last one isn't really about travel. It's the rehearsal. If you can be unhurried in the one place that reliably makes people lose the plot, the rest is easy.

### Not reacting isn't the same as not feeling

Non-reactive gets misread as stony. It isn't. The point is that things outside your control don't get to run the show — the delayed flight, the rude email, the person who cut in.

There's genuinely nothing to be gained from being visibly rattled by small things. It reads as drama, and drama is expensive. But the version where you clamp down and feel nothing is worse and doesn't hold up under pressure. What you're building is a longer gap between the thing happening and your response to it, not an absence of response.

### Say what you want, plainly

Indecisive speech leaks status faster than almost anything. Not just in a meeting — ordering a drink counts. So does saying where you'd like to eat.

If you can't say clearly what you want, it's hard to be surprised when nobody comes along with you.

### Compliments have to be specific or they're noise

"You're so beautiful" is the archetype of the useless compliment. She has heard it a hundred times, from people who hadn't looked properly, and it carries no information about her.

Specific is the entire game. Notice the thing that's actually true and particular to that person, especially something other people miss, and say that instead. Being seen precisely is rare enough to be startling — in dating, at work, with friends.

### The bit I'd rewrite

There's a piece of advice in here about being reliable but not too predictable — keeping an edge, being a bit of a maverick, on the grounds that people fantasise about James Bond rather than Mr Rogers.

Half right. Reliability really can flatten into being taken for granted, and that's worth noticing.

But manufactured unpredictability is transparent and a bit exhausting to be around. Nobody has ever been made more interesting by deliberately being harder to predict. The people who read as unpredictable in an appealing way are just people with a genuinely full life — things they're good at, places they go, opinions arrived at honestly — so you can't guess their next move because there's actually a lot going on.

Build the life. The edge is a by-product, not a technique.

### Underneath all of it

Whether people respect you is downstream of whether you appear to respect yourself, and that's mostly visible in maintenance — how you dress, whether you train, sleep, and keep yourself in order.

None of which is exciting. It's just that in a stretch where a lot of people have quietly let standards slip, ordinary self-possession stands out more than it has any right to.

A few small things to try:

- Get to the airport an hour earlier than you need to and notice what changes.
- Add half a second before your next answer.
- Give one compliment specific enough that it couldn't apply to anyone else.
- Say where you want to eat instead of asking.
