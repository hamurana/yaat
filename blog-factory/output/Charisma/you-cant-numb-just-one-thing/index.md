Turning the volume down on sadness turns it down on everything. Clarity goes. So does joy, closeness, and any sense of what you actually want. There's no dial that only affects the feelings you'd rather not have.
{{< /lead >}}

### The rational man is usually just the numb one

"I'm not emotional, I'm rational" almost always arrives with a quiet second half: *and that makes me better than you.* Which is itself a feeling, doing a lot of work in the background.

Emotions aren't decoration on top of thinking. They're part of the machinery. Damage the emotional parts of the brain and IQ tests come out fine while ordinary decisions fall apart — picking a pen colour can take half an hour. We choose things in order to feel something: valued, safe, not a failure.

### Shame is what keeps the pattern running

Here's a useful way to find yours. Think of the thing you've been telling yourself to stop doing for years — and are still doing. Not working out. How you eat. Snapping at people you like.

That's not weak willpower. Shame is the emotion whose whole function is to hold other emotions still, which means it holds the behaviour still too. Beating yourself up about it at night is the mechanism, not the cure.

The way out is usually something small and uncomfortable said out loud. An apology. An admission. The thing that makes you pucker slightly as you say it.

### Grinding is avoidance with a respectable job title

Working hard and grinding aren't the same animal. The tell is the cycle: push, push, push, burn out, check out entirely, repeat. The expensive version of checking out just has better production values.

Grinding also aims your attention at the wrong thing. Ticking items gives a small hit each time, so you keep ticking. The better question, asked honestly:

> What are the three things that would make everything else on this list irrelevant or much easier? Did I do any of them today?

The answer is almost always no. Six months of hard, undirected work can be wiped out by one shift in the tools everyone uses — and the conversations you avoided having during those six months are usually still sitting there, waiting.

Chasing a number has the same shape. The "enough" figure keeps moving — this year, then five years, then the whole family forever — because the thing actually being chased isn't money.

### Ladder the want down to the need

Before something you compulsively reach for, ask what you don't want to feel right now. Then follow the want down:

- Porn → wanting to relax → wanting to feel connected → wanting to feel whole.

The need at the bottom is never shameful. It's just being met by a strategy that can't meet it. Saying the real one out loud — *I really want to feel connected right now* — is the awkward move that works, where the substitute never quite does.

### Two scripts worth memorising

**When someone is angry at you.** Anger aimed at a person is a way of using a feeling to control them. Underneath it there's usually someone who feels unseen, out of control, or unappreciated. Address that instead: *what you're saying matters, and I want to help — it's hard to when you're shouting at me.* Said once, calmly, it can end a habit that's been running for months.

**When someone is scared.** Ask what they're scared *might happen*, never what they're scared of. Everyone can answer the first one, because it points at an outcome rather than a character flaw. Then say the honest thing: I understand that's a concern, and I am — or am not — willing to take that risk.

### The part that's hardest to believe

Good feelings are harder to sit with than bad ones. Praise, real closeness, being genuinely happy — these leave you without your usual labels, which is more exposing than being miserable in a familiar way.

It works like training. Uncomfortable, then interesting, then something you look for.

A few small things to try:

- Name the thing you've been "shoulding" yourself about for years, and make one small repair.
- Ask the three-things question before opening your to-do list tomorrow.
- Next time you reach for the usual escape, ask what you're avoiding feeling.
- Use the fear question on yourself once this week.
