{{< lead >}}
Salting your food before tasting it is a tiny thing that gets read as a large one: you decide before you have the information. Some interviewers watch for it on purpose. It's also, quietly, the cheapest table habit anyone can fix.
{{< /lead >}}

### Every rule here is the same rule

Table manners get treated as decoration, or worse, as a class thing. They're neither. Nearly every rule exists to make the meal easier for somebody else — the host, the server, the person who's currently mid-chew.

Once you see that, the list stops being arbitrary and starts being obvious.

### The mechanics, worth drilling until they're boring

- Work outside-in through the silverware. The outermost fork and knife are for the starter.
- Fork and knife together at four or five o'clock means finished. Splayed apart means still going.
- Used cutlery never goes back on the tablecloth. It rests on the plate.
- Dessert fork and spoon sit above the plate. Bread plate with its own knife, top left. Water glass and wine glass, top right.
- Napkin on your lap as you sit. At the end, folded loosely beside the plate — not on it.
- Phone on silent, in a pocket. Not face-down on the table, because you will pick it up.

Either eating method is fine, American or continental. The argument for continental — fork stays left, knife stays right — is just that you're not switching hands every few seconds, so more of your attention stays on the conversation.

### The ones that are actually about other people

- Wait for the host to lift their fork.
- Pass dishes left to right. Salt and pepper always travel together, even if someone only asked for one.
- Serve yourself with the serving spoons, never your own cutlery. Don't reach across anyone — ask.
- Take a countable, fair share. There'll be seconds.
- Try everything. A small polite portion beats a refusal. Serious allergies get mentioned before the host starts cooking, not at the table.
- Don't ask a question of someone who's just taken a bite. You've created ten to fifteen seconds of everyone waiting.
- Leaving the table is "excuse me, I'll be right back." Never the reason. Five minutes, maximum.
- Glasses by the stem. Toast with wine, champagne or beer — not water.

### Bread is local

In the US you butter the slice and bite it. In Germany that's a small faux pas: you break off a piece by hand, butter that piece, eat it, repeat.

Neither is more correct. Both are worth knowing, because the useful skill isn't memorising one country's rules — it's noticing what the table around you is doing and matching it.

### Where this goes wrong

The failure mode of caring about manners is using them to score people. Which is the one rule that really matters: never correct anyone else's, and never criticise the host, especially not in front of others.

Nobody's going to tell you when you've got it wrong either. They'll just stop inviting you. That's the whole enforcement mechanism, and it's why the small stuff is worth getting automatic — not to look refined, but so you can stop thinking about your hands and pay attention to the people you came to see.

A few small things to try:

- Taste first. Always.
- Put the phone in a pocket before you sit.
- Learn the finished position so you stop hovering over your plate wondering.
- Ask your question after they've swallowed.
- Say something specific and true about the food.
