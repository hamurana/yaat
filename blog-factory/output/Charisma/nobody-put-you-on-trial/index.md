{{< lead >}}
Walk into a room like you owe it an explanation and people will start looking for one. Most of what gets read as low confidence isn't nerves at all. It's the quiet assumption that you're being assessed, leaking out through six small habits.
{{< /lead >}}

### The six tells

- **Acting like you're on trial.** Tense shoulders, a tight smile, a slightly higher and over-polite voice, hands busy, eyes moving around the room like you're waiting for a verdict.
- **Talking like you need permission.** Sentences that trail off. Statements that curl up into questions. "Just," "maybe," "you know." Rushing so you don't take up too much time.
- **Apologising for existing.** "Sorry" when someone mishears your name. "Sorry" before an opinion.
- **Reaching for status.** Dropping achievements into gaps, name-dropping, mentioning what you own.
- **Agreeing when you don't.** The reflexive "yeah, totally" over something you actually liked.
- **Trying to be interesting.** Working to be funny or deep, which requires most of your attention, which is why the other person can tell.

### They're all one habit

Underneath every one of those is the same background question: *do they like me?*

Swapping it for a different question changes your posture and your voice before you've consciously done anything: *do I like how I'm showing up right now?* One is a verdict you're waiting on. The other is something you're in charge of.

### The apology one does the most damage

The others are aimed outward. That one's aimed at yourself.

Every unnecessary "sorry" is a small note that you're not quite allowed to take up space, and you're the one reading it. Say it enough and it stops being a verbal habit and becomes a fact about you.

The swap is to say the true thing instead of the sorry thing:

- "Thanks for waiting" instead of "sorry I'm late."
- "Here's my take" instead of "sorry, but I just think."

Same information. Neither one is rude. One of them costs you something.

### Story instead of flex

The same fact goes two ways.

*"I've been in the gym six days a week."*

*"I kept falling off with training, but once I started showing up even on the off days, I noticed I was steadier about everything else too."*

Second one is more interesting and makes the other person feel like they were told something rather than shown something. A rough test before you share: does this teach, show growth, or connect? If it's none of those, it's just for you.

### Where I'd put a limit on this

"Stop agreeing" gets taken too far. The fix isn't to disagree more, and it definitely isn't to look for friction to prove you're not a pushover — that's the same performance wearing a different jacket.

The point is narrower. When you liked the film everyone's calling rubbish, say you liked it. Low-stakes honesty, held lightly, no need to win. You don't have to have a contrary opinion about everything. You just have to stop manufacturing agreement you don't feel, because once you'll fake a small one, nobody can fully trust the big ones.

### The through-line

None of this is adding charisma. It's removing six things that were quietly costing you it. What's left underneath — someone who isn't managing your impression of them — is the thing people describe as presence.

A few small things to try:

- Catch one "sorry" this week and swap it for a thank you.
- Finish a sentence and let the silence sit rather than filling it.
- Ask one question you actually want the answer to, then don't say anything about yourself.
- The next time you disagree about something that doesn't matter, say so, pleasantly, and move on.
