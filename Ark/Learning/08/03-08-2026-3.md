**The Hook & Core Thesis**

The [[yield curve]] — specifically when short-term government bond yields rise above long-term yields ("inversion") — has preceded essentially every US recession since the 1950s, and it just finished its longest inversion on record (27 straight months, mid-2022 to late 2024) before flipping back to a still-thin normal shape.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **The core mechanism is a lending-risk analogy.** Longer loans normally demand higher interest because more can go wrong over a longer horizon; a government bond's yield is just interest on a loan to the government, plotted against how long you wait to be repaid (1 month to 30 years).
- **The most-watched comparison is the "2s10s spread"** — the 2-year yield versus the 10-year yield. The 2-year tracks current Federal Reserve policy; the 10-year reflects the market's expectations for growth and inflation over the coming decade.
- **Four curve shapes to recognize:** normal (upward-sloping, the healthy default), flat (short and long rates converge, signaling genuine uncertainty), steep (bigger-than-normal gap, typically post-recession as the Fed has cut short rates while long rates stay elevated on expected recovery), and inverted (short rates exceed long rates — "backwards," and the headline-making shape).
- **Inversion reflects the bond market's collective bet, not a mechanical cause of recessions.** Short rates are pinned high by current Fed policy fighting inflation; long rates get pulled down because investors expect the Fed will eventually have to cut rates to rescue a slowing economy, so they buy long bonds now to lock in today's yield.
- **The signal has an unreliable lag, not a fixed countdown.** Historically the gap between inversion and the actual recession has ranged roughly 6–24 months (2019 inversion → recession about 10 months later; the inversion ahead of 2008 had roughly a 22-month lag).
- **It's a probability signal, not a certainty**, and doesn't indicate severity — professional investors treat it as one input alongside unemployment claims, credit spreads, and manufacturing data.

**High-Yield Assets**

- **Track record**: [Transcript imprecise] — the video opens citing "eight recessions out of eight" correctly signaled since 1968 with one false alarm (1966), but later cites San Francisco Federal Reserve research finding every US recession between 1955 and 2018 (nine recessions) was preceded by a yield-curve inversion, with the same single 1966 false alarm. Both figures are given as-spoken; the discrepancy (eight vs. nine) is the video's own, not corrected here.
- **Current snapshot (mid-July 2026)**: 10-year Treasury yield ≈ **4.55%**, 2-year Treasury yield ≈ **4.17%**, putting the 2s10s spread at roughly **+0.4 percentage points** — technically not inverted, but a "thin, narrow normal" compared to the healthier 1–1.5 percentage-point gaps typical of a strong expansion.
- **Longest inversion on record**: continuous from mid-2022 to late 2024, about **27 months**.
- **Lag examples**: 2019 inversion (May) → recession start March 2020 (~10 months); pre-2008 crisis inversion → roughly 22-month lag.
- **DIY tool**: [[investing.com]] — search "US 10-year treasury yield" and "US 2-year treasury yield" separately, or "US government bonds yield curve" for the full plotted shape (1 month to 30 years) without doing the subtraction yourself. The same 2-vs-10 logic also works with other pairs, e.g. 3-month vs. 10-year.

**The "So What?" Factor**

The yield curve just exited its longest-ever inversion but landed in an unusually thin "normal" — not the clear all-clear a healthy curve would suggest. Given the historical 6–24 month lag between inversion and recession, this is a signal to watch alongside other indicators (unemployment claims, credit spreads, manufacturing data) rather than dismiss, and checking it yourself takes under a minute via a free site like investing.com.
