**The Hook & Core Thesis**

Across a full week of night shifts covering the sickest patients in a UK A&E, **[[Dr. Alex]]** logged the diagnosis of roughly 120 people — and all but one had an illness that could very likely have been prevented if something had changed years earlier.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- Across four night shifts (~30 patients a night in resus/majors) he wrote down the diagnosis for every single patient, leaving out identifying details.
- Of ~120 people, all but one had a condition with a preventable origin — the pattern became "monotonous," which he says is the whole point.
- The same small handful of diagnoses kept repeating: heart attacks, unstable angina, strokes, falls, diabetic amputations, kidney failure, heart failure.
- Nearly all of these patients were younger than expected for these diseases — it's not the disease that's unusual, it's the age it arrives at.
- This is not a criticism of the patients — it's a criticism of a system built to patch people up after they break rather than help them never break in the first place.
- Medicine excels at rescuing people mid-crisis (stents, catheters for a heart attack) but is poor at stopping the crisis from happening.
- A&E departments running permanently over capacity with preventable disease crowd out the room, time, and staff needed for the genuinely unpreventable cases.
- Prevention isn't only self-interest — it's leaving capacity in an overloaded system for people who have no other chance.

---
**High-Yield Assets**

- Diagnoses seen repeatedly across the week: **heart attacks**, **unstable angina**, **strokes**, **falls** (linked to sarcopenia and dementia), **type 2 diabetes**-related amputations, **kidney failure**, **heart failure**.
- Roughly 30 patients per night shift in UK emergency medicine; ~120 across a standard 4-shift week.
- The moments he flags as the real leverage points: a first diagnosis of **hypertension** or **prediabetes** — "the moments to change somebody's life for good" if acted on.
- Button-battery ingestion mechanism: a lodged battery generates a small electrical current that triggers a chemical reaction, burning through the esophagus, stomach, or intestinal wall within minutes to hours, and can erode into a major blood vessel.
- Safety guidance: keep button batteries out of reach of small children, check that battery compartments on toys/remotes/singing cards are properly screwed shut, and treat any suspected swallowing as an immediate emergency — call 999/911 and go straight to A&E rather than waiting to see if symptoms appear.

---
**Case Studies / Anecdotes**

- **The button-battery patient** — a 4-year-old girl who swallowed a button battery arrived barely conscious having already lost a large amount of blood. The resus department was already full past capacity, so much of her initial resuscitation happened in the hospital corridor. She was the one patient on his list whose illness no lifestyle change could ever have prevented, and he calls it the hardest shift of his career.
- **His brother-in-law** — diagnosed with prediabetes a couple of months earlier, with nothing done about it since, used as a live example of a missed early-intervention window.

---
**The "So What?" Factor**

Preventable disease doesn't just shorten one person's life — it eats the beds, minutes, and staff attention a hospital needs for genuine emergencies, like a 4-year-old with no other chance. Acting on an early warning sign (a hypertension or prediabetes diagnosis) while it's still easy to change isn't just personal health maintenance; it's what keeps the system able to save the people who truly can't be saved any other way.
