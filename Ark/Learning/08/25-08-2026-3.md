**The Hook & Core Thesis**

[[Warren Buffett]] — the greatest living practitioner of [[value investing]] — instructed in his will that 90% of the money left to his wife go into "a plain, ordinary [[S&P 500]] index fund." The resolution of that apparent contradiction is the whole video: **Buffett knows the difference between what worked for him personally and what works for almost everyone else.**

---

**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **⭐ The framing question: "Why would the greatest stock picker alive tell his own family to skip the thing that made him rich?"** The answer given: he had "decades of full-time focus, a small army of analysts, and access to deals regular investors simply can't get." Most people instead have "a day job, limited time to research individual companies, and a strong tendency to make emotional decisions at exactly the wrong moment."
- **⭐ Indexing wins by removing failure modes, not by being cleverer.** "A low-cost index fund removes almost all the ways you can mess up. **You can't pick a bad stock if you're not picking stocks. You can't panic sell your favourite company if you don't have one.**"
- **Buffett's record is real but is a sample of one.** He is "one guy, running one company for 60 years," achieving "something almost nobody else has been able to repeat." The video explicitly warns against reading his numbers as evidence that value investing is generally more profitable.
- **⭐ The data that matters more for a normal investor is the fund-manager data, not Buffett's.** In 2025, **79% of large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500** — and "this isn't just a one-year phenomenon." Conclusion drawn: "beating the S&P 500 consistently is extremely difficult, even for professional fund managers."
- **Value vs growth flips depending on the window you happen to live in.** Academic data back to **1926** shows value stocks beating growth over the very long run; over roughly the **last decade that pattern "flipped hard."** "Depending on which decade you happen to be investing in, either style could look like the obvious winner."
- **The bull case for value right now rests on the index looking expensive.** With the S&P 500's P/E near 30 against a long-run average nearer 20, "deliberately looking for companies that are still reasonably priced… can feel like the safer move." The video immediately hedges this: "whether that actually plays out is a different question."
- **⭐ "Value index fund" is close to a contradiction in terms, and often isn't cheap.** A mechanical screen ranks companies *relative to their universe* rather than applying an absolute threshold, and the survivors are still **weighted largely by market capitalisation** — so "a massive company can have a large weight in a value index, even if its valuation doesn't look extremely cheap." Result: such funds "can still have a relatively high average price-to-earnings ratio, which is not really following Benjamin Graham's principles."
- **DIY stock picking is named the riskiest path "by a wide margin."** If professionals with full-time teams of analysts mostly fail to beat the index, "someone picking stocks on the side is taking on a lot of risk for a strategy that's genuinely difficult to pull off well."
- **Value investing "can absolutely work, and the long-term data backs that up"** — but it demands real time and "can go through long stretches, sometimes a full decade, where it lags behind the simpler approach."
- **The offered resolution is a blend, not a winner.** Many experienced investors "hold a core of index funds for stability and simplicity, and then use a smaller portion of their portfolio to try value picks or a value-focused fund."

**High-Yield Assets**

**Buffett's will (2013)**
- **90%** into a plain, ordinary **S&P 500 index fund**.
- **10%** into **short-term government bonds**.
- Instruction was to the trustee managing money for his wife. Explicitly: "no stock picking, not trying to find the next great company, no getting fancy at all."

**The headline performance numbers**
| | Period | Compound annual return | $1,000 grows to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Berkshire Hathaway** | 1964–2024 | ~**20%/yr** | **$56 million** |
| **S&P 500** | 1964–2024 | ~**10%/yr** | ~**$304,000** |

*The video's opening states the Berkshire figure as "more than $50 million today" and the later section gives $56 million — the same claim at two levels of precision.*

**The fund-manager evidence**
- **S&P Dow Jones** publishes an annual report tracking actively managed funds against their benchmark index. *[The video does not name the report.]*
- **2025: 79% of large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500.** Described as a persistent, not one-off, result.

**Value vs growth, by era**
- **Since 1926 (academic data):** value stocks as a group **beat** growth stocks over the very long run.
- **Roughly the last decade:** value returned **~10%/yr**; growth returned **closer to 14–15%/yr**, "driven largely by big tech, and now AI-related companies."

**Valuation — the [[price-to-earnings ratio]]**
- Defined plainly: "how many dollars you're paying today for one dollar of that company's yearly profit." Higher number = paying more for the same dollar of earnings.
- **S&P 500 currently trading at a P/E close to 30**; its **long-term historical average sits closer to 20**.
- Cause given: "a small handful of huge tech companies have gotten very expensive and now make up a big chunk of the index."

**Index investing — the mechanics**
- Buys "a little bit of everything in a given market, in the same proportions as that market." An S&P 500 fund gives you "a tiny slice of all 500 of the biggest companies in the US, from Apple to a random industrial parts maker you've never heard of."
- No manager deciding what to buy or sell — "it's mostly automated, it just tracks the index."
- **Fees: some of the big S&P 500 funds charge less than 0.1% a year.**
- The underlying bet: "you're not betting on any single company, you're betting on the economy as a whole going up over time."
- **Named implementation:** a total US stock market fund or an S&P 500 fund from a large provider, with **[[Vanguard]]** given as the example.

**Value investing — origin and definition**
- Finding "specific companies that are priced lower than what they're actually worth."
- **[[Benjamin Graham]]**, a professor in the **1930s**, "more or less invented the discipline." He taught students to examine **earnings, assets and debt** to establish real worth, then compare that to the stock price — "if the price is well below the real value, that's your opportunity."
- **Buffett was one of Graham's students at Columbia**, and built [[Berkshire Hathaway]] on the idea.

**⭐ The four routes into value, ranked by what they demand of you**
1. **Value mutual funds / value ETFs** — the most common route for regular people. Professional managers apply Graham-style principles on your behalf, "usually for a higher fee than a plain index fund."
2. **Value index funds** — a **rules-based formula**, "no judgment involved, just a mechanical screen." Fees "tend to be really cheap as there is no manager behind the fund," but see the market-cap-weighting caveat above. The video's worked counter-example: the index is *not* saying "only buy companies with a price-to-earnings ratio below 15."
3. **Doing it yourself** — reading financial statements and picking stocks as Graham taught. "The riskiest path by a wide margin," requiring "real time, real financial literacy, and a level of discipline that's hard to hold on to when a stock you bought keeps dropping."
4. **Buying Berkshire Hathaway shares** — legally *not* a fund but "an actual operating company" that owns businesses outright and holds a large stock portfolio, applying Graham's principles "at an enormous scale." Buying a share means "handing your money to a team built entirely around value investing and letting them make the decisions instead of you."

**Case Studies/Anecdotes**

- **⭐ The will itself — the video's spine.** In 2013 Buffett "wrote something strange into his will," directing the trustee managing his wife's money into a 90/10 index-and-short-bonds split. The video opens on it, poses it as a puzzle, and returns to it in the closing section to answer it. The rhetorical force comes from the contrast with his own record: the man who turned $1,000 in 1964 into more than $50 million telling his family to do "something almost boring."
- **The Graham-to-Buffett lineage.** A professor in the 1930s teaching a method at Columbia; "one of Graham's students… was a young guy named Warren Buffett," who "took that idea and built an entire empire on it." Used to establish that value investing is a genuine discipline with a documented pedigree, not a folk strategy.
- **The undervalued house analogy.** Value investing is "like finding a house listed under market price because the seller doesn't realise what they have." The company "might be fine, even great" — the price simply "hasn't caught up to that yet."

**The "So What?" Factor**

The actionable takeaway is that the value-versus-index question is the wrong frame for most people. The real variable is **whether you personally have the time, the information access and the emotional discipline that value investing requires** — and the professional-underperformance data suggests most people, including most professionals, do not. The honest answer to the title is *it depends on who is asking*.

- **Treat Buffett's returns as inspiration, not evidence.** One person over 60 years is not a repeatable strategy.
- **Weigh the 79% figure more heavily than the Berkshire figure** if you are deciding what to do with your own money.
- **Expect a decade-long stretch of underperformance** from value and decide in advance whether you could sit through it.
- **Check what's actually inside a "value" fund.** Relative ranking plus market-cap weighting can leave you with a high average P/E and no real Graham discipline — look at the fund's own P/E rather than trusting the label.
- **Compare the fee lines directly:** under 0.1%/yr for a large index fund, higher for an actively managed value fund, cheap again for a rules-based value index fund.
- **Note the valuation backdrop without over-trading on it** — a P/E near 30 against a ~20 average is the strongest argument for looking at value, and the video declines to promise it will pay off.
- **Consider the core-and-satellite blend** — index funds as the core for stability and simplicity, a smaller sleeve for value picks or a value fund — as "best of both without betting everything on either one."
- **Be honest about the DIY path.** It needs real time, real financial literacy, and the discipline to hold a falling position.

*[Framing note: the title asks "which is better long-term?" and the video declines to name a winner, which is a fair reflection of the evidence it presents rather than an evasion — value beats growth since 1926, growth has beaten value over the last decade, and the deciding factor offered is the investor's own circumstances. The video states its own disclaimer that it "is not financial advice."]*
