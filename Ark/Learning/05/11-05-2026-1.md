**Topic: Does Vitamin D Build Muscle? Debunking Supplement Claims**

The video critically examines viral social media claims that high-dose [[Vitamin D]] supplements can redirect calories from fat to muscle and dramatically accelerate muscle [[growth]].

**What Vitamin D actually does** Vitamin D behaves more like a hormone than a vitamin. It's produced in the skin via sunlight, processed by the liver and kidneys into its active form, and binds to receptors found throughout the body. Its well-established roles are regulating calcium absorption (bone health), supporting immune function, and playing a modest role in muscle function — particularly in deficient people.

**The research being cited** The claims largely rest on a mouse study (not yet peer-reviewed) where mice given very high doses of Vitamin D showed lower myostatin (the "brake" on muscle growth) and increased lean mass. However, the presenter points out several critical problems: it's a rodent study, it hasn't been peer-reviewed, the doses were extremely high and dangerous, and the results haven't been replicated in humans in randomised controlled trials.

**The risks of high-dose supplementation** Vitamin D toxicity is real. Excess [[Vitamin D]] raises blood calcium, which can cause kidney damage, cardiac issues, and other serious health problems. The high doses promoted online are far above safe recommended levels.

**Who should actually supplement** People who are genuinely deficient (common in countries with limited sunlight, like the UK) can benefit from correcting that deficiency — improving muscle function, reducing fall risk in older adults, and supporting bone and immune health. The recommended approach is to get a blood test, follow NHS/standard dosage guidelines (400–1000 IU/day), and where possible get Vitamin D from sunlight, oily fish, and eggs.

**What actually builds muscle** The four evidence-based drivers of muscle growth are: progressive resistance training, adequate protein intake, sufficient sleep (when growth hormone is released), and enough total calories. Vitamin D at any dose does not replace or rival these — it simply ensures deficiency isn't undermining normal muscle function.

**Conclusion** The [[supplement]] industry is exploiting real but preliminary and misrepresented science to sell expensive, potentially harmful high-dose Vitamin D products. The honest takeaway: fix a deficiency if you have one, but don't expect supraphysiological doses to give you an edge in the gym.
