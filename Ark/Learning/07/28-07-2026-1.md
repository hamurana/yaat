**The Hook & Core Thesis**

Etiquette has nothing to do with which fork you use — it's a habit of showing kindness, consideration, and trust to other people, and the seven signs you're falling short of it all trace back to whether you're doing it only when someone's watching.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **Reducing etiquette to table manners.** Thinking etiquette starts and ends with "which fork to use" misses the point entirely — it's about building trust and showing consideration, "across the dinner table, across the boardroom table, whatever it is."
- **Refusing to take responsibility.** Assuming someone else bumped into you, not them, and never being the one who says "my fault" is corrosive — the well-mannered move in an ambiguous moment is to just take the blame yourself.
- **Hiding rudeness behind "I'm just blunt/honest."** Bluntness that isn't necessary usually creates more work and misunderstanding, not less — a softer way of saying the same thing still sets the boundary without the collateral damage.
- **Only being polite to people who can help you.** Manners that switch on for people you're trying to impress and off everywhere else aren't manners, they're performance — genuine etiquette is built in private, in moments nobody's grading you.
- **Chronic lateness.** Being consistently late signals poor time management and tells people they're not important enough to plan around — it erodes trust in romantic, professional, and friend relationships alike.
- **Phone habits.** Checking your phone around other people without noticing you're doing it becomes rude by default — the fix is pausing to ask whether you're actually handling something urgent or just opening Instagram.
- **Ignoring casual RSVPs.** Formal events aren't the only ones that need a response — for a group dinner or house party, reply the same way you were invited (text for text, call for call), confirm plus-ones in advance, and give the host notice the moment your plans change, since they're planning food and seating around your answer.

---
**High-Yield Assets**

- **The private-moment test:** the best way to build real etiquette is practicing it in moments where no one is trying to impress you and you're not trying to impress anyone — habits formed there transfer everywhere; habits performed only for an audience "fail you when you need them."
- **Irish exit verdict:** acceptable at large, casual gatherings, but only if you tell the host you're leaving — you don't owe the whole room a goodbye, but you owe the host one.

---
**Case Studies/Anecdotes**

- **The tennis-court trick:** the host, James, had a friend in London who was chronically late to their matches, so he started booking the court an hour later than he told his friend — when the friend showed up "on time" and had to wait anyway, James admitted the trick, and the friend's response was just "yeah, fair enough."
- **Molly's own phone habit:** the etiquette expert, Molly Tomlin, admits she didn't get an iPhone until 18 and still has the opposite problem — she can be too distant and slow to reply — which she uses to illustrate that the goal isn't zero phone use, it's noticing your own pattern.

---
**The "So What?" Factor**

Every one of these seven signs is really the same test applied to a different situation: do you act the same way when nobody's checking? Chronic lateness, performative politeness, and reflexive blame-shifting all fail that test. Pick the one you recognize in yourself and start practicing it in private first — that's where the habit actually gets built.
