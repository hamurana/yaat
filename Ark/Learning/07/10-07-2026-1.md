## The Hook & Core Thesis
[[Humphrey Yang]] walks Vanguard's newest report on average and median 401k balances for every age bracket — and the single loudest signal in the data is that **[[Automatic Enrollment]] beats willpower**: balances are up across the board year over year, but mostly because the market did the lifting, while the people who durably win are the ones whose contributions were automated before they could procrastinate.

## Key Arguments Checklist
- **Automatic enrollment is the single biggest behavior driving higher balances.** Auto-enrolled employees start with *lower* balances but overtake voluntary enrollees by years 2–3, and by year 10+ the gap is $220K vs $133K. Vanguard's behavioral-finance research pins the voluntary-plan failure on three traits — lack of planning skill (can't delay gratification), indecision (complex choice → no choice), and plain [[Procrastination]] — and auto-enrollment neutralizes all three at once. Corollary: automate *anything* you can in your finances, including annual contribution-rate increases.
- **The averages are skewed; medians tell the real story.** Median balances run at roughly a quarter to a third of the averages in every bracket (high earners maxing out drag the mean up). By ages 55–64 the median is ~$107K against Fidelity's 10x-salary guideline — the typical person has about a tenth of the target.
- **But the doom is overstated by what the data excludes.** These are 401k balances only — no IRAs, brokerage accounts, home equity, or spouse's savings. And Fidelity's multiples assume the portfolio replaces income alone, ignoring Social Security (average payment ≈ $2,000/month). The portfolio only has to bridge the gap between Social Security and annual expenses.
- **Contribution rates barely move across a lifetime** (8–10.3% under 25 → 12.8–14.3% at 55–64). Habits set at the first job persist for decades — which is exactly why the enrollment default matters so much.
- **Time is the multiplier.** $50K in a 401k at age 25, never touched again, grows to ~$1.086M by 65; the same $50K at 34 grows to ~$543K. A 2–3% savings-rate increase at 38 compounds harder than scrambling at 55.
- **Cashing out on a job change is the classic leak**: roughly 51% of people in their 20s and 43% in their 30s cash out their 401k when leaving a job.
- **Don't treat the 401k like an ATM**: ~13% of plan participants have a loan outstanding, and leaving (or losing) the job with the loan unpaid can convert it into a withdrawal — income tax plus the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Borrow from anything but the 401k.
- **New for 2026 — the high-earner Roth catch-up rule**: if you earned over $150K in the prior calendar year, *all* catch-up contributions to a workplace plan (401k, 403(b), 457(b)) at age 50+ must be made as Roth, in after-tax dollars. No upfront deduction anymore, but most employers now offer the Roth option.
- **Retirement math anchor**: know your annual spend, then target ~25× annual expenses — the [[4% Rule]] — so a $50K/year lifestyle needs a $1.25M portfolio to draw $50K indefinitely.
- **Endgame rules (65+)**: have an explicit withdrawal plan; don't forget [[Required Minimum Distributions|RMDs]] from age 73 on traditional 401ks/IRAs (Roth accounts are exempt); and stay partially invested — 100% cash at 65 feels safe but fails if you live to 100.

## High-Yield Assets
**Vanguard 2026 balances by age** (average / median):

| Age | Average | Median |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 25 | $7,259 (from $6,899) | $2,234 (from $1,948) |
| 25–34 | $50,261 | $18,732 |
| 35–44 | $120,000 (from $103,000) | $46,919 (from $39,958) |
| 45–54 | $214,991 | $78,730 |
| 55–64 | $305,006 | $107,269 |
| 65+ | $330,186 | $103,202 (dips — withdrawals begin) |

- **Contribution rates (voluntary / automatic)**: under 25 → 8% / 10.3%; 25–34 → 9.9% / 11.9%; 35–44 → 10.6–12.3% (flat YoY); 45–54 → 11.2% / 12.8%; 55–64 → 12.8–14.3%.
- **Fidelity savings milestones**: 3× salary by 40, 7× by 55, 10× by 65.
- **2026 contribution limits**: standard 401k $24.5K; age 50+ catch-up +$8K → $32.5K total; ages 60–63 "super catch-up" of $11,250 → $35,750 total (a four-year window: 60, 61, 62, 63; same >$150K-must-be-Roth rule applies).
- **Social Security timing**: claim at 62 → 70% of full benefit; delay to 70 → 124%. Average payment ≈ $2,000/month.
- **Compounding benchmarks**: $50K at 25 → ~$1.086M at 65; $50K at 34 → ~$543K.
- **Auto vs voluntary enrollment at year 10+**: $220K vs $133K.
- **Default fund pick**: an S&P 500 fund is a good starting point if the plan offers it — but check the allocation actually fits your risk profile.
- **Priority order**: emergency fund + high-interest debt first → 401k to the full employer match → Roth 401k if offered (young = lower bracket now, 35–40 years of tax-free growth) → your own retirement before the kids' 529 plan.

## Case Studies / Anecdotes
- **The oxygen-mask rule for parents**: fund your own 401k before the kids' 529 — "nobody is going to lend you money to retire comfortably," but student loans exist for college.
- **The three-trait employee**: Yang's favorite finding — someone can lack planning skills, freeze on complex decisions, *and* procrastinate, and auto-enrollment quietly fixes all three without the person changing at all.
- **The 4% rule worked example**: $50K annual expenses × 25 = $1.25M portfolio → withdraw $50K/year without technically running out.

## The "So What?" Factor
Automate the decision away from yourself: enroll (or confirm you're enrolled) today, capture the full match, and set contribution increases to trigger automatically with every raise until you hit a ~15% savings rate. Benchmark your balance against the *median* for your bracket and Fidelity's multiple for your age; if you're behind at 35–44, raise the rate now rather than at 55 — and never cash out or borrow against the account on a job change. Over 50 (and especially 60–63), turn on catch-up contributions, remembering the new 2026 Roth requirement if you earn over $150K.
