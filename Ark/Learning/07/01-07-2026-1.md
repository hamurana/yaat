**The Hook & Core Thesis**

Modern home design quietly deleted features that made houses genuinely livable — not because they were ugly, but because they cost money and didn't photograph well. Bringing them back, even in updated form, is almost always worth it.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **Open-concept killed the transition space.** Front doors now open straight into great rooms, so outdoor chaos has nowhere to land — you can have the open feel *and* a small closed [[mudroom]].
- **The kitchen island is a snack bar, not a dining room.** It replaced dedicated eating space with hunched-over bar-stool meals nobody lingers at.
- **Kitchens are now designed for looks over storage.** Flat-front cabinets and open shelving photograph well but delete the [[pantry]] — an enormous amount of real storage.
- **Ceiling height should be intentional, room by room.** Modern builds go either box-flat 8ft everywhere or one grand vaulted room that wrecks acoustics and heating bills.
- **Laundry got squeezed into closets.** When laundry has no room of its own, its mess bleeds into every other space.
- **Small architectural features do heavy lifting.** Pocket doors, built-ins, transoms, a separate toilet room, and a screened porch each solve a real daily problem for little cost during a renovation.
- **A good renovation chases function, not trends.** "You're not living in a photo, you're living in a house."

---
**High-Yield Assets**

The 10 forgotten features, each kept specific:

1. **The real [[mudroom]]** — not a single hook and basket, but a buffer zone with bench, closed storage, and a coat closet. Even **6ft deep by 8ft wide**, closed off with a door or partial wall, changes daily life. Standard in homes built **before the 1970s**.
2. **The formal dining room** — a table seating **six to eight** plus a buffet/sideboard. Fair criticism was the room used twice a year; the fix was never "kill dedicated eating space entirely."
3. **The [[pantry]]** — a walk-in or even a **5ft-deep reach-in closet** with adjustable shelving beats maxed-out drawers. Renovation rule: if a client wants to remove a back wall, first ask what's behind it — if it's a pantry, "we are having a long conversation."
4. **The [[pocket door]]** — slides into the wall, eats no floor space; functionally superior to a swing door in small baths, laundries, offices, and closets. Fell out of fashion because cheap construction made them rattle. Superior to a barn door (gap at the wall edge, noise, industrial look that dates fast).
5. **Built-ins** — window seats with storage, bookcases flanking a fireplace, hallway linen closets, breakfast-nook bench seating. Common in **craftsman and colonial** homes. "Built-ins feel settled because they are settled."
6. **Ceiling-height variation** — lower coved ceilings in bedrooms for coziness, higher in living/dining for expansiveness, plus tray and coffered ceilings for depth. A massive vaulted room leaves warm air sitting **20 feet** overhead.
7. **The proper utility room** — with room to move, a **utility sink**, cleaning storage, space for an extra fridge/freezer, and a folding surface that isn't your bed. The sink also handles muddy dogs, paint brushes, and mop buckets.
8. **Interior transoms** — small horizontal windows above doors, common **early-to-mid 20th century**. Move light and air through the house without sacrificing privacy; light up hallways that would otherwise be dark tunnels; enabled cross-ventilation before AC.
9. **The separate toilet room / water closet** — the toilet in its own enclosed room, separate from tub/shower/vanity. Common in **older and European-style** homes. Two people can use the master bath at once, and odors stay contained.
10. **The sleeping porch** — a screened outdoor room, common in warmer climates in homes built roughly **1900–1950** where people slept in summer before AC. Modernised as a covered, screened, potentially heated outdoor room with real flooring and ceiling fans — one of the higher-return renovation investments, though it doesn't add taxable interior square footage.

**Case Studies / Anecdotes**

- **The speaker's credentials:** **[[Jerry Davis]]** — over two decades in residential construction, renovation, and design consulting, having walked through hundreds of new builds, flips, gut renovations, and custom builds.
- **The repeating pattern he sees:** homeowners chase a look from a design blog or home-improvement show and trade away the things that would have made the home "10 times better to actually live in."
- **The open-concept mess:** families stacking stuff against walls, dropping bags on dining chairs, piling shoes by the door "until it looks like a garage" — then renovating and *not* fixing it because they want the open feel.
- **The pocket-door reaction:** in older homes where every bathroom had one, every client using it for the first time says the same thing — "Why don't all doors do this?"

---
**The "So What?" Factor**

Before you sit down with a contractor or designer, take a screenshot of this list — these features are exactly what gets cut from plans to save a few thousand dollars, and almost every homeowner regrets it later. Renovate for how the house *works* every day, not for how it photographs. The more your house works for you, the less you'll ever want to leave it.
