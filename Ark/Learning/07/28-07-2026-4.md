**The Hook & Core Thesis**

A mirror only shows whether an outfit looks good standing still — eight ordinary real-life movements (sitting, walking through a doorway, shaking a hand, reaching into a pocket, buttoning a collar, climbing stairs, a photograph, and which clothes you actually reach for) reveal whether it truly fits, and stylish men over 60 test for that instead of trusting the mirror.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **The chair test (trousers).** A waistband can look fine standing but cut into the stomach, flare the pockets, or pull across the thighs the moment you sit. Fix: a medium-to-slightly-higher rise, with the waistband secure but not squeezing and enough room through the seat and thighs to sit without pulling.
- **The doorway test (jacket shoulders).** The shoulder seam is what people register first, before brand or price, and it's also the hardest part of a jacket to alter — a tailor can fix a waist far more easily than a shoulder. The seam should end at the natural edge of the shoulder with the sleeve falling cleanly, not collapsing or bulging.
- **The handshake test (sleeve length).** Extending a hand puts the sleeve-cuff-watch line on full display. The jacket sleeve should end near the wrist bone with about a quarter-to-half inch of shirt cuff showing; the shirt sleeve itself should reach the base of the thumb.
- **The pocket test (jacket body).** Slipping a hand into a trouser pocket shifts your shoulders, waist, and hips — a correctly fitted jacket adjusts with you. Too tight shows diagonal pull lines across the stomach; too loose lets the whole jacket shift and drop off one shoulder.
- **The collar test.** The area closest to the face gets the least attention but changes the whole read of an outfit. A properly fitted shirt collar allows one finger to slide between it and the neck; the jacket collar should sit flush against the shirt collar without a visible gap (known as "collar gap").
- **The staircase test (trouser movement).** Climbing stairs exposes what standing never will — tight trousers pull sharply above the knee, loose ones swing and gather behind the knee. The fix isn't "slim vs. loose" but moderate room through the seat and thighs with a gentle taper below the knee, and a slight break where the front just touches the shoe.
- **The camera test (proportions).** A camera shows the whole structure a mirror hides at close range — a jacket that's too short, trousers sitting too low, or a shirt hanging too far past the waist can each quietly shorten the legs and add visual weight, even when no single piece is "wrong" on its own.
- **The closet test (what you actually reach for).** The clothes pushed to the back or left on the hanger are usually rejected by your body, not your taste — you unconsciously avoid the collar that tightens after an hour or the waistband that needs constant adjusting. Compare your three most-worn pieces against three you avoid to find your personal fit pattern, and use that pattern instead of the size printed on the label.

**High-Yield Assets**

- **Eight-test framework**: chair (trousers sitting), doorway (jacket shoulders), handshake (sleeve length), pocket reach (jacket body), collar (shirt + jacket collar gap), staircase (trouser movement), camera (whole-outfit proportions), closet habit (which pieces you actually wear).
- **Sleeve-cuff rule**: jacket sleeve ends near the wrist bone, leaving about ¼–½ inch of shirt cuff visible.
- **Collar-fit rule**: one finger should slide comfortably between a buttoned shirt collar and the neck.
- **Photograph technique**: take front, side, and walking shots with the camera at waist/chest height (not from above, which distorts proportions), and judge the largest shapes — jacket division, waistband height, trouser-hem-to-shoe line — before looking at small details like watch or pocket square.

**The "So What?" Factor**

Before buying anything new, run what you already own through these tests: sit down, walk through a doorway, shake a hand, reach into a pocket, button the collar, climb stairs, and photograph the full outfit. A close-but-imperfect piece is often a cheap tailoring fix — sleeve length, trouser break, waist shape. But if the shoulders are wrong or the proportions fight your body no matter what you do, let it go rather than trying to make it work.
