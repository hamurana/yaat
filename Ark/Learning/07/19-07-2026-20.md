## The Hook & Core Thesis
Most people jump into the market with zero strategy — guessing, copying, or relying on vibes (buying a stock because someone on Twitter said it was going "to the moon"). Experienced investors instead use one or a mix of three core strategies: **[[fundamental analysis]]**, **[[technical analysis]]**, and **[[macro analysis]]**. Each answers a different question — *what is it worth, where is the price going,* and *what is the whole economy doing.* (Explicit disclaimer: not financial advice.)

## Key Arguments Checklist
- **Fundamental analysis = a company's true worth**, for long-term investors; the method Warren Buffett and Peter Lynch rely on.
- **Technical analysis = reading price and volume on charts**; easy and visual, but tells you *what* not *why*.
- **Macro analysis = the big-picture economy**; best for broad instruments like index funds and ETFs, blind to individual companies.
- **Each has real limits** — fundamentals can be manipulated (Enron), technical patterns fake out, and macro ignores company detail — which is why serious investors mix them.

## High-Yield Assets (the three strategies)
**1. Fundamental analysis** — figuring out what a company is actually worth, not what the price says ("I invest in companies, not stocks"; Buffett: "never invest in a business you cannot understand"). Two inputs:
  - **Quantitative** (the numbers) — from the three financial statements: **income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement**. Focus areas: how much money the company makes, how much debt it carries, how well it invests in its future.
  - **Qualitative** (the un-numberable) — **brand strength** (well-known and trusted?), **leadership** (experienced and honest management?), **competitive advantage** (something unique others can't copy — a moat), and **industry trends** (growing or shrinking market?).
  - *Example — Netflix*: even if subscriber growth and earnings soften, it retains a trusted brand, a huge content library, and constant experimentation/global reach.
  - **Advantages**: reveals true value; ideal for long-term investing (overall health, not short-term swings); grounded in real numbers (revenue, earnings, cash flow).
  - **Limitations**: qualitative judgment is subjective; relies on past performance and predictions that can be wrong; companies can **manipulate numbers or hide bad news** (**Enron** looked solid until it collapsed).

**2. Technical analysis** — reading charts rather than reports; focus on **price, volume, and patterns**.
  - **Price** = the up/down movement; **volume** = shares traded, "how much energy is behind a move."
  - **Trends**: an **uptrend** makes new highs and falls less in between; a **downtrend** keeps dropping and struggles to bounce.
  - **Support** = a "floor" where buyers step in and price bounces; **resistance** = a "ceiling" where sellers take profits and price pulls back.
  - **Breakout** = price breaking above resistance or below support, possibly starting a new trend; a **fakeout** is a breakout that snaps back and traps early traders.
  - **Advantages**: relatively easy and visual; quick to learn.
  - **Limitations**: shows *what* price is doing, not *why*; ignores fundamentals and news; patterns don't always play out; relies on past data assuming history repeats, while markets are unpredictable.

**3. Macro analysis** — zooming out to the whole economy: government policy, global events, inflation, economic health.
  - **Monetary policy** (central banks like the **Federal Reserve**): mainly via **interest rates** — low rates make borrowing cheaper, businesses expand, people spend, markets tend to rise; rate hikes to fight inflation slow borrowing/spending and usually hit stocks.
  - **Fiscal policy** (government spending and taxes): more spending gives a short-term push; high taxes or spending cuts slow things down.
  - **Key indicators**: **GDP** (economic growth), **unemployment rate**, **consumer confidence**, **manufacturing reports** (factory activity). Track them free on an **economic calendar** (e.g. investing.com).
  - **Geopolitics**: wars, trade tensions, tariffs, even elections move markets on expectations.
  - **Advantages**: shows the big picture; great for index funds/ETFs and picking strong markets/regions.
  - **Limitations**: ignores individual-company detail like earnings and financial health.

## Case Studies / Anecdotes
- **The Twitter "to the moon" buy** that crashes right after — the opening hook for investing without a strategy.
- **Netflix** as the qualitative-strength example (brand and library outlasting a soft quarter) and **Enron** as the fundamentals-can-lie warning (looked solid until it fell apart).

## The "So What?" Factor
Stop trading on vibes and pick a lens that matches your goal: **fundamental analysis** for long-term ownership of individual companies (read the financial statements, judge the moat and management), **technical analysis** for timing entries/exits by price and volume (mind fakeouts), and **macro analysis** for broad index/ETF positioning (watch rates, GDP, and the economic calendar). Best of all, combine them — each covers the others' blind spots.
