**The Hook & Core Thesis**

Many elderly patients are on medications nobody has reviewed in years — the original reason has passed and the drug now quietly does more harm than good. For seven common medications, anyone over ~65 taking them long-term should ask their doctor one question: *do the benefits still outweigh the risks now they're older?* (Education, not personal medical advice — never stop these drugs on your own.)

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **Older bodies handle drugs differently.** Kidneys and liver clear them slower (so doses build up), the brain is more sensitive to sedation, and multiple meds interact and stack.
- **The recurring danger is falls.** A drug that makes a young person slightly drowsy or dizzy can put an older person on the floor — a broken hip, cracked skull, or brain bleed that begins a slide into hospital, immobility, and dependence.
- **The harm is silent and cumulative.** Nobody connects the slow decline back to the tablets.
- **There's an official reference: the [[Beers criteria]].** A list of medications to use with extra caution in the over-65s; nearly all seven sit within it.
- **The fix is a [[medication review]] and [[deprescribing]].** Not fewer pills for their own sake — the right tablets, right dose, right person.
- **Do not stop suddenly.** Several cause dangerous withdrawal or rebound; taper only under a doctor/pharmacist.

---
**High-Yield Assets**

The seven medications, kept specific:

1. **["Z-drugs"] sleeping tablets** — **[[zopiclone]]**, **[[zolpidem]]** (sold as **Ambien** in the US). Handed out freely for poor sleep, but in older people risk falls, fractures, daytime confusion, and dependence — while research shows they add only a *small* amount of extra sleep. Modest benefit vs. serious risk.
2. **[[Benzodiazepines]] ("benzos")** — **[[diazepam]]**, **[[lorazepam]]** (US: **Xanax**). Prescribed for anxiety and sometimes sleep; can carry someone through a genuine crisis. In older bodies: heavy sedation, memory problems, unsteadiness, falls. Among the most dependence-forming drugs — dangerous to stop suddenly; taper slowly with a doctor.
3. **Older anti-histamine sleep aids** — **Benadryl**, **Nytol**, built on **[[diphenhydramine]]**. Assumed safe because they're over-the-counter, but have **[[anticholinergic effects]]**: confusion, blurred vision, constipation, falls — and long-term use is *linked in studies to higher dementia risk*.
4. **[[Hydrochlorothiazide]] (HCTZ)** — a blood-pressure "water tablet" (diuretic). Can tip older people into dehydration and pull salts out of balance (low potassium, low sodium) → dizziness, weakness, falls. Especially risky during illness, hot weather, or low fluid intake. Safer alternatives exist; needs monitoring if taken long-term.
5. **[[Gabapentin]] and [[pregabalin]]** (US: **Neurontin**, **Lyrica**) — for nerve/chronic pain. In older people: dizziness, drowsiness, mental fog, unsteadiness. Increasingly prescribed for pain they may not even be touching — worth asking whether the relief is real or the person is just being sedated. Combined with other calming meds, can dangerously slow breathing.
6. **[[Proton pump inhibitors]] (PPIs)** — **[[omeprazole]]**, **[[lansoprazole]]**, **[[pantoprazole]]** — stomach-acid suppressants for heartburn/reflux. Not a dramatic danger; the problem is being started as a short course and carried on for years/decades unreviewed. Long-term links: low **vitamin B12**, low **magnesium**, weaker bones and more fractures.
7. **[[NSAIDs]]** — non-steroidal anti-inflammatory painkillers: **ibuprofen** (US: **Advil**), **naproxen**, **diclofenac**. Treated as harmless and sold everywhere, but taken regularly by older people it's one of the riskiest: stomach bleeding, kidney injury (worse if dehydrated or on other kidney-affecting drugs), and raised blood pressure → higher heart-attack and stroke risk. Occasional dose usually fine; more than a week for general aches → ask about something safer.

**Concept — [[deprescribing]]:** the proper term for safely stopping medications no longer earning their place; done by tapering the dose down gradually under supervision.

---
**Case Studies / Anecdotes**

- **The speaker:** **[[Dr Alex]]**, ten years in emergency medicine, who sees "a handful" of elderly patients weekly on medications they shouldn't be taking, unreviewed for years.
- **PPIs for decades:** he meets many people who've taken acid tablets for as long as they can remember, never asked whether they're still needed.
- **Rebound example:** stopping **lansoprazole** suddenly often causes *more* acid reflux than at the start, because the body needs time to wind down.

---
**The "So What?" Factor**

Don't panic and don't stop anything alone — these aren't emergencies. Book a **[[medication review]]** with your doctor or pharmacist and go through every tablet one by one, asking for each: *is this still needed at my age, and do the benefits still outweigh the risks?* Then taper anything unnecessary down gradually under supervision. Given the range covered — sleep, anxiety, blood pressure, nerve pain, reflux, everyday pain — there's a good chance you or someone you love is on at least one right now.
