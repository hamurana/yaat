## The Hook & Core Thesis
The stock price on your screen is one of the *least* important things about investing. [[Warren Buffett]]: "price is what you pay, value is what you get." His entire edge over six decades is valuing businesses by their **[[intrinsic value]]** — the present value of all cash a business will produce over its life — and buying only with a **[[margin of safety]]** below that value. The video walks a full **discounted cash flow (DCF)** calculation on a fictional company. (Disclaimer: not financial advice.)

## Key Arguments Checklist
- **Buy the business, not the ticker.** "If you wouldn't buy the entire business, you shouldn't buy the stock" — a share is a piece of a real company with employees, customers, and cash flow.
- **Ignore Mr. Market's mood.** [[Benjamin Graham]]'s **Mr. Market** shows up daily offering emotional prices (euphoric or depressed); don't let him tell you what a business is worth.
- **Intrinsic value is an estimate, a range — not a precise number** — built on assumptions about future cash.
- **The analyst's job is to turn a stock into a bond.** A bond prints its cash flows; a stock doesn't, so you must estimate them and discount them back to today (DCF).
- **Never buy at intrinsic value — demand a margin of safety** (Buffett might require a 30–40% discount) because the future is uncertain.

## High-Yield Assets
- **Buffett's definition of intrinsic value**: "the present value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life" — the number you'd get if you were all-knowing about every dollar of cash from now until judgment day, **discounted at the proper rate**. Three ideas inside it: (1) all future cash, (2) *when* it arrives, (3) discounting it back to today.
- **The stock-to-bond framing**: on a bond the cash flows are printed (how much, when, principal back); on a stock they aren't — the analyst "prints" them onto the certificate via DCF.
- **The 7-step DCF (worked on fictional "Harbor Tools" — a boring, stable, predictable business, the kind Buffett likes)**:
  1. **Find owner cash.** Buffett cares about **owner earnings** = net income + non-cash charges − *maintenance* capex. Problem: companies report *total* capex, not the maintenance portion. Practical conservative fix: treat **all** capex as maintenance → **Free Cash Flow = operating cash flow − total capex**. This may understate cash to owners but reduces the risk of overstating value. *Assume FCF = $120M/year.*
  2. **Estimate growth conservatively.** Buffett avoids optimism; assume a mature business growing with the economy at **4%/year**.
  3. **Project cash flows** for **10 years**.
  4. **Choose the discount rate** — the return you require to justify laying out money today (Buffett often uses long-term interest rates or a reasonable equity return). *Assume 10%.*
  5. **Discount each year's cash flow to present value** — a future dollar is worth less than today's, so present values shrink the further out they are (even as the nominal flows grow).
  6. **Calculate terminal value** — the business doesn't vanish after year 10. Grow year-10 FCF (~**$171M**) by a small steady **2%** terminal rate and divide by (discount rate − terminal growth) = **10% − 2%**, giving a terminal value ≈ **$2.18B**; discount that back 10 years at 10% → present value ≈ **$924M**.
  7. **Add it all up** — PV of the 10 years of cash flows + PV of terminal value ≈ **$1.75B intrinsic value**.
- **Compare to market cap**: at **$2B** it's overvalued; at **$1.75B** fairly valued; at **$1.1B** now it's interesting. This comparison is the whole point of valuation.
- **Margin of safety**: Buffett never buys at intrinsic value — a 30–40% discount protects him if he's wrong and boosts returns if he's right, because estimates are never perfect and reality rarely unfolds as expected.

## Case Studies / Anecdotes
- **Harbor Tools** — the fictional "boring, stable" company used to demonstrate every DCF step end-to-end, from $120M FCF to a $1.75B intrinsic value and the buy/hold/pass thresholds.
- **Buffett & Berkshire Hathaway** — six decades of beating the market "not by trading or guessing, but by valuing businesses," as the credibility anchor for the whole method.

## The "So What?" Factor
Value a company by estimating its future free cash flow, projecting ~10 years, discounting it (plus a terminal value) back to today at your required return, and summing to an intrinsic value — then only buy well below that number for a margin of safety. But note the closing caveat: today most stocks are priced on sentiment and growth hype rather than real value, so genuinely undervalued opportunities are rare and finding them takes patience, careful research, and discipline.
