## The Hook & Core Thesis
Younger generations have **~86% less purchasing power** than baby boomers did in their 20s — it's not avocado toast or TikTok, it's the data: homes, education, cars, and living costs have massively outpaced wages. The video proves the financial squeeze is real, explains the demographic causes, then gives concrete rules to still build wealth from a worse starting position.

## Key Arguments Checklist
- **Housing is the biggest driver.** The home-price-to-income ratio has blown past even the 2006–08 bubble; homes now cost far more relative to income than they did for boomers.
- **Home prices have outpaced inflation for decades**, especially the last ~10 years — so it's not just general inflation, housing specifically has ballooned.
- **The causes are structural/demographic**: less competition and earlier dual-income marriages for boomers vs. delayed marriage, exploding population, and a $1.7T student-debt crisis for the young.
- **You can still win with discipline**: budget by fixed ratios, invest regularly (Roth IRA), and increase income by job-hopping.

## High-Yield Assets
- **The home-price-to-income ratio** (years of median income to buy the median home outright — a benchmark, not literal): **2022** median home ~**$433,100**, median household income ~**$70,000** → ratio **~6.1**. **1988** (when the average boomer turned 33): median home ~$110,000, median boomer income ~$27,000 → ratio **~4**. Historically a US home cost ~**5×** yearly income; the 2006–08 bubble hit **7**; today's ratio is *past* bubble levels. **UK is worse** — consistently over 7 for a decade, lately over 9.
- **Case-Shiller vs. income**: median household income (black) stayed above the home-price index (red) from ~1955 to ~2005 (good — housing manageable); the red crossed over pre-2008, fell back after the 2010 crash, and has since shot far above income again.
- **Inflation vs. home prices** (Bureau of Labor Statistics): $100,000 spent on a **home** in 1990 ≈ **$377,000** today, but $100,000 of general **goods/services** in 1990 ≈ **$231,000** today — homes have outpaced inflation by a wide margin.
- **Why boomers had it easier (demographics)**: (1) less competition — far fewer people in the 1970s–80s, so lower housing demand; (2) **earlier marriage → dual-income households younger**, making homes affordable. Today, per **Pew Research Center**, ~45% of millennials *don't* live in their own family household (only ~55% do, vs. ~69% prior generations and ~85% for the silent generation).
- **Education**: tuition (private *or* public) now ~$30,000–50,000/year; **student debt is a $1.7 trillion crisis**; over 1991–2021 average tuition more than doubled; average loan balance at graduation **tripled** (from ~$10,000 to ~$30,000); ~7% of borrowers owe over $100,000. Meanwhile graduates have risen **250% since 1970**, so more competition for the same jobs — "entry-level jobs are no longer entry-level," and average graduate starting salary is ~$50,000–60,000.
- **Cost of living**: Gen Z/millennials pay ~40–60% more for gas than boomers did in their 20s. **Cars** are a major drag — average car payment **$716/month**, average loan term ~69 months, with auto- and credit-card debt at all-time highs. **73% of millennials and 66% of Gen Z live paycheck to paycheck.**

## The Fixes (budget ratios + wealth-building)
- **The 30% rule for housing**: rent (or mortgage + insurance + taxes) should be ≤30% of gross income (e.g. $50K/year → ~$1,250/month rent) — leaving room for other expenses; hard in coastal cities like Seattle/Boston, so watch other costs if you exceed it.
- **The 10% rule for cars**: keep car payments under 10% of gross monthly income. Auto-loan rates are ~8–13% now, so **pay a car off fully if you can**.
- **Invest regularly, starting with a Roth IRA** (earnings grow tax-free; contribution limit $6,500/year at filming). Compounded ~40 years at the average market return: **$250/month → ~$904,000**; **$400/month → ~$1.44M**; maxed out → nearly **$2M**.
- **Increase income by job-hopping**: per the **Wall Street Journal**, switchers out-earn stayers — often 10–30% more per move. His Silicon Valley friends stay 1–2 years, switch (even laterally by title) for a raise, and repeat, reaching much higher positions and pay within a decade.

## Case Studies / Anecdotes
- **The boomer-vs-millennial home math** (ratio 4 in 1988 vs. 6.1 in 2022; some 1980s high earners paid off a house in 1–2 years of wages) — the concrete backbone of the "it's not laziness" argument.
- **The tech job-hoppers** in Silicon Valley — the real-world illustration that repeated switching, even lateral, compounds into far higher compensation and skills over ten years.

## The "So What?" Factor
The squeeze is real and mostly structural, not a personal failing — but you can still get ahead from a worse starting line. Cap housing at **30%** of gross income and cars at **10%**, pay off high-rate auto loans, and funnel the savings into a **Roth IRA** every month (even $250 compounds to ~$900K over 40 years). Then grow income deliberately by **switching jobs** every year or two rather than waiting for raises. Discipline on the ratios plus consistent investing and rising income is the path to matching — or beating — the previous generation.
