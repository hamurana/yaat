## The Hook & Core Thesis
Tennis whites were the world's "first athleisure" — but a century ago the all-white kit was a class *status symbol*, not a performance choice. The Gentleman's Gazette team traces how tennis dress went from rigid wool formality to functional modern sportswear, then field-tests the old outfits to prove they look 10/10 but are hopelessly impractical.

## Key Arguments Checklist
- **Tennis began as an upper-class pastime split along class lines.** At the turn of the 20th century it was played by the wealthy at private clubs and country houses with manicured lawns — grown out of lawn tennis and tied to Victorian ideas of leisure, etiquette, and social status. It doubled as a social occasion.
- **The white dress code was class signaling, not practicality.** White was a "visual marker of privilege": keeping white clothing clean before household washing machines/laundromats required either all-day labor or the money to pay servants. Working-class ("blue collar") people wouldn't wear white — it dirtied too easily.
- **Early men's tennis wear was formal and rigid.** White flannel trousers (long), long-sleeved shirts, sometimes jackets or sweaters. Showing too much skin broke etiquette — a short-sleeved shirt would have been "the equivalent of wearing underwear on the court." Plus tennis blazers and even hats.
- **The kit loosened as the sport got more athletic.** Through the 1900s: lighter, less-structured shirts, sometimes shorter sleeves. Sweaters were reportedly worn "for warmth between matches" — which the hosts find absurd given how hot it already is (evidence it was barely athletic).
- **Mid-century → functional.** Shorts became a staple once people realized long woolen flannels were wrong for tennis; colors softened; cuts allowed more movement; synthetic "miracle fibers" that dried faster arrived — yet **white remained the color** regardless of material.
- **The white code broke in the 1970s.** Tournaments abandoned strict white; television, sponsorship, and brand identity encouraged color and bolder styling, and modern synthetics enabled bold colors, stripes, and motifs.
- **Wimbledon is the holdout.** To this day it keeps the all-white rule — the British love of tradition.
- **The field-test verdict:** stylistically a 10/10, but practically you "sweat your ass off." Great for looking the part, nothing more.

## High-Yield Assets
- **[[René Lacoste]]** brought the polo shirt into the mainstream; his famous crocodile motif first appeared on his **blazer**, not the polo shirt.
- **Wimbledon's official outfitter is [[Ralph Lauren]]** — notable as an *American* brand (rather than a British house like Hackett). Ralph Lauren's Wimbledon look: pleated pants in a lighter cotton, the club colors (**purple and green**), and a bold-stripe tennis blazer.
- **The 1970s inflection point** — TV, sponsorship, and brand identity plus synthetic fibers ended pure white on most courts.
- **Fabric comparison from the on-court test (75°F / ~25°C, 65% humidity):**
  - **Poplin cotton shirt** — showed clear sweat stains all over the front and back.
  - **Piqué cotton** [transcribed "picanet"] — sweat noticeably *less* visible; the textured weave seems to hold the fabric slightly off the skin.
  - **A knit sweater layer** hid sweat stains — but is itself brutally hot.
  - **Full 100% wool flannel outfit** with wool tennis sweater — "absolutely bonkers," hot, though reportedly not dramatically hotter than the cotton version.
- **Period footwear:** **PF Flyers** canvas shoes, popular at the time.
- **Styling tip demonstrated:** a reversible summer belt doing "double duty" as a tennis belt (white side for the court).

## Case Studies / Anecdotes
- **The on-court experiment:** hosts **Jack**, **Raphael**, and **Tom** play actual games in traditional whites to test the "does white hide sweat" theory. Result: the sweater-wearer's stains stayed hidden (because of the sweater, not the white), while the plain cotton shirt showed clear stains front and back — debunking the idea that white itself was practical.
- **The closing line:** "that was good fun, but please, let's never do this again — maybe with shorts. And a short-sleeve shirt." — the practical takeaway delivered as a joke.

## The "So What?" Factor
Wimbledon's all-white tradition is a fossil of Edwardian class signaling, not sports science — the whites were about who could afford to keep them clean. If you want the heritage-tennis *look* (Lacoste polos, a striped blazer, pleated cottons in club colors), wear it knowing it's costume, not performance gear: choose textured piqué over flat poplin to disguise sweat, skip the wool flannels entirely, and save the long sleeves and sweaters for the clubhouse, not the court.
