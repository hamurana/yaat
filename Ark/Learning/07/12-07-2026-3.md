## The Hook & Core Thesis
The Hollywood image — a room of guys in suits screaming "buy, buy, buy" — is not where investment banks make their fortune. Less than half of [[Goldman Sachs]]' $53B+ 2024 revenue came from trading; the biggest, steadiest, most boring-sounding money comes from advising on mergers and managing rich people's money. Four distinct engines drive it, and a four-part moat makes the business almost impossible to disrupt.

## Key Arguments Checklist
- **Banks barely trade with their own money anymore.** After the 2008 crisis, regulators — in the US via the [[Volcker Rule]] — cracked down on big speculative bets. Today when a bank trades it's almost always on behalf of a client (pension fund, corporation, asset manager), not betting its own cash on market direction.
- **Engine 1 — [[M&A advisory]]:** The classic banker job. When one company buys another, the bank values the target, negotiates terms, structures the deal for tax/legal safety, and holds the client's hand through months of back-and-forth. The fee is a **percentage of deal size that shrinks as the deal grows** — and it's a "winner keeps winning" business: advise one major deal and you get the call for the next, which is why the same handful of names dominate.
- **Engine 2 — [[underwriting]]:** Helping a company raise money via an [[IPO]] (selling stock publicly for the first time) or issuing bonds. The under-appreciated part: the bank often **buys the shares from the company first at an agreed price, then resells them** — profiting on the spread if it goes well, but stuck holding unwanted shares (real balance-sheet risk) if it goes badly.
- **Engine 3 — trading & [[market making]]:** Not directional betting — it's being the airport currency-exchange booth: always ready to buy or sell, profiting from the tiny bid-ask spread repeated thousands of times a day. When a pension fund must offload $500M of stock without crashing the price, the bank's desk absorbs it for the spread plus fees. **Small margin, enormous scale — a volume business.**
- **Engine 4 — [[asset & wealth management]]:** The least flashy and arguably smartest model because it's **recurring**. An annual fee (~0.5–1% of assets) charged whether markets rise or fall. Unlike one-time M&A fees (deal closes, bank must go find the next), wealth-management fees recur year after year "like a subscription."
- **The real moat is four things no clever app can replicate — relationships, distribution, balance sheet, trust:**
  - **Relationships** — CEOs/CFOs hire the banker who's known their company for 15 years and can be trusted with secrets, not a random bank off a search.
  - **Distribution** — selling $2B of new stock means actually finding institutional buyers willing to write enormous checks fast; big banks have direct lines to thousands of them.
  - **Balance sheet** — underwriting sometimes means buying shares first with tens of billions of the bank's own capital sitting ready to absorb risk.
  - **Trust** — built deal by deal; a single leak or botched IPO can destroy it overnight.
- **Investment bank ≠ commercial bank ≠ hedge fund.** A **commercial bank** takes deposits and lends mortgages/car loans (Bank of America, Chase in their retail role). A **hedge fund** takes wealthy/institutional money and actively bets on markets (closest to the "gambling" image). An **investment bank** does neither at its core — it's a *facilitator* for deals, fundraising, trading flow, and managing client money. Blended firms like [[JP Morgan]] (commercial + investment under one roof) add to the confusion. This matters because critics attacking banks for "reckless gambling" are usually describing hedge-fund behaviour or pre-2008 practices that no longer exist in the same form.

## High-Yield Assets
- **Goldman Sachs 2024**: over **$53B** total revenue; less than half from trading.
- **M&A fee scale**: small deals (<$10M) → **5–10%**; billion-dollar-plus mega-deals → roughly **0.5–1.5%**. (1% of $1B = $10M for one deal.) Bank often also arranges the buyer's financing for a *separate* fee.
- **Underwriting fees**: IPOs **3–7%** of the amount raised; bond issuances usually **under 2%** (bonds are safer, more standardized).
- **Goldman rankings/figures**: ranked #1 globally in announced & completed M&A in 2025; trading + market-making + IB-fees division ≈ **$35B in 2024** (record results in equities); asset & wealth management division alone **>$16B in 2024**, managing **>$3 trillion** in assets.
- **SpaceX IPO (as stated in the video)**: went public on the NASDAQ **June 12, 2026** at **$135/share**, raising ~**$75B** — described as the **largest IPO in history**, valuing the company at ~**$1.75 trillion**. 10 banks shared the underwriting, led by Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, with JP Morgan, Citigroup, Barclays, and UBS rounding out the syndicate. *[Forward-looking figure asserted by the speaker — preserved as-stated.]*
- **Concrete scale illustration**: a $10B acquisition at ~0.75% = **$75M** for one transaction (6–12 months), earned by a team of ~20–40 bankers ≈ 1,200+ years of the ~$60k US median salary — and often just one of several fees.

## The "So What?" Factor
Stop picturing investment banking as trading-floor gambling — that's the movie version (*The Wolf of Wall Street*, *The Big Short*) and largely a pre-2008 relic. The money is in **advice and access**: M&A advisory, underwriting, market-making spreads, and recurring wealth-management fees. Understanding the four-part moat (relationships, distribution, balance sheet, trust) explains why fees stay high and why a startup can't undercut them, and separating investment banks from commercial banks and hedge funds clarifies which criticisms actually apply to whom. *(Not financial advice — the video's own disclaimer.)*
