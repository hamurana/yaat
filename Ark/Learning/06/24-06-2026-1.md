**Why Arterial Stiffness Matters**

Arterial stiffness directly correlates with risk of heart attacks, strokes, kidney problems, and dementia. It's visible on X-rays as calcification running alongside bone — a literal display of damage accumulating over time. Crucially, arterial stiffness is significantly within your control. Several common daily habits push it in the wrong direction, often without any awareness.

**Habit 1 — Too Many Refined Carbohydrates**

Every blood sugar spike causes oxidative stress on the endothelium (the delicate lining of arteries) — like rust forming inside the vessel walls. People with insulin resistance have measurably stiffer arteries because repeated spikes damage the vessel walls and prevent them from relaxing normally.

The fix isn't eliminating carbs — it's sourcing them from whole natural foods: vegetables, beans, lentils, and proper whole grains. On bread: most "whole grain" supermarket loaves are ultra-processed and spike blood sugar similarly to white bread. The best choice is an uncut, seeded sourdough; pre-slice and freeze it to convert it to resistant starch, which is even better for blood sugar.

**Habit 2 — Antibacterial Mouthwash Twice a Day**

Bacteria on the tongue convert dietary nitrate (from beetroot, leafy greens) into nitrite, which the body turns into nitric oxide — the molecule that tells arteries to relax and lower blood pressure. Antibacterial mouthwashes containing chlorhexidine kill these beneficial bacteria. Studies show blood pressure rises over days of regular use because the body can no longer produce as much nitric oxide.

Standard fluoride mouthwashes (Listerine, Colgate) don't cause this problem — only antiseptic/antibacterial ones do. For most people who brush and floss properly, no mouthwash is needed at all.

**Habit 3 — Sitting Still for Hours**

When sedentary, blood flow through leg arteries slows and the gentle friction that keeps artery walls healthy and smooth diminishes. Three hours of uninterrupted sitting measurably reduces the ability of leg arteries to widen; taking short walking breaks every 30 minutes completely reverses this. Going to the gym in the morning but sitting the rest of the day still counts as sedentary — arteries pay the price regardless.

Fix: stand up and move for 2–3 minutes at minimum every half hour. Calf raises while the kettle boils, phone calls while walking.

**Habit 4 — Skipping Water First Thing in the Morning**

After a night of losing fluid through breath and skin, blood is more concentrated and viscous. The early morning is also the window when heart attacks and strokes are most common — partly because of this thicker blood. A glass of water on waking restores blood volume and helps blood flow more easily. (Exception: those with heart failure or kidney disease who have been given specific fluid limits should follow their doctor's guidance instead.)

**Habit 5 — Sitting Down Immediately After Eating**

After eating, blood sugar rises rapidly. Sitting amplifies the spike; movement flattens it. Muscles that are working pull glucose out of the blood directly without needing insulin. A meta-analysis in Sports Medicine found even 2–5 minutes of light walking after a meal dramatically lowered post-meal blood sugar compared to sitting. The goal is simply not sitting for 15–30 minutes after eating — a short walk or a few squats is sufficient.

**Habit 6 — Eating Late into the Evening**

In a healthy pattern, blood pressure falls 10–20% overnight ("nocturnal dip"). People whose pressure stays elevated through the night — "non-dippers" — carry a higher risk of stroke and heart problems. Eating late keeps digestion, insulin release, and the nervous system active at the time the body is trying to wind down, blunting this protective dip.

Fix: finish the last meal at least 3 hours before bed, and make it high in protein, fibre, and whole grains to stay full longer without late-night snacking.

**Habit 7 — High Salt, Low Potassium**

High salt acts directly on artery walls, making vessels less elastic in ways partly separate from blood pressure alone. Potassium works in the opposite direction — helping vessels relax — and most people get far too little. Most dietary salt comes hidden in processed foods (breads, sauces, ready meals), not from the salt shaker.

Fix: cook from scratch to control salt intake, and load up on potassium-rich foods — leafy greens, beans, lentils, potatoes with skin, whole fruit. This pattern (similar to the DASH diet) is one of the most reliable long-term interventions for arterial health.
