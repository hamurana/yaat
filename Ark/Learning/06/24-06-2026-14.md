**The Overlooked Ones**

These wealth killers go beyond obvious overspending — several are things well-intentioned, financially-conscious people do wrong.

**1. Staying in the wrong city**

Geography is one of the most impactful and least-discussed financial decisions. Median household income: Kansas City ~$69k, Austin ~$90k, San Francisco ~$135k+. Even accounting for higher cost of living, the salary delta compounds dramatically over a career. Your first salary anchors every future negotiation. Geographic arbitrage works across borders too — Italian workers commuting into Switzerland, earning Swiss wages, then living on Italian cost of living. The network effect matters too: the people you meet, the opportunities you get exposed to, are all city-dependent. You can always move back home once established. Staying in a low-opportunity city by default is a silent wealth killer.

**2. Overfunding emergency reserves**

A 3–6 month emergency fund is standard. But people with $80–167k in a high-yield savings account "just in case" are paying an opportunity cost. Example: monthly expenses $4k → six-month emergency fund = $24k → sixteen-month = $64k. The extra $40k sitting at 3.5% HYSA instead of being invested at ~8–9% market average costs ~$145,000 over 20 years. Holding 16+ months of expenses in cash is often a scarcity mindset, not rational risk management.

**3. Divorce**

The US divorce rate is 40–50% for first marriages, 60% for second, 73% for third. Only 15% of couples sign a prenup. Direct divorce costs often exceed $20k in legal fees alone. Hidden costs: refinancing a home at current interest rates, forced sale during a bad market, splitting retirement accounts (requiring a court order with possible tax penalties), splitting business assets. Total cost: $50–100k+, sometimes much more. Finding the right partner is one of the most consequential financial decisions of your life.

**4. Trying to look rich (Keeping Up with the Joneses)**

The new car may be leased. The designer clothes could be borrowed. The bougie apartment may consume 60% of take-home pay. The "30K millionaire" — someone making $30k/year who acts like a millionaire — pays for the VIP table at a club and then can't buy drinks. The people who look rich often aren't; the wealthy often look ordinary. Comparison to peers and Instagram highlight reels is guaranteed to erode wealth.

**5. Optimizing for salary instead of equity**

If you work at a startup, public company, or pre-IPO firm and have access to equity, taking more equity over base salary can be transformative. A single good equity outcome can outperform a decade of salary. Framework: find out total shares outstanding from HR/legal, calculate your equity as a percentage, estimate what the company might be worth at liquidity or IPO. $0.1% of a $100M IPO = $100k. Equity has upside; cash doesn't. Caveat: due diligence on the company is essential.

**6. Staying on the investment sidelines**

$10k in the S&P 500 from 1996–2025: fully invested = $192k+. Miss the 10 best days = 56% less. Miss 20 best days = 74% less. Miss 30 best days = 84% less. You can't predict which days those are — trying to time the market means you're likely to miss them. Cash waiting for a "better time" is losing purchasing power to inflation. At minimum, park uninvested cash in a HYSA while rates are good.

**7. Sunk-cost loyalty (staying at a job too long)**

A 3–5% annual raise barely keeps pace. Ten years at a company that started you at $60k gets you to $70k. Meanwhile, job switchers see average earnings jumps of 11%+, sometimes 30%+. Switching every 1–2 years lets you reset your salary anchor — going laterally in title to gain pay, or laterally in pay to gain title. By your mid-to-late 30s, the compounding of those resets puts you far ahead. Companies will pay exactly what they have to — not a penny more unless you ask.

**8. High-interest debt**

Not all debt is equal. A mortgage or student loan for a degree that pays off — calculated leverage, generally acceptable. High-interest debt (credit cards at average 22.11% APR as of 2026) on a $10k balance = $185/month in interest alone, before touching principal. Wealth-building while carrying high-interest debt is nearly impossible. The rule: never carry high-interest debt from month to month.

**9. Buying too much car**

Average new car in 2026: $51k+, ~$750/month payment. Add insurance, maintenance, depreciation, gas: easily over $1,000/month total. $1,000/month invested for 10 years at 8% = $213k. A reliable used car averages $537/month — $213 less. That delta invested over 10 years = $45k. Same commute. Same life. For those who care about how the car looks: buy one with ~30k miles, ~3 years used. Still looks and feels nearly new, dramatically lower total cost of ownership.
