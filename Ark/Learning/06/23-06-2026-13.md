**What the P/B Ratio Measures**

Price-to-Book (P/B) ratio = Market Price per Share ÷ Book Value per Share. Book value per share = (Total Assets − Total Debts) ÷ Shares Outstanding. It compares what the stock market is willing to pay for a company versus what the company actually owns on paper.

**Worked Example**

Company A: $100M assets, $40M debt → Book value = $60M. With 10M shares: book value per share = $6. Stock trading at $12 → P/B = 2. Investors are paying twice what the company is worth on its balance sheet.

**Interpreting the Number**

- **P/B < 1**: Stock trades below book value — possibly undervalued, or possibly a warning sign
- **P/B = 1**: Market price equals book value — potentially fairly valued
- **P/B > 1**: Investors expect future growth and are paying a premium above net assets

**Industry Examples**

- **Tech Giant Corp (P/B = 10.5)**: AI company — most value is in software, IP, and brand, none of which appears on the balance sheet. High P/B is expected and normal.
- **Classic Furniture Co (P/B = 0.7)**: Physical assets (warehouses, inventory) judged outdated by the market.
- **Steady Bank LLC (P/B = 1.2)**: Banks typically have P/Bs close to 1 because their assets (cash, loans) are tangible and easy to value.

**The 2008 Warning**

During the 2008 financial crisis, many bank stocks dropped to P/B ratios below 1, appearing to be obvious bargains. But their balance sheets hid risky investments that were overvalued — not worth what the books claimed. Lehman Brothers collapsed. Bank of America and Citigroup needed government bailouts. A low P/B doesn't mean cheap; it can mean the assets aren't worth what's claimed.

**Where P/B Works and Where It Doesn't**

Best for asset-heavy industries: banks, real estate, manufacturing — where assets are tangible and reliably valued. Poor indicator for technology, software, or consumer brands where the real value is in intellectual property, reputation, or recurring revenue — none of which appear on the balance sheet. Coca-Cola's P/B above 10 reflects brand power and consistent profitability that book value can't capture.

**In Practice**

Find the P/B ratio on Yahoo Finance under Statistics. Always pair it with other metrics (P/E ratio, revenue growth, debt levels, cash flow) — no single number gives the full picture.
