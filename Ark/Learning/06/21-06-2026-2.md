**Overview**

Stock prices aren't set by executives in suits — they emerge from a real-time battle between buyers and sellers. This video explains the mechanics of how that price forms (the "how") and what determines whether a stock is fundamentally worth $5 or $500 (the "why").

---

**The Order Book: How Price Is Mechanically Set**

At the heart of every stock exchange is the **order book** — a live, constantly updating list of:

- **Bids**: what buyers are willing to pay
- **Asks**: what sellers are willing to accept

The current displayed price on any app is simply the price of the *last completed transaction* — not necessarily what you'd actually pay if you tried to buy or sell right now. The real price you'd transact at depends on the current bids and asks.

**How the price moves:**
- When buyers are eager, they bid higher to attract sellers → price rises
- When sellers are scared (bad news, falling price), they lower their asks to find buyers quickly → price drops

This is purely mechanical: supply and demand playing out in milliseconds.

**Intrinsic Value: The "Why" Behind a Price**

Intrinsic value is a company's *real* worth based on its fundamentals — earnings, assets, and growth potential — stripped of market hype or panic. It's the answer to: what should this company be worth, rationally?

Key caveat: **intrinsic value is an estimate, not a fact**. Different analysts applying the same methods arrive at different numbers. It's less like weighing apples and more like guessing what those apples will sell for next year.

---

**The Four Main Valuation Methods**

**1. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)**
Estimate the company's future cash profits, then "discount" them back to today's value — because future money is worth less now due to inflation and opportunity cost.

Example: a company generating $100/year for 3 years at a 10% discount rate → those flows are worth ~$91, $83, $75 today. Add a terminal value (what the company is worth after the forecast period, discounted back) — say $1,000 discounted to $751 — and the company is worth ~$1,000 today. With 100 shares outstanding, that's $10/share. Trading at $8 = potentially undervalued; at $12 = potentially overvalued.

**2. Dividend Discount Model**
Focuses on dividends — the cash a company pays shareholders. Estimate future dividends and discount them back. Best for stable, dividend-paying businesses like utilities. Example: a stock paying $2/share growing at 5% annually → calculate the present value of that income stream.

**3. Asset-Based Valuation**
Simple: total assets minus total liabilities = intrinsic value. Divide by share count for per-share value. Works well for companies with substantial physical assets — real estate firms, banks, manufacturers. Example: $500M in assets, $200M in debt → $300M intrinsic value.

**4. Earnings Multiple (P/E Ratio)**
Take earnings per share and multiply by an industry-standard multiple. Example: if tech companies typically trade at 20× earnings and your company earns $5/share, intrinsic value ≈ $100/share. Fastest method, but relies on comparisons and can be inaccurate.

---

**Value Investing vs. Market Reality**

Value investors (the archetypal example: Warren Buffett) buy stocks trading *below* intrinsic value and hold long-term, ignoring short-term noise. Buffett's framing: "Investing is like buying a farm. You look at its soil and crops, not what the neighbour paid yesterday."

But most stocks don't trade at intrinsic value — and that gap is often intentional:

- **Market sentiment**: excitement bids prices above fundamentals
- **Future expectations**: growth stocks (tech, biotech) are priced on *expected* profits years from now, not today's numbers. Tesla traded far above any rational intrinsic value calculation for years — but buyers were pricing in future technology and growth, not current earnings
- **Macro environment**: low interest rates (e.g. 2020–2022) made stocks more attractive than bonds, driving prices up even for companies with poor fundamentals

---

**Where Intrinsic Value Falls Short**

Intrinsic value is least useful for:
- **Startups and unprofitable companies**: calculated intrinsic value may be near zero, but the stock trades high on future potential
- **High-growth tech/biotech**: current earnings don't reflect what investors are actually betting on

The market can also ignore intrinsic value for years — "staying irrational longer than you might expect." It's a useful lens, not a reliable short-term predictor.
