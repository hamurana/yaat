**What Is Elicitation?**

Elicitation is a CIA-derived technique, originally developed by John Nolan (book: *Confidential*, now out of print). The core idea: use statements instead of questions to get people to volunteer sensitive information. When you ask a direct question, the brain triggers a small security alarm. Statements bypass it entirely — people feel they're correcting you or volunteering, not being interrogated.

**The Three Core Techniques**

**1. Triggering the need to correct the record**
Make an inaccurate statement and let the other person correct you. Example: tell a Whole Foods employee "I read that all Whole Foods staff just got bumped to $26/hour" — they'll say "What? I make $17." No one asked. Cold War example: Soviet spies would tell a US Navy sailor "I know the German submarines can outrun yours because their propellers are 22 feet in diameter." The sailor would turn around and correct them, revealing classified specifications. The disbelief layer works similarly — "There's no way your CEO would move the office in February, interest rates are too high" — and suddenly someone who "wasn't supposed to say anything" explains the February timeline.

**2. Bracketing**
Give a range and let the person land inside it: "Was that between March and May?" or "Are you in the 39 to 59 range?" People are compelled to correct the bracket even when the information is sensitive.

**3. Disbelief**
Express surprise or skepticism — "No way that entire trip was flawless" — and the person immediately begins explaining the challenges, problems, or details they otherwise wouldn't share. The negative statement invites positive disclosure.

**The Framework for Using It**

Start with "So" or "I bet" to frame the statement naturally:
- "So you've been doing this for three years"
- "I bet there were still some challenges there"
- "I bet you overcame a whole lot to get here"

Follow up with a recap: "So, no creamer, no sugar — you're extremely specific about coffee." The person elaborates. You never asked a single question.

**Key Principle**

The more sensitive the information, the fewer questions you should ask. Statements feel like conversation; questions feel like interrogation. Elicitation is not manipulation for its own sake — it's understanding that direct questions often generate guarded responses, while statements that invite correction or elaboration open the door to authentic disclosure.
