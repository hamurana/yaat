A fund pools investor money to buy a mix of assets (stocks, bonds, real estate) managed collectively rather than individually.

**Index funds**: Passively track an index (S&P 500, Nasdaq, Dow, etc.) with no active stock-picking, so fees are very low (0.02–0.20% expense ratio). Diversified across hundreds of companies, relatively low risk, priced once daily after market close (NAV pricing). Popular with Vanguard, BlackRock/iShares, Fidelity—recommended by experts like Buffett since beating the market consistently is hard.

**Mutual funds**: Similar pooling structure but usually actively managed by professionals trying to beat a benchmark. Higher fees (0.5–1.5%+), and most don't consistently outperform the market. Also priced once daily after close. Appeals to those who trust a manager's strategy or want niche exposure.

**Hedge funds**: Private funds for accredited/high-net-worth investors, using aggressive strategies (short selling, leverage, derivatives) for high returns—and high risk. Known for "2 and 20" fees (2% management + 20% of profits). Not for average investors.

**ETFs**: Hybrid of index funds and stocks—diversified like an index fund but trades throughout the day at real-time prices like a stock. Low fees (0.03–0.75%), mostly passive, often commission-free.

**Bottom line**: Index funds/ETFs suit most everyday long-term investors (low cost, diversified). Mutual funds fit those backing a specific manager/strategy despite higher fees. Hedge funds are for wealthy investors comfortable with high risk and fees.
