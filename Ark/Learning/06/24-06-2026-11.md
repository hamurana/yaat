**The Philosophy**

Buy-it-for-life items are the opposite of planned obsolescence — products designed for the dump. The case for them: you don't need to replace them year over year, which is better for your wallet long-term, and better for the environment. Spencer's rule: always check the secondhand market first. Over 50% of his BIFL items were purchased used, often at 30–50% below retail.

**The 5 Items**

**1. Casio Watch (A158WEA-9 or F91W)**
Founded 1946, watch-making since the 1970s. The BIFL story isn't about a warranty — it's about robustness. Stories all over the internet of these watches lasting decades. Battery alone lasts ~10 years before replacement. The A158 (metal band) and F91W (resin band) are nearly identical in face design. Picked up used for $20–35 CAD. The adjustable band means no trip to a watch shop. The retro tech aesthetic is a bonus in an era where that's having a moment. For ultimate durability: Casio G-Shock.

**2. Rotring 600 Mechanical Pencil**
Founded 1928 in Germany. The 600 model launched 1989 with full metal body, knurled grip, brass construction. Old models made in Germany; new ones in Japan — both well-regarded. Uses standard 0.5/0.7 leads. One vulnerability: drop it on the tip and it bends, but the tip is a replaceable part. The black version develops a brass patina as paint chips — comparable to aged Leica cameras. A true industrial design classic.

**3. ThinkPad T480**
Original IBM line starting 1992; sold to Lenovo in 2005. The T480 is widely considered the last great ThinkPad: final T-series with removable battery (plus internal, enabling hot swaps), dual hard drive slots, dual RAM slots, replaceable keyboard/screen/trackpad/battery. Uses standard Phillips head screws. Webcam has a physical privacy switch — a feature modern MacBooks lack. Upgraded to 32GB RAM and 1TB SSD, with a backup motherboard bought for $20. Running Linux, this laptop is projected to last 10–15 years. The newer T14 Gen 5 (partnered with iFixit) appears to be returning to similar repairability.

**4. Opinel No. 8 Knife**
French company, founded late 1800s, still made in France. Simple materials: beechwood handle, carbon steel blade (stainless also available), metal safety collar (the iconic Opinel ring). Cost: ~$20 CAD. The BIFL case: extreme simplicity — no springs, no locks. Stories of 1950s and 60s knives still functioning. Not a bushcraft knife for splitting firewood, but excellent for average tasks. Limited warranty covers manufacturing defects only, but the design is so simple there's almost nothing to fail.

**5. Red Wing Iron Ranger Boots (or equivalent Goodyear-welt boots)**
American company, Minnesota factory, over 100 years of production. Originally made for miners, factory workers, farmers. Key feature: Goodyear-welt construction — sole is stitched (not glued) to the insole and outsole, making the sole replaceable even after decades of wear. The best investment in footwear is in the construction method, not just the brand — any Goodyear-welt boot qualifies. Alternatives: Thursday Boot Company (often recommended on BIFL subreddit), LL Bean Katahdin Boots (Chippewa-made). Spencer bought his Iron Rangers used for ~$180 CAD (vs. $500+ new) and his LL Bean boots for ~$85 CAD. Both still going strong years later.

**The Common Thread**

Simplicity is durability. Each item succeeds because of what it isn't: no unnecessary complexity, no proprietary systems, no features designed for obsolescence. The secondhand market makes nearly all of these accessible — buying used is the entry point, not a compromise.
