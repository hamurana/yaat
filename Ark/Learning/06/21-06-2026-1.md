**The Core Question**

What happens if you always invest at the worst possible time — right at a market all-time high? Research shows the answer is counterintuitive: it still beats waiting for a dip.

---

**The Three Investor Archetypes**

The video sets up a comparison using three investor types:

- **Dip-Waiting Wendy** — disciplined, never invests at all-time highs, waits for a 10% pullback before buying
- **Bad-Timing Bridget** — invests every year but always at the absolute peak; worst luck imaginable
- **The non-investor** — paralysed by fear, never invests at all; inflation silently erodes savings year after year

Intuitively, Wendy should crush Bridget. The data says otherwise.

---

**What the Research Actually Shows**

The Archbridge Family Office analysed S&P 500 returns back to 1990, comparing three strategies: waiting for a 10% pullback, investing immediately at all-time highs, and investing on random days.

Result: **investing at all-time highs produced higher average returns than waiting for a dip** — and even beat random-day investing.

Why? All-time highs cluster during strong bull markets — periods of earnings growth, economic expansion, and heavy investment flows. Healthy markets don't hit one high and collapse; they hit many highs in a row. While Wendy sits in cash waiting for the "perfect entry," the market keeps moving higher. Sometimes the 10% dip never comes. Other times it arrives long after prices have already moved far above where she could have bought in.

This is the **hidden cost of waiting**: it doesn't feel risky — it feels safe. But waiting has a price: missed gains, missed compounding, missed reinvested dividends, and most importantly, missed time.

---

**The Cost of Missing the Best Days**

Hartford Funds studied the impact of missing the market's best days across decades of S&P 500 data:

- Missing only the **10 best days** over a 20-year period cut total returns by **more than half**
- Missing the **20 best days** reduced returns by **over 70%**

The critical detail: many of those best days happen during volatile periods right after crashes — when fear is highest. Investors who wait for clarity often miss the recovery entirely.

---

**Why Our Brains Resist Buying at Highs**

Loss aversion and regret aversion are deeply wired. We feel losses far more intensely than equivalent gains. When markets are at all-time highs, the brain screams "danger" — even though historically, ATHs are most often followed by more ATHs.

Markets spend a surprisingly large proportion of time near record levels. The perceived danger of buying high is mostly psychological, not statistical.

---

**The Practical Takeaway: Consistency Over Perfection**

The data strongly favours **dollar cost averaging** — investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule regardless of market conditions. This removes emotion from the decision entirely. It means Bridget keeps investing through crashes and recoveries, and her early investments have decades to recover and compound. A market peak today often looks like a valley 10–20 years later.

---

**The Diversification Caveat**

All of the above research is based on the US market. This matters because of concentration risk in the S&P 500:

- The MSCI All Country World Index contains ~2,500 stocks — roughly **5× the S&P 500's 500**
- Technology stocks alone now make up ~**41% of the S&P 500** — a concentration level not seen since the dot-com bubble (per Rockefeller Capital Management)
- The market-cap weighted S&P 500 vs. an equal-weighted version reveals how much recent performance is carried by a small handful of mega-cap names

The S&P 500 has historically outperformed the MSCI World, largely due to US company dominance over recent decades. But it is clearly less diversified — if those few mega-caps stumble, the index falls because of them specifically.
