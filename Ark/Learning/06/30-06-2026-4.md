**The Hook & Core Thesis**

Clear thinking is a trainable skill, and most smart people sabotage it with three predictable traps. Fix them and you stop spinning on hard problems while everyone else stays stuck — the rare ability that lands you in the top 1% of people others want on their team.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **When stuck, think *less*, not harder.** Feeling stuck signals [[cognitive overload]] — pushing harder makes clarity worse.
- **Overwhelm is a triage problem, not a capacity problem.** You don't need to see how it all connects to know the next move.
- **Aim for accuracy, not 100% confidence.** Chasing certainty causes [[paralysis by analysis]]; widen your margin of error instead.
- **Confidence is a by-product of scope.** A broader claim is more confident; a narrower one is more precise — you trade between them.
- **Real high performers reframe problems; "optical" performers just sound confident** by ignoring the hard parts.

**High-Yield Assets**

- **Speaker credentials:** **[[Justin Sung]]**, a learning & cognitive-performance coach for **14+ years**, and a former medical doctor.
- **Trap 1 — "think harder":** the fix rests on **[[working memory]]** (the brain's "workbench"), which is universally limited. Solution ties to the computer-science principle of **[[separation of concerns]]** — resolve a few factors at a time, then add the next piece.
- **Trap 2 — "overwhelm = capacity":** reframe overwhelm as a **triage signal**. Uses the **[[Theory of Constraints]]**: ask *what one constraint, if removed, makes everything else simpler?*
- **Two practical tools:** (1) the **[[dependency map]]** — dump every factor, draw which ones block which, and visually spot the biggest constraint (he uses it for decisions, project/product planning, prioritization, and upskilling); (2) **schedule a small weekly block** (even **1 hour/week**) for non-urgent constraints that are "needle-movers." Logic: if a constraint takes 6 months to clear and goes urgent in 6 months, *it's urgent now*.
- **Trap 3 — confidence intervals:** a statistics concept misapplied to decisions. Worked example: a blood-pressure drug lowers BP **10 points ± 3 (95% CI)** → true effect is 7–13 in 95 of 100 runs. **Counterintuitive key:** *more confidence = less precision.* "3 billion ± 3 billion English speakers" is 100% confident but useless. So to raise confidence, **widen the interval**, don't shrink it.
- **Learning-to-learn as the ultimate constraint:** improving learning efficiency **20–30%** compounds for life. Plug for his free newsletter (3–5 min/week of pre-triaged strategies) and a companion **"think on paper"** video.

**Case Studies / Anecdotes**

- **The complex decision spiral:** optimizing factor A worsens B, optimizing B sacrifices C — so you "sleep on it," re-run mental simulations in the shower and in bed, and it only gets *harder* because you're accruing fatigue. Clarity drops the more you try.
- **On-call as a junior doctor:** holding the phone after hours covering **150 patients**, sometimes **<10 seconds of rest across 5 hours**. Spending 5–10 extra minutes placing a catheter line, he missed ~5 calls — which taught him to shift from *"how do I get through all of this?"* to *"what's actually important to focus on?"* — i.e. triage aggressively.
- **Marketing manager example:** overconfident move = just reuse last year's data; paralyzed move = endless testing; correct move = "70% confident last year's message still works, but 99% confident one of *these five* will," then quick-test the five.
- **Sales-funnel example:** instead of 9 months / 40 variations chasing an exact conversion rate, conclude "99% confident conversion lands between 2–7%," and design the funnel to scale up or pull back rather than betting on a single 10% break-even assumption.

---
**The "So What?" Factor**

Next time you feel stuck: (1) narrow your focus to two or three factors and separate the concerns; (2) treat overwhelm as a cue to find the single biggest constraint via a dependency map, and reserve a weekly hour for the important-but-not-yet-urgent ones; (3) stop hunting for certainty — widen your claim until you're confident *enough* to act, then build in room to adjust. Acknowledging uncertainty honestly is what makes thinking clearly possible.
