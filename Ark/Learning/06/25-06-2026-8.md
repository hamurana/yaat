## The Core Problem

Most presenters lose the room in the first ten seconds — not because they're bad, but because they play it safe. They start with their name, role, and context. The audience switches off before the talk begins. The solution is to start with surprise: something unexpected that earns attention before asking for it.

---

## The 5 Opening Techniques

**1. Open With a Question**
Ask something the audience genuinely has to think about — "What's the scariest thing you've ever done?" Questions are powerful because the moment you ask one, the audience's brain instantly searches for an answer. Even without answering aloud, they're now engaged. You have their full attention.

**2. Surprising Statement**
A statistic, fact, or bold claim that challenges what people believe — something that makes them think *wait, is that true?* That moment of doubt is attention. Execution tip: say it slowly. Pause before and after the statement. Example: "400... [pause] the average office desk has 400 times more bacteria than the average toilet seat." The pause lets the surprise land.

**3. Start With a Story**
Two of the most-viewed TED Talks of all time open with stories. Stories work because they turn the talk into a movie — the moment a story begins, people stop analyzing and start experiencing. Key: skip the long background. Start right in the moment. Where and when? What are you doing? What's going wrong? Drop the audience straight into the scene.

**4. Make a Big Promise**
At the start of any talk, the audience has one question: *what's in it for me?* Answer it directly with what they will *gain* — not what you will *talk about*. Instead of "Today I'll talk about body language," say: "By the end of this talk, you'll know how to appear confident in any high-pressure situation." That's a promise worth listening for.

**5. Visual Action Hook**
The most advanced technique — and the most memorable when done well. Don't just say something unexpected; *do* something unexpected on stage. It doesn't have to be dramatic: walk on stage and say nothing for five seconds. Hold up an object without explaining it. Write a word on a flip chart before speaking. The principle: do something that makes people curious before you've said a single word.

---

## The Underlying Logic

Obama's "I'm Michelle's husband" works because it's surprise — it breaks the expected pattern of introductions. All five techniques share that logic: pattern interrupt at the top, before the audience has decided whether to pay attention or not.
