**What VO2 Max Actually Is**

VO2 max stands for the maximum volume of oxygen your body can take in and use per minute at full effort. It's essentially a single score for the combined health of your heart, lungs, circulation, and mitochondria — the tiny energy factories inside muscle cells. Every link in that chain (lungs → heart → blood vessels → muscles → mitochondria) must function well for the number to be high. Think of it as horsepower: a bigger number means a more powerful engine.

**Why It Predicts How Long You Live**

A study of over 100,000 people who had fitness measured on a treadmill found that the least fit group had a mortality risk equal to — or in some cases worse than — smoking and diabetes. Crucially, the researchers found no ceiling: the fitter people were, the longer they tended to live, with no point of diminishing returns. Being unfit isn't a minor lifestyle footnote; on the data, it ranks alongside the conditions we fear most.

From around your 30s, VO2 max naturally drops ~10% per decade without intervention. That slow drain is why people gradually lose the ability to climb stairs, carry shopping, or get on the floor to play with grandchildren. Raising your VO2 max is like starting that decline from a much higher baseline — a fit 60-year-old can carry the engine of an unfit 30-year-old.

**The Reserve Principle**

The real reason to build aerobic capacity isn't just performance — it's reserve. Illness, injury, and hospitalisation happen to everyone. People who enter those events fit come out far better. An already frail 60-year-old who gets sick often emerges noticeably more frail and looking older within weeks. The goal is to go into older age with enough reserve that setbacks only drop you a few rungs on the ladder rather than knocking you off it entirely.

**How to Actually Improve It**

A meta-analysis of 50+ studies found that High Intensity Interval Training (HIIT) improves VO2 max faster than steady moderate exercise. HIIT means very short bursts of very hard effort followed by easier recovery periods — the same principle whether running, cycling, swimming, or walking up a steep hill.

Two specific protocols proven to work, done ~3 times per week:

- **15/15**: 15 seconds at 90–95% max heart rate, 15 seconds easy recovery at 60–70%, repeated 40–60 times.
- **4×4**: 4 minutes at 90–95% max heart rate, 3 minutes gentle recovery at ~70%, repeated 4 times.

Max heart rate estimate: 220 minus your age (rough but functional as a starting point). A decent smartwatch (Garmin, Apple Watch) will estimate it and show real-time zones, removing most of the guesswork.

**The Injury Risk — Don't Ignore It**

The doctor shares a personal warning: years of HIIT-style running led to a stress fracture that stopped him running for two years and cost him months of hard-built fitness. His point: the biggest risk with HIIT is injury, and in your 50s or 60s a serious setback can become the thing that ends your exercise life entirely. The injury isn't just the broken bone — it's everything the bone takes with it: mood, confidence, habit, and fitness.

**The Minimum Effective Dose**

A balanced weekly framework rather than pure HIIT:

1. **Steady aerobic base** — 2–3 sessions/week of conversational-pace cardio (brisk walk, easy jog, bike, swim). This does improve VO2 max, just more slowly.
2. **Resistance training** — 2+ sessions/week covering all major muscle groups.
3. **HIIT** — just 1–2 sessions/week to sharpen the engine.
4. **Daily movement** — break up sitting throughout the day.

The steady aerobic work builds durability; the HIIT sharpens the ceiling. Doing only HIIT maximises the training signal but maximises injury risk too. Balance is the sustainable path.
