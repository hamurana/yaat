**What a Fund Is**

A fund pools money from multiple investors to buy a diversified basket of assets. Instead of picking individual stocks, you buy into the fund and own a slice of everything it holds. Vanguard's VTI ETF, for example, holds over 3,600 stocks — accessible with a single transaction from $1.

**Mutual Funds**

Professionally managed pools that can be actively or passively managed. Actively managed mutual funds have a manager monitoring investments daily, aiming to beat the market. Cost: expense ratios of 0.5–1.5% annually, paid whether the fund gains or loses. They trade only at the end-of-day NAV (net asset value), not intraday. Minimum investment typically $500–$5,000. Examples: Vanguard's VPMAX, Fidelity's FBGRX.

**Index Funds**

Passively managed funds that simply track an index — a defined list of companies by size or sector. The S&P 500 index contains the 500 largest US-listed companies; an S&P 500 index fund buys all 500. No active selection; when a company drops out of the index, it's sold and the replacement is bought automatically. Because there's no active management, fees are typically under 0.5%. Index funds come in two styles: mutual fund (trades at end of day) and ETF (trades intraday).

**ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds)**

ETFs trade on stock exchanges during market hours exactly like individual stocks — buy and sell any time via a trading app. Most ETFs are passively managed index funds, though actively managed ETFs exist. Minimum investment as low as $1. This makes them the most beginner-accessible option. Examples: VOO (S&P 500), QQQ (NASDAQ 100, tech-heavy), VT (US + international), SMH (semiconductors only). Bitcoin ETFs also exist.

**Hedge Funds**

Mutual fund-style structures for ultra-wealthy, accredited investors. Minimum investment $100,000 to several million. Strategies are aggressive — short-selling, complex derivatives, betting against the market. High fees. Examples: Bridgewater Associates, Citadel, Millennium Management. Not publicly accessible.

**Which Performs Best?**

Counter-intuitively, passively managed index funds and ETFs consistently outperform the majority of actively managed funds and hedge funds over the long term in the US. Reasons: (1) beating the market consistently is extremely hard; (2) high expense ratios compound against active fund returns over time. Some actively managed funds do beat passive benchmarks — but most don't do so consistently.

**Quick Decision Framework**

- **Flexibility + low minimums + low fees**: ETF
- **Prefer not watching intraday prices**: mutual fund (index style)
- **Want someone else to manage it actively**: actively managed mutual fund (higher fees)
- **Ultra-wealthy with appetite for risk**: hedge fund
