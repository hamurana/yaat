**The Hook & Core Thesis**

The "unluckiest" investor who only ever buys at all-time highs actually *beats* the disciplined investor waiting for a dip. All-time highs cluster in bull markets, so buying at records historically outperforms waiting — because the real cost is the opportunity lost while you sit in cash. Consistency beats perfect timing.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **Investing at all-time highs beats waiting for a dip** — and even beats investing on random days, historically.
- **"Waiting for a dip" is still market timing** — a bet that prices fall, which fails if the bull market simply lasts longer than you expect.
- **All-time highs cluster** during earnings growth and expansion; healthy markets hit many highs in a row, not one then a collapse.
- **The cost of waiting is opportunity cost** — missed gains, compounding, reinvested dividends, and time.
- **Missing a handful of best days devastates returns**, and those best days cluster right after crashes when fear is highest.
- **Dollar-cost averaging beats timing** because it removes emotion.
- **Caveat: concentration risk** — the S&P 500 is now heavily tech-weighted and less diversified than global indexes.

---
**High-Yield Assets**

- **The three investors:** **"Waiting-for-a-dip Wendy"** (never buys at highs, waits for a 10% pullback), **"Bad-timing Bridget"** (invests every year at the absolute peak), and a third who never invests and watches **inflation** destroy their savings.
- **[[Archbridge Family Office]] study** — analyzed **[[S&P 500]]** returns back to **1990**, comparing three strategies: waiting for a 10% pullback, investing immediately at every new all-time high, and investing on random days. **Buying at all-time highs produced the highest average returns** — beating both the dip-waiting and random-day strategies.
- **[[Hartford Funds]] "best days" study** — staying fully invested dramatically beat missing just a few top days. **Missing the 10 best days over 20 years cut total returns by more than half; missing the 20 best days cut returns by over 70%.** Many of those best days fall during volatile periods right after crashes.
- **Behavioral drivers:** **[[loss aversion]]** and **[[regret aversion]]** — we feel losses far more intensely than gains, so all-time highs trigger a "danger" reflex even though highs are often followed by more highs.
- **[[Dollar-cost averaging]]** — regularly investing a fixed amount regardless of conditions; works by removing emotion.
- **Concentration-risk data:**
  - **[[T. Rowe Price]]** — the **MSCI All Country World Index** holds ~5× the companies of the S&P 500 (**2,500+ vs ~500**).
  - **[[Rockefeller Capital Management]]** — **technology stocks make up ~41% of the S&P 500**, a concentration not seen since the dot-com bubble.
  - **[[Guinness Global Investors]]** — the gap between the market-cap-weighted S&P 500 and an equal-weighted version shows how much recent performance rests on a handful of mega-caps.
- **Balance:** the S&P 500 has still historically outperformed the **MSCI World** long-term, driven by US company strength — it's just less diversified.

**Case Studies / Anecdotes**

- **The tree-planting analogy** — does it matter whether you planted on the sunniest or cloudiest day of the year? Not really. What matters is that you planted it and gave it decades to grow. A market peak today often looks like a valley 10–20 years from now.
- **Bridget through 2000 and 2008** — yes, investing right before a 40–50% crash hurts, but she invests *every* year, so her early money has decades to recover and her overall record still holds up against Wendy's cash drag.

---
**The "So What?" Factor**

Stop waiting for a better entry price — the data says it costs you more than it saves. Invest a fixed amount regularly (dollar-cost averaging) regardless of whether the market "feels too high," because all-time highs beget more all-time highs and the best days can't be predicted. One caveat before dumping everything into a single S&P 500 fund: it's now unusually concentrated in mega-cap tech, so weigh diversification against a broader global index like the MSCI World.
