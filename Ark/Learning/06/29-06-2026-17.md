**The Hook & Core Thesis**

The "10,000 steps a day" target was a 1960s marketing slogan, not science — and for most people over 50 it's too high. **[[Dr. Alex]]** argues the real target is lower (~7,500 steps), and that what matters most isn't steps at all but three pillars of movement spread across the whole day.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **7,500 steps captures nearly all the benefit** for older adults; the death-risk curve flattens there, so chasing 10,000 buys very little extra.
- **The three pillars beat any step count:** aerobic cardio, short bursts of hard effort (for [[VO2 max]]), and resistance training.
- **VO2 max is one of the strongest predictors of lifespan** ever measured — stronger than blood pressure or cholesterol.
- **Prolonged sitting kills even if you hit your step target.** An hour at the gym doesn't cancel 14 hours seated; aim to be lightly active all day.
- **The optimal routine isn't the right routine** for older bodies — intense bursts get risky with age, so progression matters more than theory.
- **Pick a reachable goal.** A target that defeats you is worse than no target; build the [[habit]] in layers over a year.

---
**High-Yield Assets**

- **Origin of "10,000 steps":** a Japanese company's 1960s pedometer marketed before the **Tokyo Olympics**, named the **"10,000-step meter."** The figure was picked because the Japanese character for 10,000 (万) resembles a person mid-stride — pure marketing, zero science.
- **2019 study (~16,000 older women, avg early 70s):** death risk fell as steps rose but **plateaued around 7,500 steps/day**; beyond that, gains continued only very slowly.
- **Larger multi-country analysis:** protective effect plateaued at **6–8,000 steps/day for over-60s**, and **8–10,000 for younger adults** — i.e. 10,000 fits a fit 30–40-year-old, not the over-50s chasing it.
- **The three pillars:**
  1. **Aerobic / steady cardio** — keeps heart and lungs strong.
  2. **Short bursts of hard effort ([[HIIT]])** — the most efficient way to raise **[[VO2 max]]**. A treadmill study of **100,000+ people** found the fitter the person (higher VO2 max), the longer they lived, with no real ceiling.
  3. **Resistance training** — muscle is a **metabolic organ** that mops up blood sugar and acts as a reserve in illness.
- **Vigorous "incidental" movement study (accelerometers):** people doing **3–4 minutes/day** of vigorous everyday movement (hurrying for a bus, carrying shopping upstairs) had **40–50% lower** death rates from heart disease and cancer.
- **Resistance-training meta-analysis:** just **30–60 minutes/week** of muscle-strengthening work tied to **10–20% lower** risk of death, heart disease, diabetes, and cancer — independent of cardio.
- **Tools:** a **[[Garmin]]** watch (worn daily) estimates VO2 max reliably; any cheap step counter (~£20–30) is enough to start.

**Case Studies / Anecdotes**

- **The builder / gardener** — on their feet all day, constantly lifting heavy loads, regularly breathless hauling sand or pushing a wheelbarrow. Without trying, they hit all three pillars woven through normal life — almost exactly how our ancestors lived for hundreds of thousands of years before modern life engineered movement out.
- **"Advice changes with who's in front of me"** — a 30-year-old gets pushed hard toward intensity; an 80-year-old gets pointed gently at a daily walk and standing up from a chair. The principle never moves: pick a goal you can actually reach.
- **The chair squat** — resistance training can start as nothing more than standing up and sitting down from a chair, then a few dumbbell movements at home.

---
**The "So What?" Factor**

Stop feeling guilty about missing 10,000 — aim for 7,500, and don't even start there if you're sedentary. Build in layers: more walking through the day first (2,000 → 5,000 → 7,500), then a couple of gentle breathless bursts a week, then resistance training twice a week. Take a full year to lay all three in slowly rather than doing everything at once and quitting. The best routine is the one you'll still be doing this time next year — and sitting less all day matters more than one hard gym session.
