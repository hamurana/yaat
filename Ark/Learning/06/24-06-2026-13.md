**The Frame**

Personal stories of real losses from the creator's own 20s. Your 20s aren't just important — a $1,000 mistake now could cost you a multi-million dollar business opportunity. And a $1,000 smart decision in your 20s compounds into something transformative.

**The 7 Worst Purchases**

**1. Cheap goods**
"Buy cheap, buy twice." A cheap lock on a company car let the thief in — and he left the dismantled lock on the street as if to say, don't insult me. Buying cheap isn't frugal; it's expensive long-term. Better metric: return on investment, not lowest price.

**2. Get-rich-quick courses**
Everyone is vulnerable given the right circumstances — even professional scam investigators get caught. Pattern: infomercial promises, bonus items for buying now, engineered scarcity and panic. When the creator phoned in, the "bonus VHS tapes" were just generic information available in cheap books. Trying to get a refund? They push you to the next product in the funnel. Modern form: Discord signal groups where the tipster earns whether you win or lose. The tactic: they sell you the dream and give you just enough to make you want more. Counter: if they can do the deal today, they'll do it next week — never decide while in panic.

**3. Designer clothes on store credit**
Walked into a designer shop with no price tags, got fitted for clothes beyond his cash, and signed up for a store credit card. After a year of payments, he'd paid only interest — not a dollar off the principal. Store credit cards are designed for one purpose: recurring revenue from customers who don't understand the interest rate trap. Research the right credit card before making any credit decision.

**4. Stock signals and tips**
A friend at a meat market gave him a couple of tips that paid off — then he got hooked. The next tip: MediAT Invest. Lost the majority of the investment within weeks. The dot-com bubble made random stocks look like wisdom. The lesson: even a monkey is right some of the time. When someone gives a signal, they're almost always being paid a commission regardless of your outcome. You bear all the risk; the tipster earns either way. This experience is what pushed him toward index funds.

**5. Sports betting**
A friend "who had never lost" guided him through horse racing. First bet: $500, horse came in second. Advised to try again — doubled to $1,000, same result. Missed the next two races; they won. Put $4,000 down to "get his money back." Horse came in third. Total loss: $5,500. The trap: chasing losses amplifies them. The casual sports bet is fine in moderation with a fixed budget. When fun stops, stop. The addictive app model now hooks people earlier.

**6. Unnecessary medical costs**
When you're in pain, you'll pay anything. He signed up for a year of spinal treatments he didn't actually need because he was vulnerable and hadn't researched the clinic beforehand. The practitioner had a sales funnel of escalating treatments. Moral: always get a second opinion, especially for ongoing treatment commitments. Research before you're desperate.

**7. Over-promised services**
A web designer convinced him that Adobe Flash was essential for a top-tier business website — knowing it wasn't right but selling what he had. $10,000 later: a beautiful, completely non-functional site. It couldn't sell anything, wasn't searchable on Google, and the designer disappeared on a job in Dubai. The principle: once you've invested money, sunk-cost thinking makes you keep throwing more in. Counter: the seven-day rule — wait seven days before any significant purchase or service commitment. It removes panic and allows research.

**The Takeaway**

Mistakes are only true mistakes if you make them twice. The lesson from each one: patience, research, and the ability to walk away from a deal are more valuable than the urgency any salesperson creates.
