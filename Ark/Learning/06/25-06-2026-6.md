## The Test

Sit down on the floor and stand back up without using your hands, knees as levers, or grabbing anything. Score starts at 10:
- Lose 1 point each time you use a hand (going down or up)
- Lose 1 point each time you use a knee
- Lose 0.5 points for a noticeable wobble

Most people in their 50s score 5–8. A study of 2,000+ middle-aged adults found that those scoring 3 or below had **nearly 5× higher mortality** over the following 13 years compared to those scoring 8+. The test isn't measuring whether you can get off a floor — it's measuring everything else going on inside your body.

---

## What the Test Actually Measures

Standing up from the floor without assistance requires:
- **Quadriceps** — generating force from a fully loaded position
- **Glutes, hip flexors, core, calves** — coordinating in sequence with precise timing
- **Nervous system** — orchestrating all of the above in real time
- **Sufficient muscle mass** — to actually lift you

The composite score reflects: muscle mass, neuromuscular coordination, balance, joint mobility, and **functional reserve** — your body's buffer for coping with stress, absorbing a fall, recovering from illness.

## Muscle Loss: The Silent Crisis

From the mid-30s onward, everyone loses muscle unless actively doing something about it:
- **1–2% per year** initially
- **1.5–3% per year** after 50
- **30–50% of peak muscle mass** gone by the late 70s

The most affected fibres are **type 2 (fast twitch)** — the ones that generate explosive force, catch you when you fall, and power you out of a chair. Slow twitch endurance fibres hold up better. Fast twitch fibres shrink and disappear disproportionately. These are exactly what the sit-to-stand test measures. The medical name for this progressive loss is **sarcopenia** — one of the most underappreciated health crises in modern medicine, because it's so gradual it feels like normal ageing.

## Why Muscle Is a Metabolic Organ

Skeletal muscle — particularly the large muscles of the legs and hips — is not just about strength. It's a primary metabolic organ:

**Blood sugar regulation.** After a meal, muscles absorb 70–80% of blood glucose. When muscle mass declines, glucose sits in the bloodstream longer, the pancreas releases more insulin, cells become insulin-resistant — one of the earliest steps toward type 2 diabetes and the downstream cascade of metabolic disease (heart disease, fatty liver).

**Energy engine.** Skeletal muscle is where the vast majority of mitochondria live — the structures that convert glucose and fat into fuel. More muscle = more mitochondria = more efficient energy engine = better metabolic flexibility (smooth switching between burning carbs and fat). When muscle mass drops, the engine literally shrinks. This is why people describe feeling more tired and sluggish as they age — not because of age itself, but because their metabolic engine has contracted. **This is reversible, even into your 70s and 80s.**

**Brain maintenance.** When muscle fibres contract, they release signalling molecules called **myokines**. One key myokine crosses the blood-brain barrier and raises **BDNF** (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) — a growth and maintenance signal for brain cells that supports new neural connections, memory, and learning, and is protective against cognitive decline. Exercise is currently the single most evidence-backed intervention for reducing dementia risk — stronger than any supplement or drug. Muscle is a significant part of why.

## The Cascade That Ends Independence

In emergency medicine, muscle loss has a clear endpoint: a minor trip (step, bath) → hip fracture → hospital admission. Around **1 in 3 people** who suffer a hip fracture do not return to independent living. The **one-year mortality rate** after hip fracture in older people is 20–30%. Strong quadriceps and hip stabilisers slow a fall, absorb the impact, and give you the split second of reactivity that keeps a stumble from becoming a fracture. Atrophied muscles don't.

## What to Do About It

**Daily movement foundation.** Walking, taking stairs, getting on and off the floor. Highly underrated as a metabolic intervention. But walking alone won't preserve fast-twitch fibres.

**Progressive resistance training.** Minimum 3 sessions per week, 15–25 minutes each, working hard enough that the last few reps feel genuinely difficult. No gym or equipment required — body weight squats, split squats, hip hinges, step-ups target the exact muscles the sit-to-stand test measures.

**Protein — more than you think.** After 40, **anabolic resistance** develops: muscle becomes less responsive to dietary protein signals. The practical consequence: you need significantly more protein than in your 20s. Current evidence: **1.6–2g per kg of body weight per day** for mid-life people actively maintaining muscle. Spread across meals — your body can only absorb ~30–40g for muscle protein synthesis at one sitting. Eggs, meat, fish, legumes, dairy, Greek yoghurt. A large protein hit at dinner with little earlier in the day is less effective than distributing it throughout.

---

## The Core Insight

Muscle is not about how you look or how strong you feel. It is a metabolic organ, an energy engine, a brain-support system, and a fall-prevention mechanism. Its loss starts silently in your 30s and ends — for many — in a hospital ward. Unlike genetics or age, muscle is modifiable at any age. The biology responds to the right stimulus. You just have to start.
