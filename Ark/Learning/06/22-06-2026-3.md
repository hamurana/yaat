**Why Market Cap Alone Misleads You**

Market capitalization — share price × total outstanding shares — gives a quick snapshot of what investors think a company is worth. But it ignores debt and cash, which means two companies with identical market caps can have radically different true costs. Enterprise value fixes this.

**The Enterprise Value Formula**

EV = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash

This is the number that matters when you want to know what it would actually cost to acquire a company. If you buy a business, you inherit its debt and receive its cash — so EV reflects the real price.

**Side-by-Side Example**

Two companies, both with a $500M market cap:
- Company A: $200M debt, $50M cash → EV = $650M
- Company B: $0 debt, $100M cash → EV = $400M

Company A is $250M more expensive to acquire despite identical market caps. The debt load is the hidden cost.

**What Each EV Scenario Signals**

- **EV > Market Cap** (Company A): significant debt inflates total cost; acquiring this company means taking on financial obligations
- **EV < Market Cap** (Company B): strong cash position suggests financial stability and flexibility for investment or expansion — but investigate *why* the cash is sitting there

**EV Multiples: How Investors Use It**

**EV/EBITDA** — Compares enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. A lower ratio suggests the company is undervalued relative to its earnings; higher may indicate overvaluation.

**EV/Revenue** — Useful for early-stage companies that aren't yet profitable, where EBITDA is meaningless. Compares enterprise value to total sales. Doesn't account for profit margins, so must be used alongside other metrics. High-growth unprofitable companies carry more risk.

**Key Takeaway**

Professional investors use EV over market cap because it gives a fuller picture. A cash-rich company with no debt is cheaper to own than its stock price implies. A debt-laden company is more expensive than it looks. Always pair EV analysis with qualitative context — high debt could signal growth investment or financial distress; excess cash could signal discipline or stagnation.
