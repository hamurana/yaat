**The Frame**

Magnetic people don't hustle for attention or try harder. They do a few almost-invisible things that completely shift the energy in a room. The creator, a doctor, describes going from painfully shy (never raised her hand, terrified of being judged) to genuinely enjoying walking into rooms full of strangers. The shift came from these five small habits — not personality change.

**Habit 1 — The Two-Second Eye Contact Rule**

Research: people who hold eye contact ~60–70% of the time are rated more confident, likable, and trustworthy. The micro-tweak: when greeting or conversing, hold their gaze just two seconds longer than feels natural. Not an unblinking stare — just being present, signalling "I see you, I'm here with you." Tested ordering coffee: holding eye contact a beat longer made the barista smile wider, her whole energy shifted. As a doctor, a moment longer of eye contact caused patients' shoulders to drop and they'd relax.

**Habit 2 — Open Posture Reset**

Before you speak, your body has already introduced you. People read posture, shoulder position, how you enter a room. Magnetic people don't fold in — they expand, sending the signal "I belong here." The tiny habit: when entering a room or sitting down with someone, take a one-second reset — uncross arms, relax shoulders, angle torso toward the person. Research (Amy Cuddy's power pose work) shows posture influences both how others perceive you and how you feel. At a writer's conference in New York knowing no one, walking in with head up and open posture led to meeting writers, agents, editors, and Lee Child.

**Habit 3 — The Name Echo**

Neuroscience: hearing your own name activates unique brain regions — a small dopamine hit. People light up when they hear their name even if they don't show it. The habit: use someone's name three times — once on greeting, once naturally mid-conversation, once on saying goodbye. This also helps with remembering names: forcing yourself to use it immediately dramatically improves retention. Warning: don't overdo it ("So Kim, tell me Kim...") — that's creepy. Used lightly, it makes people feel seen and memorable.

**Habit 4 — The Micro-Pause**

Communication researchers: people who pause even briefly are perceived as more thoughtful, authoritative, and charismatic. Think Steve Jobs during keynotes — making a bold statement and letting it hang in the air. The creator learned this in medical residency: during radiology sessions when asked to read a scan, she trained herself to pause rather than blurt something out. The effect was unexpected — a senior resident told her she seemed poised and confident under pressure. The habit: before answering a question or after making a point, take one breath. Let words have room to land. Confidence isn't speaking faster — it's showing you're not in a rush to prove yourself.

**Habit 5 — The Curiosity Spark**

The mistake most people make: trying to be interesting instead of being interested. Harvard Business Review: people who ask thoughtful follow-up questions (not just one, but continued follow-ups) are rated as more likable and competent. The habit: when someone shares something, ask one follow-up question that goes slightly deeper. "What's been the most exciting part of your work lately?" instead of "how's work?" "What do you love most about living there?" instead of "where are you from?" The payoff: the other person feels heard, relaxes, opens up — and almost always turns curiosity back on you. You get to be interesting by showing them how interesting they are.

**The Five in Summary**

1. Hold eye contact two seconds longer
2. Reset posture — open, belonging
3. Use the name echo naturally (not creepily)
4. Master the micro-pause
5. Spark conversations with genuine curiosity
