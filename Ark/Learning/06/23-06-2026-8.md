**The Calcium Paradox**

A meta-analysis in the British Medical Journal found that calcium supplementation above 1g/day without vitamin D was associated with a ~30% increased risk of heart attack. Yet the same patients showing arterial calcification also have low bone density — bones losing calcium while arteries gain it, in the same body at the same time. The underlying cause is a deficiency most people have never heard of: vitamin K2.

**What Vitamin K2 Does**

K2 activates two critical proteins: *osteocalcin* (pulls calcium into bone matrix) and *matrix GLA protein* (prevents calcium from depositing in artery walls). Without K2, both proteins sit inactive. Calcium consumed — whether from food or supplements — has nowhere safe to go, so it ends up in blood vessels and heart valves rather than bones. This drives vascular stiffness, rising systolic blood pressure, and ultimately heart failure. The Rotterdam study found that people with the highest dietary K2 had a 50% lower risk of dying from cardiovascular disease.

**K2 vs K1 — A Critical Distinction**

Vitamin K1 (found in leafy greens like kale) handles blood clotting. Vitamin K2 handles calcium trafficking. These are entirely different molecules. Eating more salad does not solve a K2 deficiency. K2 comes from two sources: fermented foods (natto, aged hard cheeses like gouda and edam) and animal products from grass-fed animals (eggs, butter, dairy, liver). The bacteria in fermentation produce MK7, a long-chain K2 that stays active in the bloodstream for more than a day.

**How Modern Diets Stripped K2**

Over the last 60 years: cattle moved to grain-fed feedlots, chickens confined to barns, dairy shifted indoors, fermented foods largely disappeared from Western diets. Then from the 1980s onwards, low-fat dietary advice eliminated egg yolks, full-fat dairy, and animal fats — the very foods where K2 lives. K2 is fat-soluble, so even if you eat K2-containing foods, it needs dietary fat to be absorbed. The modern low-fat diet systematically removed every dietary K2 source simultaneously.

**Warfarin and the K2 Connection**

Warfarin (blood thinner used for AF, DVT, mechanical heart valves) works by blocking vitamin K — but it blocks K2 as well as K1. This inhibits matrix GLA protein activation, accelerating vascular and valve calcification over time. CT calcium scores in long-term warfarin patients are significantly higher than in those not taking it. Newer anticoagulants (apixaban, rivaroxaban) block specific clotting factors directly and do not touch K2 metabolism at all.

**Bone Health**

The same K2 mechanism that protects arteries also protects bones. In Japan, K2 is prescribed at high doses for osteoporosis and has been shown to reduce vertebral fracture risk. Dutch and Norwegian studies using food-like doses of MK7 showed improvements in bone density over three years. The conclusion: a large body of people have been mass-prescribed calcium supplements that don't prevent fractures and may mildly increase cardiovascular risk, while the actual missing nutrient — K2 — was being systematically removed from the food supply.

**Practical Guidance**

Rather than supplementing K2 in isolation, the goal is to build a Mediterranean-style dietary pattern and stop fearing K2-containing foods. Foundation: olive oil, oily fish 2–3x/week, vegetables, legumes, nuts, seeds, whole grains, minimal ultra-processed foods. Included within that: hard aged cheese, free-range eggs, grass-fed butter, quality meat. The PREDIMED trial showed this pattern reduced major cardiovascular events by ~30% versus low-fat diet over five years — and it is not a low-fat, anti-animal-products diet.

Vitamin D and K2 work together: D pulls calcium from the gut into the bloodstream, K2 directs it into bones. Taking vitamin D alone without K2 risks raising blood calcium without directing it correctly.
