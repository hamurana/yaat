**What the Taste Economy Is**

The taste economy describes a shift from buying what you need to buying what expresses who you are. Two forces drove it: (1) the democratisation of supply chains (Alibaba, global trade platforms) made quality goods cheaper and more accessible; (2) the internet — Instagram, TikTok, YouTube — delivers taste to you passively. Things you'd never have discovered through TV ads are now served in your feed. Together, these made it easy and affordable to care about quality, aesthetics, and brand identity in everyday purchases.

The analogy: everything used to taste like plain bread. Now the world has Doritos-level flavour optimisation everywhere. When exceptional quality is the baseline, ordinary feels like "life with the volume off."

**How Consumers Changed**

Educated consumers now shop by ingredient, material, and process — not just brand or price. They know the difference between polyester and natural fibre, understand gut microbiomes, read ingredient lists on skincare. This knowledge comes from free, abundant content from experts willing to educate at scale. The "best thing" search (best foil surfboard, worst foil surfboard, what to avoid) hasn't changed — but now it happens on TikTok and Instagram as much as Google.

**Three Tactics That Work in This Environment**

1. **The best thing** — build a brand story entirely around owning one superlative quality. Comfort hoodies found their market in "softest hoodie" and built hundreds of creators telling that story. The category doesn't need to be obvious; it needs to be something people genuinely want once they hear it framed.

2. **Ingredients/materials** — educated consumers buy the active compound, not the category. They want the Niacinamide 6% zinc 1%, not just "a toner." Brands that lead with transparency on what makes their product work convert this curiosity.

3. **Transformation** — in a world where everything looks good, the differentiator is a credible promise of a life-changing result. The Peloton commitment, the 30-day program outcome. The enthusiasm that comes from education creates buyers who want to participate, not just purchase.

**Four Archetypes of the Premium/Luxury Buyer**

1. **Cultural Omnivore** — deeply educated through lived experience. Equally comfortable with fine caviar and a taco truck. Would never buy entry-level luxury (no Gucci wallet). Spends on quality when the quality is genuinely there. The best customer for premium goods that actually deliver.

2. **Petite Bourgeoisie** — aspires to be an omnivore, consumes influencer recommendations, spends above their means signalling arrival. Buys Loro Piana hats and Aesop soap. The market for entry-level luxury products. Poor personal finance decision; excellent demographic to sell into on the way to their next stage.

3. **True Elite** — ultra-wealthy, luxury is simply normal spending. Impossible to reach through Instagram or influencer campaigns (they don't follow brands). Requires long-form storytelling, exclusive events, experiences inside spaces they already inhabit.

4. **Aspiring** — uneducated but newly moneyed. Attracted to logos and signalling. Buys the cheapest available luxury item as a status marker. A double-edged market: sell too much to this group and you erode the luxury perception; ignore them and you lose a generation who might mature into better buyers.

**The Enabled Economy — How Marketing Changed**

Old stack: brand social accounts + paid ads + a few influencers + maybe a celebrity.

New stack: brand-owned content + hundreds of enabled creators making organic content for free + paid media amplifying whatever works from both + experiences designed to be shared (not just seen). The gap activation at Coachella wasn't about selling hoodies at the event — it was buying a ripple effect of thousands of micro-influencers at a disproportionately content-heavy moment, each wearing the hoodie in Coachella footage.

**The Deepest Insight**

Operational marketing (landing pages, media buying, A/B tests) is increasingly commoditised. The only scarce input is the insight — the correct read of what people actually feel versus what they say they feel. People self-deceive constantly. The marketer who can distinguish real motivation from stated preference and build conviction around it is the one who wins.
