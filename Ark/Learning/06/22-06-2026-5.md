**The Study: A Two-Week Diet Swap**

A 2015 study published in Nature Communications, led by researcher Stephen O'Keefe, took 20 African Americans from Pittsburgh and 20 rural South Africans from KwaZulu-Natal and swapped their diets for two weeks. Americans were fed a traditional rural African diet (high fibre, whole plant foods). South Africans ate a typical American diet (low fibre, processed, refined carbohydrates).

The two groups were selected for a reason: African Americans have bowel cancer rates roughly 13× higher than rural South Africans — around 65 per 100,000 vs. under 5 per 100,000. Both populations are genetically similar, so the gap had to be explained by lifestyle and diet. Prior colonoscopies in rural South Africa had found almost none of the precancerous polyps common in Western populations.

**What They Measured**

Before and after the swap, every participant underwent colonoscopies and the researchers measured:
- Gut inflammation markers (a major driver of bowel cancer)
- Cell division rates in the bowel lining (abnormally fast = elevated cancer risk)
- Gut microbiome composition (which bacterial species were present)
- Butyrate levels — a short-chain fatty acid produced by gut bacteria that is one of the most powerful natural anti-cancer compounds the body makes

**What Happened in 14 Days**

The results were striking in both directions. African Americans on the rural African diet saw their cancer risk markers fall: inflammation dropped, cell division slowed, gut bacteria shifted toward protective species, and butyrate levels climbed. South Africans on the American diet experienced the exact opposite: inflammation rose, cell division accelerated, protective bacteria declined, and butyrate dropped. Two weeks. Fully reversed risk patterns in both groups.

**Why Fibre Is the Mechanism**

The explanation came down overwhelmingly to dietary fibre. The traditional African diet provides at least 55g/day from maize, beans, vegetables, and whole plants. The American and UK diet averages around 14g/day, mostly refined carbs and processed meats. Fibre is food for the protective bacterial species in the colon. Those bacteria ferment fibre and produce butyrate, which:
- Nourishes the cells lining the colon
- Calms inflammation
- Encourages abnormal cells to die off rather than divide
- Strengthens the gut barrier, preventing leaky gut and systemic inflammation

Remove fibre, and you starve those bacteria. Butyrate levels plummet, the bowel lining becomes vulnerable, and cancer risk starts climbing.

**Practical Steps**

- Aim for at least 30g fibre per day (the UK recommendation is a bare minimum; most people manage 15–20g)
- Build meals around vegetables, whole grains, fruits, nuts, seeds, and fermented foods
- Add beans or lentils 4–5 times per week — rich in resistant starch, one of the best fuels for butyrate production
- Swap white bread for seeded sourdough; swap white rice for quinoa, bulgar wheat, or mixed grain bags
- Start meals with raw veg and hummus or lightly cooked veg — a simple way to add fibre before every main
- Aim for variety: 30 different plant foods per week across all categories (herbs, spices count)
- Cut ultra-processed food — it crowds out fibre and has been engineered to bypass satiety signals

**On Fibre Supplements**

The study participants who were protected used no supplements — they ate real food. Fibre supplements deliver one or two isolated fibre types; your microbiome needs hundreds of different kinds. They let you feel like you've addressed the problem without actually changing your diet.
