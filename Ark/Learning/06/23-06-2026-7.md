**The Context**

After taking an 84% pay cut, the creator made a rule: life should still feel beautiful, intentional, and enjoyable on a fraction of previous earnings. The nine items below are the outcome of that constraint — small indulgences that create disproportionate daily quality.

**1. Physical books — only the keepers**

Read most new books on Kindle. But when a book genuinely changes something — one you'll reread, recommend, or give away — buy the physical copy. The result: a curated shelf of only the books that have mattered, rather than a collection of everything ever read. Curation over accumulation.

**2. Ask "can I get the same thing for less?"**

Before any purchase that feels unreasonably priced: do I need this? Can I use less of it? Can I get the same thing cheaper? This turned cutting a £100+/month smoothie habit into making the same thing at home for £2 each. And an Apple Fitness subscription into a single free 10-minute YouTube stretch routine used daily for six months. Framing substitution as a game rather than deprivation makes it sustainable.

**3. A physical journal and a nice pen**

Used for feelings, emotions, and thoughts — not brainstorming or work. A plain notepad (no guided prompts, no structure) for freedom to write anything. Photos of important entries are backed up in the Day One app — analog feeling with digital backup. The act of writing slows thinking down in a way that typing doesn't.

**4. Scented candles and diffusers**

Almost always burning something. Particularly valuable when eating alone — it transforms a solo meal from something that could feel sad into something intentional and pleasant. Dupe candles (£2–5) work fine for the ambience; the effect is about creating an environment, not brand prestige.

**5. A tidy wardrobe with matching hangers**

One Sunday afternoon, matching hangers (£20–30 from Amazon), clothes grouped by category. The result: getting dressed feels like browsing a retail shop rather than rummaging through a jumble sale. More of the wardrobe gets worn because it's actually visible. A tidy desk and tidy kitchen counter operate the same way — order in the environment creates a felt sense of having your life together.

**6. A nice water bottle**

Having something that fits the aesthetic of your desk makes you actually drink from it. More water means more focus, better skin, better energy. The simplest non-negotiable health habit made enjoyable through the object itself.

**7. Plants instead of fresh flowers**

Weekly fresh flowers were the first thing cut after the pay cut. Plants replaced them: longer-lived, cheaper, and they make a space feel alive rather than just furnished.

**8. For every upgrade, a corresponding downgrade**

The rule: every time you add a nice-to-have expense, remove something else of equal cost. The coffee shop habit could come back only when food delivery came off the budget. This does two things: the luxury feels paid for (no guilt, no resentment about overspending), and it forces an honest comparison of which thing is actually worth more to you. Most of the time, the maths reveals you don't actually want the new thing as much as you thought.

**9. Dermatologist over luxury skincare**

One £200 visit revealed that expensive base creams were causing breakouts. The result: four specific products, no premium branding, skin improved and maintained for years. The lesson: the luxury was the expertise, not the packaging. One expert consultation beats ongoing spend on guesses.
