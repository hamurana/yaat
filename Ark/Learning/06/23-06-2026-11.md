**What EPS Measures**

Earnings Per Share (EPS) = (Net Income − Preferred Dividends) ÷ Shares Outstanding. It answers: how much profit does each common share of this company earn? Preferred shareholders get paid first from profits at a fixed rate, so their dividends are subtracted before dividing.

**Worked Example**

Company A: $5M net income, $200k preferred dividends, 1M shares → EPS = $4.80  
Company B: $8M net income, $300k preferred dividends, 2M shares → EPS = $3.85  
Despite Company B having higher absolute profit, Company A generates more per share — suggesting greater capital efficiency. That said, EPS alone doesn't determine which is the better investment: revenue growth, debt levels, and cash flow must also be considered.

**What Moves EPS**

- **Profit up** → EPS up (higher sales, lower costs, improved efficiency)
- **Shares increase (dilution)** → EPS down — the same profit is divided among more shares, like cutting a birthday cake into more slices
- **Buybacks** → EPS up — fewer shares means each remaining one claims a larger slice of profits; companies sometimes use buybacks specifically to boost EPS and attract investors

**Negative EPS**

A net loss produces negative EPS. Startups and growth companies often run negative EPS while investing heavily before becoming profitable. Negative EPS isn't automatically disqualifying — look at revenue growth, cash position, and the reason for losses.

**EPS and the P/E Ratio**

EPS feeds directly into the Price-to-Earnings ratio: Stock Price ÷ EPS. A higher P/E means investors are paying more per dollar of earnings, usually because they expect future growth.

**Limitations**

- Ignores debt and future growth potential
- Companies with few shares can appear more profitable without actually being stronger businesses
- EPS can be manipulated through accounting choices — always cross-reference with other metrics
- Best used for comparing companies within the same industry, not across sectors
