**What OMAD Actually Is**

One Meal A Day (OMAD) means fasting for ~23 hours and eating everything in a single hour. On a dial from "constant grazing" to "maximum fast," this is the extreme end. The appeal: low insulin for most of the day (favouring fat burning), no calorie counting, and reclaimed time. These are real benefits — but they come with significant trade-offs.

**What the Science Actually Shows**

Three studies tell the story clearly:

1. **New England Journal of Medicine (1-year, ~140 people)**: Half followed a calorie-restricted diet with a tight eating window, half just cut calories eating normally throughout the day. After 12 months — identical weight loss, identical fat loss, identical blood pressure and blood sugar. The fasting window added nothing.

2. **A second trial (~116 people)**: Same finding. The "magic" of fasting almost disappears when tested properly. It works because it makes you eat less. That is the whole mechanism.

3. **Calorie-matched study (15 people)**: Fed the exact same calories as either one meal or three meals. Even with identical calories, the OMAD group had higher fasting glucose, a poorer insulin response after meals, and elevated LDL cholesterol. The metabolic profile worsened despite no calorie difference.

**The Muscle Problem**

In one of those trials, a significant portion of weight lost on OMAD was lean muscle, not fat. Muscle building requires a steady supply of protein spread across the day — one large hit undermines this. As you age, protecting muscle becomes one of the most important things you can do. OMAD actively works against it.

**The Long-Term Risk Signal**

A large observational study of 24,000+ people found those eating one meal a day had a higher risk of death, including from heart disease, than those eating three meals. Correlation, not causation — but a large and consistent signal pointing in a concerning direction.

**The Practical Problems**

- To eat a full day's nutrition in one hour, you'd need enormous volumes of whole food — most people can't manage it. The practical result is either quality drops (drifting toward calorie-dense processed food) or quantity stays too low (slow calorie deficit, continued muscle loss)
- Timing: most people do OMAD as an evening meal (easiest to skip food during busy workdays). But research shows the body handles food more efficiently in the morning and early afternoon. The most practical timing is metabolically the worst
- Social cost: eating is connection — breakfast with kids, lunch with colleagues, dinner with friends. OMAD forces all of daily life to orbit one eating window; everyone else eats without you

**The Better Alternative**

Three proper meals inside an 8-hour window, with a 14–16 hour overnight fast built in. This delivers most of the metabolic upside of fasting — lower insulin baseline, some fat burning overnight — while preserving muscle protein synthesis, keeping glucose stable, and being sustainable for life. The unglamorous approach wins.

**The Deeper Point**

If you're drawn to OMAD, it's usually because you want to change something — weight, energy, health. But the underlying problem almost never was eating more than once a day. It was food quality, inactivity, poor sleep. Switch to OMAD while leaving those unchanged and you've changed *when* you eat but none of the causes of the problem.
