**The Hook & Core Thesis**

Recessions aren't dangerous because the market falls — they're dangerous because of what you do while it's falling. The drop is almost always temporary; what makes a temporary drop a *permanent* loss is hitting sell at the wrong moment. The winners never called the bottom — they decided their plan in advance and followed it mechanically.

---
**Key Arguments Checklist**

- **Panic selling, not the recession itself, is what destroys returns.** Same crash, opposite outcomes, based on one decision.
- **You can't predict recessions** — not even professionals with research teams can reliably call the bottom.
- **The market is forward-looking; economic data is backward-looking.** Bad economic news and a bad time to invest are not the same thing.
- **Waiting for good news guarantees you miss the recovery** — by design, the confirming data arrives after the best days have passed.
- **Recency bias makes every recession feel uniquely fatal** even though the pattern repeats almost identically.
- **Decide your rules now, while calm** — not repeatedly, while scared.

---
**High-Yield Assets**

- **The [[S&P 500]] case:** bottomed **March 2009**, fully recovered by **early 2013** (~4 years). The panicked seller who waited until it "felt safe" missed most of that recovery.
- **Definition of a recession:** a significant, economy-wide decline lasting more than a few months. Common rule of thumb: **two consecutive quarters of declining GDP**. In the US the official call is made by the **[[NBER]] (National Bureau of Economic Research)**, looking at employment, income, industrial production, and spending — *not just GDP*.
- **The NBER lag:** recessions are declared **months after they start**, so by the time you hear "recession" on the news, the economy has usually been in one for a while.
- **Recession frequency / length:** since the **1850s**, the US has had **34 recessions**, long-run average ~**17 months** (skewed by pre-modern crises). **Since WWII**, average ~**10–11 months**. Extremes: **2007–2009 lasted 18 months**; the **2020 pandemic recession was over in just 2 months**.
- **Best and worst days cluster together:** in **March 2020**, two of the S&P 500's **10 worst** trading days *and* two of its **11 best** days happened in the same month — extreme volatility runs in both directions because extreme uncertainty does.
- **March 2020 recovery:** S&P fell ~30% in weeks, bottomed around **March 23rd**, then began one of the fastest recoveries in modern history — while headlines were still negative and unemployment was still climbing.
- **The three mistakes:** (1) **recency bias** ("this time is different"); (2) **waiting for good news**; (3) **confusing the economy with the market**.

---
**Case Studies / Anecdotes**

- **"This time is different," three times:** 2020 — it's a global pandemic, the economy is physically shut down; 2008 — it's the banks, the financial system is broken; 2000 — the internet bubble proved dot-coms were a fraud. Every time the story changed; the underlying pattern didn't.
- **The March 2020 free-fall** — every headline was "worst crash since 2008," lockdowns spreading, no end in sight — yet that was the moment statistically closest to some of the best days to have stayed invested for. Those who waited for the news to feel better waited well past the recovery.

---
**The "So What?" Factor**

Don't try to time the bottom — decide your rules now, while thinking clearly. Specifically, settle these *today*: what happens to your regular contributions if the market drops hard (and whether you'll add extra); your actual emergency-fund threshold in months of expenses; and the concrete trigger (a percentage or a date) for rebalancing. None of these require predicting anything — they just require deciding once when calm instead of repeatedly when scared.
