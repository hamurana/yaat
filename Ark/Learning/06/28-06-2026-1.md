**Topic: Captivating an Audience — A Giving Mindset and the Three A's of Public Speaking**

Great speakers win the room before they say anything substantive: they show up to **give**, not to take, and then deliver through three traits — **authenticity**, **awareness**, and **audacity**. (The title promises "4 tips"; the talk frames it as a giving attitude plus the "Three A's.")

---

**The Foundation — Show Up to Give**
The single most important shift is intention. You were invited because you've seen, done, or tried something others need to hear, so your job is to give that away. [[Takers]] are easy to spot and they repel audiences: they answer questions with "buy my book," plaster every slide with their Instagram, email, and website, and open by reciting their own credentials ("six PhDs, advised CEOs and generals"). The best speakers want nothing back — **not even your approval**. A standing ovation is earned if the audience decides what you gave had value; it is never the reason you show up.

**The cited proof:** every top speaker the presenter names operates from a giving stance — [[Sir Ken Robinson]], [[Amy Cuddy]], [[Brené Brown]], [[Dan Pink]].

---

**A #1 — Authenticity Engages**
Audiences can feel realness, so you have to be yourself or you'll never connect.

*Anecdote — the PowerPoint poll (TEDx, November 2017):* Preparing his first TEDx talk at 26, the presenter agonised over one question — use a PowerPoint or just himself? He polled 12 close friends and family. His sister and brother said use it (back yourself up with stats); his mom said no, "use you." The vote came back **6–6**. Stuck on the tie, he realised he'd never asked the one person who mattered: himself. The lesson — **know yourself and speak from the gut before you seek outside advice.** His prep ritual: rehearse a talk "at least 100 times" before delivery, including conversationally in the shower.

---

**A #2 — Awareness Connects the Speaker to the Audience**
Situational awareness lets you read the room and adapt when things don't go as planned.

*Anecdote — Sioux Falls, South Dakota:* He arrived prepared for a serious, professional motivational talk and walked into a party — hours of open bar, the crowd "lit." The old, over-prepared version of him would have ploughed ahead with the wrong content. Instead he opened with a story: on the flight from Sacramento, a seatmate reacted to "I'm presenting in Sioux Falls" with a blank stare and three words — **"Are you sure?"** A man in the audience laughed so hard "like he was at a Kevin Hart special" that the whole room broke up. The presenter laughed from the stage and rode the energy: *"If this is how it's going to be, let's have a good time."* The takeaway — **be fluid and malleable; play on what is actually happening in the room.**

---

**A #3 — Audacity Informs and Inspires**
You must be bold; you can't "half step" into a presentation. Fear drives most people — fear of others' opinions, of criticism, of not being good enough, of tripping on a word — and boldness is the antidote.

The chief tool of audacity is **story**. The brain operates in pictures, and a story paints the picture in the listener's mind. **A study of top TED Talks (the million-view ones) found 85% were story-centric.** Stories cross every industry and era — your personal story, [[Martin Luther King]]'s speech, a head of state.

**Governing rule of storytelling:** *"Never tell a story without making a point, but never make a point without telling a story."* Use the story to drive home what you're trying to say.

---

**The "So What?"**
Public speaking is fundamental to nearly every career, yet fear holds most people back. The actionable path: enter with a giving attitude, decide what *you* are genuinely comfortable with before crowdsourcing advice, stay alert enough to adapt to the real room, and build your message around stories told boldly with a clear point. Rehearse relentlessly — but let authenticity, not the script, lead.
